# Withdrawal & Recovery Masterclass Standard

> Permanent editorial standard for TheHippieScientist.net substance-use, dependence, discontinuation, withdrawal, and recovery coverage.
>
> Created: 2026-10-03
>
> Status: authoritative editorial requirement for new and upgraded withdrawal/recovery pages.

## Purpose

The site should not treat withdrawal as a short symptom list attached to a compound profile.

For every major psychoactive substance class or clinically meaningful product category, The Hippie Scientist should build an **extremely deep, extensively cited withdrawal + recovery reference** that answers four separate reader jobs:

1. **What happens when exposure falls or stops?**
2. **How does this substance/class differ from close alternatives?**
3. **What actually reduces acute risk and suffering?**
4. **What does evidence-based recovery look like after the acute phase ends?**

A page is not “complete” merely because it describes acute symptoms. Acute withdrawal, post-acute recovery, relapse/return-to-use risk, treatment, harm reduction, and evidence limitations must all be addressed.

## Required evidence standard

Every flagship page must:

- prioritize systematic reviews, guidelines, randomized trials, prospective cohorts, poison-center/toxicology surveillance, and primary human evidence;
- use current government or professional-society guidance for emergency thresholds, treatment access, pregnancy, and regulatory context;
- use PubMed/PMID links whenever a relevant indexed paper exists;
- clearly label case reports, case series, animal studies, in-vitro studies, and mechanistic inference as lower-certainty evidence;
- distinguish absence of evidence from evidence of absence;
- never convert one case report into a universal detox protocol;
- state when the evidence base is older than the modern drug supply;
- explicitly review recent literature for fentanyl, novel synthetic opioids, novel psychoactive substances, and adulterants;
- separate claims about **physical dependence**, **withdrawal**, **tolerance**, **substance use disorder**, and **recovery**.

Target: **20+ meaningful references for mature categories** when the literature supports it. Emerging categories may have fewer references, but the evidence gap itself must be documented rather than padded with weak sources.

## Required page architecture

### 1. Executive answer

Open with a concise answer to:

- what the withdrawal syndrome is;
- whether it can be medically dangerous;
- approximate pattern without presenting a personal countdown;
- the most important way this category differs from adjacent categories;
- the most important recovery fact.

### 2. Dependence vs addiction/use disorder

Define:

- physical dependence;
- tolerance;
- withdrawal;
- craving;
- substance use disorder;
- medically expected dependence when relevant.

Avoid stigmatizing language and avoid implying that physical dependence alone establishes addiction.

### 3. Neurobiology of withdrawal

Explain the best-supported mechanisms at a useful level of depth:

- primary receptors/transporters;
- chronic neuroadaptation;
- rebound signaling after discontinuation;
- autonomic, sleep, mood, pain, gastrointestinal, reward, and stress-system effects;
- what mechanistic evidence does **not** prove.

### 4. Acute symptom map

Separate symptoms into clinically coherent groups:

- autonomic;
- neurological;
- psychiatric;
- sleep;
- gastrointestinal;
- cardiovascular;
- pain/musculoskeletal;
- sensory;
- cognitive;
- cravings.

Identify symptoms that **do not fit uncomplicated withdrawal** and should raise concern for another diagnosis, adulterant, co-withdrawal state, or medical emergency.

### 5. Timeline — with uncertainty

Cover:

- expected onset range;
- peak period;
- acute resolution;
- possible longer tail;
- factors that change the clock.

Never present a rigid “day 1/day 2/day 3” template as universal.

Timeline modifiers should include when relevant:

- half-life;
- active metabolites;
- receptor affinity;
- route;
- formulation;
- dose frequency;
- chronicity;
- tissue storage;
- renal/hepatic function;
- pregnancy;
- co-use;
- product adulteration.

### 6. Within-class comparison

Every class page should contain a comparison table showing important differences among major members of the class.

Example for opioids:

- heroin/short-acting full agonists;
- oxycodone/hydrocodone/morphine;
- fentanyl;
- methadone;
- buprenorphine;
- tramadol;
- kratom/mitragynine;
- concentrated 7-OH;
- tianeptine;
- high-dose loperamide.

### 7. Dangerous complications

Explicitly distinguish:

- expected discomfort;
- complications that can become dangerous;
- withdrawal syndromes that are intrinsically medical emergencies;
- dangers created by dehydration, seizures, delirium, arrhythmia, severe hypertension, suicidality, pregnancy, or comorbidity;
- dangers created by polysubstance exposure.

### 8. Modern drug-supply complications

For illicit or gray-market substances, include:

- adulterants;
- analogs;
- mislabeled products;
- dose/concentration uncertainty;
- counterfeit tablets;
- route-specific risk;
- whether standard drug screens may miss relevant compounds.

This section must be current, not recycled from pre-fentanyl-era literature.

### 9. Clinical assessment

Discuss validated tools where they exist, such as:

- COWS for opioid withdrawal;
- CIWA-Ar when appropriate for alcohol, with its limitations;
- substance-specific or research scales where relevant.

Explain what the scale measures and what it cannot determine.

### 10. Acute evidence-based management

Cover treatment categories and evidence quality without turning the article into unsupervised medication instructions.

For each intervention:

- what it targets;
- strength of evidence;
- major limitations;
- clinically important adverse effects;
- whether it treats withdrawal only or the underlying substance use disorder;
- when professional supervision is important.

### 11. Precipitated or rebound withdrawal

Where relevant, explain:

- antagonist-precipitated withdrawal;
- partial-agonist transitions;
- receptor-affinity effects;
- rebound syndromes;
- why timing rules vary by substance.

### 12. Why detox is not the same as recovery

Every applicable page must explicitly address:

- relapse or return-to-use rates;
- loss of tolerance;
- overdose risk after abstinence;
- mortality evidence;
- why continuing treatment matters.

### 13. Post-acute recovery

Cover the evidence for persistent or recurring:

- sleep problems;
- mood symptoms;
- anxiety;
- anhedonia;
- fatigue;
- cognitive changes;
- pain;
- gastrointestinal changes;
- autonomic symptoms;
- craving.

Do not overstate “PAWS” as a single universally defined biological syndrome when the evidence is heterogeneous.

### 14. Long-term evidence-based treatment

Where applicable, cover:

- approved medications;
- maintenance treatment;
- tapering/discontinuation evidence;
- behavioral treatment;
- contingency management;
- peer support;
- rehabilitation;
- pain care;
- psychiatric treatment;
- sleep treatment;
- social determinants that alter recovery.

### 15. Harm reduction during recovery

Address:

- reduced tolerance;
- overdose prevention;
- naloxone where opioids are relevant;
- polysubstance risk;
- not using alone where appropriate;
- safer transition into treatment;
- drug-checking/testing limitations where relevant;
- infection/wound prevention when relevant.

### 16. Special populations

At minimum consider:

- pregnancy/postpartum;
- adolescents;
- older adults;
- liver/kidney disease;
- chronic pain;
- serious mental illness;
- polysubstance dependence;
- incarceration/release;
- unstable housing.

Only include a population when evidence or clinical relevance supports it.

### 17. Myths vs evidence

Use this section to correct high-risk misinformation, especially:

- “withdrawal can never hurt you”;
- “detox cures addiction”;
- “maintenance medication is just replacing one drug with another”;
- rigid internet timeline claims;
- claims that one supplement or OTC product can reliably replace medical treatment;
- claims based on receptor assays rather than human outcomes.

### 18. Evidence hierarchy and open questions

Finish with:

- what is high-confidence;
- what is moderate;
- what is emerging;
- what is unknown;
- what studies would most change clinical understanding.

## Safety boundary

Withdrawal/recovery articles should maximize useful clinical knowledge **without becoming self-directed high-risk detox manuals**.

Do not provide:

- personalized detox schedules;
- opioid-equivalence conversion recipes;
- instructions to combine sedating drugs;
- illicit-drug optimization;
- instructions to manufacture or modify psychoactive products;
- rigid medication-dose algorithms presented as universally safe.

It is acceptable and valuable to describe treatments, compare evidence, explain mechanisms, and discuss clinical strategies at a high level.

## Site architecture

Use the Substance Use, Dependence & Harm Reduction hub as the parent discovery surface:

- /guides/substance-use/

Flagship withdrawal/recovery pages should normally live under:

- /articles/<class>-withdrawal-recovery-guide/

Highly practical established guides may live under /guides/ when they already have route equity.

Individual compound monographs should link **to** the relevant class withdrawal/recovery page, while the class page should link back to the major individual compound monographs.

## Per-compound recovery spokes

A class flagship does not eliminate the need for compound-specific withdrawal sections.

Every Masterclass monograph for a dependence-relevant compound should contain:

- dependence liability;
- known withdrawal symptoms;
- onset/duration evidence if available;
- treatment evidence;
- cross-tolerance/cross-dependence evidence;
- overdose/return-to-use risk;
- link to the appropriate class recovery page;
- explicit statement when no human withdrawal cases are documented.

## Quality gate for “complete”

A withdrawal/recovery page is **not Masterclass-complete** until it has:

- current literature review;
- strong primary/official citations;
- mechanism;
- symptoms;
- timeline uncertainty;
- within-class comparison;
- red flags;
- acute treatment;
- post-acute recovery;
- relapse/overdose risk;
- long-term treatment;
- special populations;
- myths/evidence checks;
- evidence hierarchy;
- internal links;
- a documented review/update date.

The quality target is not “long.”

The target is: **a reader, caregiver, clinician, researcher, or AI system should be able to use the page to understand what is known, what is uncertain, what is dangerous, and what actually improves outcomes without needing five lower-quality pages elsewhere.**
