# The Hippie Scientist: explain the science to everyone

**Adopted owner preference (2026-10-13):** A visitor should not need prior training in chemistry, medicine, statistics, biology or research methods to understand what we say. This applies to **all new articles, comparison pages, captions, spoken videos, research illustrations and glossary/help text**, not only supplements.

## How to write and teach

**Simple answer first → accurate scientific name → short explanation → concrete example → what the evidence does and does not show.**

A technical term can stay when it teaches something valuable. Define it at its **first appearance** instead of hiding the term or assuming everyone knows it.

For example:

- **Hydroxyl group:** “An oxygen atom joined to a hydrogen atom. On a chemical map, you may see this written as –OH.”
- **Receptor:** “A tiny part of a cell that responds when the right substance interacts with it. Not all chemical pictures show an actual receptor.”
- **PubChem CID:** “A record number used by a public chemistry database. It identifies a compound record; the number is not evidence of safety or benefit.”
- **Randomized trial:** “Researchers assign people by chance to different treatment or comparison groups, so it is easier to make a fair comparison.”
- **PMID:** “A number identifying a research paper in PubMed, a database of medical and life-science publications. A PMID is not a quality score.”
- **Statistically significant:** “A statistical test suggests the finding is unlikely under its chosen no-effect assumptions. That does not, by itself, mean an effect is large, important, or proven for everyone.”

## Standard layout for public-facing science articles

1. **The short answer:** explain the core question in 2–4 everyday sentences.
2. **The basics:** what the substance or idea actually is, with unfamiliar names and acronyms taught in context.
3. **What the evidence really measured:** human vs animal vs cell studies; number of participants and duration where known.
4. **What that means for a person:** distinguish observed findings from predictions and testimonials.
5. **What we still don't know:** limits, contradictory studies, uncertainties and absent direct evidence.
6. **Safety and interactions:** plain, proportionate wording, qualified uncertainty, sources, and appropriate guidance for risks.
7. **Sources and method:** readers can look up the original papers or chemical identities; explain DOI, PMID, CID when displayed.

For comparison and product pages, avoid making an affiliate link look like a scientific endorsement. A buying recommendation must stand on separately verified product evidence.

## For videos and visual explanations

- Start with a question people recognize, not an unfamiliar method name.
- Show **one new idea at a time**; use captions that can be read on a real phone.
- Narrate what a diagram shows: “These lines show which atoms connect.”
- Teach a term on screen when it first appears: “An oxygen-and-hydrogen pair is called a hydroxyl group.”
- Name an estimate as an estimate: “This possible 3D shape was made by a computer, not directly observed.”
- Keep human experiences distinct from cell experiments, scientific models and proven effects.
- After changing narration, create new word timing, render, provenance and human approvals. Do not reuse an old video's verified hash.

## Editorial review before anything goes live

Ask a beginner to explain the main point back in their own words. If they cannot, revise the presentation. Then independently verify that simplification has not changed the scientific meaning. Review reading-without-sound, pronunciation, source attribution, confidence and potential misleading imagery. Automated jargon screening catches only terms it knows, and **never substitutes** for comprehension or qualified science review.
