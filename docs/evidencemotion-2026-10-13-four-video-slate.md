## 01 — DMT family: Four relatives, four different stories

**Platform title:** "4 DMT-Related Molecules: Tiny Changes, Different Effects"

**Hook:** "People say DMT like it's just one thing. Chemically, the story gets much more interesting."

**Narration draft (~150 words):**

> People talk about DMT like it's just one thing. But meet four related molecules that aren't interchangeable.
>
> First is N,N-DMT—the compound most people mean when they say DMT.
>
> Change the chemical structure and you get 5-MeO-DMT, short for five-methoxy DMT. That small change alters how it interacts with serotonin receptors, and researchers have found meaningful differences from N,N-DMT.
>
> Then there's 5-HO-DMT, also called bufotenine. It's found in certain plants, including the tree associated with yopo. Its ability to reach the brain differs from its chemical relatives.
>
> And 4-HO-DMT? That's psilocin—the active substance formed when the body processes psilocybin from certain mushrooms.
>
> Same broad tryptamine family. Different chemistry, different pharmacology, and potentially very different subjective experiences.
>
> These aren't four versions of the same high, and one molecule can't safely substitute for another. What's remarkable is how much a tiny molecular change can matter.

**Visual vocabulary (cut to final narration, timings not hardcoded):**
1. Isolated actual N,N-DMT structure, with atom-position labels and source-verified connectivity.
2. Single source-verified transformation to 5-MeO-DMT. Indicate atom substitution only, **not** a biochemical conversion taking place in a person.
3. Side-by-side 5-HO-DMT bufotenine; highlight the *different position-5 substituent*. Explain BBB access **qualitatively**, no made-up percent.
4. Position-4 hydroxyl identity: psilocin; concise non-animated mushroom-to-psilocybin-to-psilocin explanation, not consumption directions.
5. Same-family family tree + separate receptor profile profiles as **schematic** representation, not verified protein docking.
6. End on "Related ≠ interchangeable" / source card; no potency or safety ranking.

**Claim locks:** DMT vs 5-MeO pharmacology differs; bufotenine = 5-HO-DMT; psilocin = 4-HO-DMT; chemical family does not establish interchangeability. Avoid implying every molecule is ordinarily called "DMT" by users. Avoid unsupported precise effect/strength ranking. MAOI interactions can be severe but do not instruct combinations.

**Pronunciation audition:** "en-en dee-em-tee"; "five methoxy dee-em-tee"; "bufotenine" (verify in actual voice); "SILL-oh-sin" (human audition required).

## 02 — Long-term cannabis: What changes, what recovers?

**Platform title:** "What Daily Weed Use Can Change in Your Brain"

**Hook:** "If someone uses weed every day for years, is the brain permanently changed? The real answer is more interesting."

**Narration draft (~160 words):**

> What happens to your brain if you use weed every day for years?
>
> THC interacts with a signaling system that helps regulate memory, mood, sleep, and more. One of its main targets is the CB1 receptor.
>
> With repeated exposure, the brain can reduce how available these receptors are. That adaptation helps explain why people may need more THC to notice the same effects.
>
> Research also links heavier, longer-term cannabis use—especially when it starts young—to differences in learning, memory, and some thinking skills. But these studies can't prove every difference was caused by cannabis.
>
> And here's what surprises people: the brain can adapt again after stopping. Some brain-imaging research finds CB1 receptor measures moving toward typical levels over several weeks. Some thinking skills also improve, but the timeline varies.
>
> Sleep may actually feel worse at first, with unusually vivid dreams or irritability during withdrawal.
>
> So no, the research doesn't support either extreme: "weed does nothing" or "every user's brain is permanently ruined." The real story is adaptation, risk, and potential recovery.

**Visual vocabulary:**
1. Human-level timeline showing daily use over years, no judgement.
2. Close-up THC → CB1 conceptual receptor occupancy, labeled "scientific illustration."
3. Fewer *available* receptors visual metaphor; do not imply irreversible receptor death.
4. Memory/attention tasks overlay with confidence badge "observational association," not direct causal simulation.
5. Stopping/cannabis withdrawal phase: calendar + sleep wave/dream metaphor.
6. Receptor availability partially returning, timeline "some studies: weeks," with reminder cognitive recovery is variable.

**Claim locks:** Do not generalize CB1 normalization to *all people within four weeks*; do not claim a universal cognitive recovery time, drop in IQ, permanent harm, or causality from observational association. Withdrawal/sleep effects vary and medical advice should not be personalized. Differentiate acute impairment, residual effects, and persistent risk.

**Reference anchors:**
- 2026 systematic review of cessation, 26 studies: PMID **41872072**, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41872072/
- 2026 longitudinal narrative review: PMID **42108274**, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42108274/
- 2025 cognitive-domain review: PMID **40710910**, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40710910/

**Suggested social copy:** Does long-term cannabis use change your brain? What researchers know about tolerance, memory, withdrawal, and recovery—without the scare tactics. #CannabisScience #THC #Neuroscience #TheHippieScientist

## 03 — ADHD: Five scientific facts that explain everyday experiences

**Platform title:** "5 ADHD Facts That Make Everyday Life Make Sense"

**Hook:** "ADHD can make five minutes feel like forever—and two hours disappear."

**Narration draft (~160 words):**

> ADHD isn't just about being distracted. Here are five things research can help explain.
>
> One: time perception. Some people with ADHD have measurable difficulty estimating intervals, and a large meta-analysis found average differences across studies.
>
> Two: working memory. That's your brain's temporary notepad. You can know exactly what you were doing—and still lose the next step.
>
> Three: rewards. Tasks with an immediate payoff may be easier to start than equally important tasks with a faraway reward.
>
> Four: attention isn't a simple on-or-off switch. Interest, urgency, environment, and context can change how much focus someone can sustain. People often call intense absorption "hyperfocus," but it's not a stand-alone test for ADHD.
>
> Five: knowing a task matters isn't the same as being able to initiate it. Planning, inhibition, and other executive skills play a role.
>
> None of these facts defines every person with ADHD, and none by itself establishes a diagnosis. But together, they show why "just try harder" misses the science.

**Visual vocabulary:** One flowing ordinary human situation, not five numbered PowerPoint slides: warped clock → disappearing sticky note → delayed vs immediate feedback → attention spotlight shifting → task start gate. Show varied circumstances without implying every ADHD brain is biologically identical. Visualize attention networks only as explicitly marked metaphor.

**Claim locks:** ADHD is heterogeneous. Do not claim a simple dopamine deficiency, laziness, superpower, deterministic diagnosis or that everyone experiences hyperfocus. "Task initiation" is explanatory everyday language, not a diagnostic biomarker.

**Reference anchors:**
- Time perception meta-analysis, PMID **38145491**, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38145491/
- 2026 review of inhibitory control / reward processes, PMID **41872162**, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41872162/
- Effort-related decision-making review, PMID **33521326**, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33521326/
- Adult decision-making meta-analysis, PMID **25477020**, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25477020/

**Suggested social copy:** ADHD isn't just difficulty paying attention. Time, working memory, reward, and task initiation all help explain the bigger picture. #ADHD #BrainScience #Neuroscience #TheHippieScientist

## 04 — Yopo: An ancient plant, a surprising molecule

**Platform title:** "Yopo: The Ancient Seed with a DMT-Related Compound"

**Hook:** "A South American tree produces seeds containing a molecule related to DMT. Its story is thousands of years old."

**Narration draft (~155 words):**

> Long before modern psychedelic science, some Indigenous communities in South America had traditions involving seeds from trees called Anadenanthera.
>
> One of those plants is Anadenanthera peregrina, associated with yopo.
>
> This isn't a mushroom, and it isn't the same preparation as ayahuasca.
>
> One notable molecule associated with these seeds is bufotenine, also called five-hydroxy DMT.
>
> That little "hydroxy" group may sound trivial, but chemistry matters. Bufotenine doesn't cross into the brain the same way some related tryptamines do, so scientists can't treat them as equivalent compounds.
>
> Archeological and historical research traces ritual use of Anadenanthera seeds back thousands of years, but living Indigenous traditions are more than a chemistry lesson.
>
> And here's the fascinating limit of science: finding a molecule, or measuring a receptor in a lab, doesn't tell us exactly what ancient experiences felt like.
>
> Yopo connects botany, human history, and neuroscience—and each part deserves to be understood on its own terms.

**Visual vocabulary:**

2. Respectful illustrated South American distribution map (only source-supported region).

**Claim locks:** Yopo is culturally rooted; do not imply all Indigenous groups used the same species, preparation, or ritual. No prep, extraction, administration or dosing instructions. No pseudo-documentary images of specific communities. Chemical constituents vary; do not claim bufotenine is the only constituent.

## 05 — What science actually says about psychedelics and your brain

**Platform title:** "Do Psychedelics Really Rewire Your Brain? Here's the Science"

**Primary hook:** "You've heard psychedelics rewire your brain. But is that really what brain scans show?"

**Alternative hook A (curiosity):** "Scientists scanned people's brains on psychedelics. The results were stranger than anyone's favorite internet claim."

**Alternative hook B (evidence-first):** "Psychedelics change how brain regions communicate. That's NOT the same as proving they grow new brain cells."

**Narration-first draft (~200 words; natural pacing, not hardcoded):**

> You've probably heard that psychedelics rewire your brain. But what does that actually mean?
>
> Classic psychedelics like psilocybin and LSD act mainly through a serotonin receptor called five-H-T-two-A.
>
> During an experience, brain networks that normally work in separate groups can communicate differently. An April 2026 analysis combined eleven brain-scanning datasets across several psychedelics and found increased communication between some of these networks.
>
> That's fascinating. But it doesn't mean you're unlocking a hidden ninety percent of your brain.
>
> In one closely monitored study, some changes after psilocybin lasted for weeks.
>
> Here's an important distinction: changing communication between brain regions isn't the same as growing brand-new synapses.
>
> Animal research suggests some psychedelics can promote neuroplasticity. But in a small 2026 human brain-imaging study, researchers did *not* find a statistically significant overall increase in one marker of synaptic density.
>
> Meanwhile, some carefully supported clinical studies report improvements in depression, while others have mixed results—and potential harms matter, too.
>
> So are psychedelics capable of changing the brain? Yes, in measurable ways.
>
> Do we know exactly which changes lead to lasting benefits, or that every person's brain gets permanently 'rewired'? No. That's the science we're still figuring out.

**Production concept:** Premium cinematic neuroscience explainer. No stock-psychedelic kaleidoscope wallpaper. Let the *scientific distinctions* drive meaningful visual transformations. Scenes cut to the measured, locked narration waveform.

**Visual beat vocabulary:**
1. Extreme close-up of neurons forming an *existing* network, hook "REWIRED?" appears as a question, not as an asserted outcome.
2. Receptor-level camera movement to correctly labeled **5-HT2A** on a neuron; a small molecule approaches a schematic binding pocket. Explicitly label *illustration*, not exact docking footage.
3. Human brain atlas with **within-network** and **between-network** connectivity contrasted. Connectivity is a *correlation in recorded activity*, not new structural axons. Caption: "2026 pooled fMRI: 11 datasets"; show concise source tag/DOI.
4. Camera changes from brain-network color map to three-layer split: **functional communication / structural synapses / lived experience**. Visually keep these three categories separate.
5. A calendar turns forward to "weeks" only for the specific 2024 human psilocybin observation; no lasting-for-everyone claim.
6. A PET scan-style representation (not fabricated actual study PET scan) and a null result: "2026 human study: 15 people; no significant overall change in measured SV2A density at 1 week." Add smaller note: subset/setting differences were exploratory.
7. Depressive-symptom outcome visualization: **some trials positive, some inconclusive**, never equate fMRI network differences with clinical effectiveness.
8. Finish on living network with labels "**changes measurable; mechanisms incomplete**"; minimal brand CTA.

**Claim/evidence locks (mandatory):**
- Classic serotonergic psychedelics primarily engage 5-HT2A; not all things colloquially called psychedelics act the same (e.g., ketamine, MDMA are distinct).
- 2026 mega-analysis: 11 distinct fMRI datasets, several classic psychedelics, cross-network changes with substantial heterogeneity; do **not** infer universal within-network weakening or increased anatomical neural wiring.
- 2024 Siegel *Nature*: small precision-imaging study in healthy participants; some changes in hippocampal/default-mode *functional connectivity* persisted weeks; not evidence permanent rewiring or that effects generalize to everyone.
- July 2026 Johansen *Translational Psychiatry*: 15 healthy participants; PET SV2A marker at baseline and one week; no statistically significant **overall** change; a therapeutic-like vs MRI setting comparison was an exploratory difference, not proven universal synapse gain; absence of SV2A marker change does not disprove every plasticity mechanism.
- Aug 2026 Stoliker *Nature*: 62 adults; context (music, meditation, movie) matters for how psychedelic brain signals reorganize; avoid saying experiences simply "scramble" the brain.
- 2026 mental-health trials show **mixed** results across designs/outcomes: a 60-person NHS feasibility RCT reported a favorable symptom rating, while EPISODE missed its primary response endpoint despite improvements in secondary scores; this topic is *not* treatment advice.
- Some participants in psychedelic research experience acute distress, anxiety and adverse effects; those vulnerable to psychosis/mania require special caution; don't minimize persistent adverse effects reported in some trials.
- Do not claim "neurogenesis in humans", "permanent rewiring", "unlocking unused brain", "cures depression/trauma", or "one trip fixes neural pathways". Avoid dosing, preparation and sourcing directions.
- Visually distinguish **functional connectivity** (statistical correlation in signals) from **structural connectivity** (physical neurites/synapses). fMRI images are measurements/analyses, not direct photos of neurons making connections.
- Add manuscript-specific subtitle for unusual terms: 5-HT2A = "serotonin receptor"; neuroplasticity = "the brain's capacity to change with experience"; SV2A = "a protein researchers use as an indirect measure of synapses."

**Reference anchors, primary studies:**
- Girn et al., *Nature Medicine* (2026), international 11-dataset mega-analysis: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04287-9
- Stoliker et al., *Nature* (2026), contextual effects, 62 participants, PMID **42618786**: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42618786/
- Siegel et al., *Nature* (2024), precision functional mapping, PMID **39020167**: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39020167/
- Johansen et al., *Translational Psychiatry* (2026), PET synaptic marker, null overall result: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41398-026-04285-y
- Rucker et al., *Nature Medicine* (2026), NHS depression trial: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04541-0
- Mertens et al., *JAMA Psychiatry* (2026), EPISODE mixed primary/secondary results: https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2026.0132
- Mechanism systematic review, PMID **41412413**: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41412413/

**Pronunciation audition:** "five-H-T-two-A" (receptor); "neuro-plasticity" as ordinary flowing speech (not robotic syllable segmentation); "psy-lo-SIGH-bin" (phonetic must be verified with ear-check).
