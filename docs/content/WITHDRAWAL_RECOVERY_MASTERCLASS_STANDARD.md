# Withdrawal & Recovery Masterclass Standard — The Hippie Scientist

> **Status:** Permanent editorial standard  
> **Applies to:** every major psychoactive-drug category and every high-priority individual substance with meaningful dependence, discontinuation, withdrawal, or recovery questions  
> **Created:** 2026-10-03  
> **Purpose:** make TheHippieScientist.net's withdrawal and recovery coverage unusually complete, evidence-graded, harm-reduction focused, and clinically honest.

## Core rule

A page is not “complete” because it lists withdrawal symptoms.

Every flagship withdrawal/recovery page must answer the full reader journey:

1. **What adaptation occurred?**
2. **What happens when exposure falls or stops?**
3. **What is shared with the rest of the drug class, and what is different?**
4. **When does withdrawal begin, peak, and resolve — and how uncertain is that timeline?**
5. **Which symptoms are uncomfortable, which are dangerous, and which suggest another diagnosis or co-withdrawal?**
6. **What does evidence support for acute management?**
7. **What does evidence support for treatment of the underlying substance-use disorder when one is present?**
8. **What happens after the acute syndrome — sleep, mood, pain, cognition, craving, relapse risk, tolerance, and functioning?**
9. **What materially reduces mortality or severe harm during recovery?**
10. **Which claims are well established, which are based on observational data/case reports, and which remain unknown?**

Withdrawal management and recovery are separate concepts. A page that ends when acute symptoms stop is incomplete.

## Required evidence hierarchy

Prefer sources in this order when they exist:

1. Current government or specialty-society clinical guidelines and consensus statements.
2. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses.
3. Randomized controlled trials.
4. Prospective human cohorts and laboratory studies.
5. Large retrospective cohorts, surveillance systems, and poison-center data.
6. Case series and case reports.
7. Preclinical evidence for mechanisms only.
8. Expert commentary only when higher-level evidence does not answer the question.

Every important quantitative claim should trace to a primary source or high-quality evidence synthesis. Do not cite a secondary article for a primary result when the original study is reasonably available.

## Mandatory page architecture

### 1. Executive answer

Open with a concise answer to:
- what the withdrawal syndrome is;
- whether it can be medically dangerous;
- what most strongly changes its course;
- whether withdrawal management alone treats the underlying disorder;
- the most important recovery safety issue.

### 2. Emergency / do-not-miss section

Near the top, identify symptoms or situations requiring urgent medical assessment.

This must be class-specific. Do not use generic alarm language.

Examples:
- alcohol/sedative-hypnotics: seizure, delirium, severe autonomic instability;
- opioids: severe dehydration, inability to keep fluids down, altered consciousness, respiratory depression suggesting intoxication/co-exposure rather than uncomplicated withdrawal, pregnancy, serious medical illness, or acute psychiatric danger;
- stimulants: suicidality, psychosis, severe agitation, chest pain or neurologic symptoms.

### 3. Dependence is not the same as addiction

Define and distinguish:
- tolerance;
- physical dependence;
- withdrawal;
- craving;
- substance use disorder/addiction;
- recurrence/return to use.

Do not imply that physical dependence by itself proves addiction.

### 4. Biology of withdrawal

Explain the relevant receptor, transporter, autonomic, stress-system, sleep, reward, and neuroadaptation biology.

Separate:
- established human evidence;
- mechanistic inference;
- animal/in-vitro findings.

Avoid receptor trivia that does not help a reader understand symptoms, risk, or treatment.

### 5. Symptom map

Organize symptoms by domain when useful:
- autonomic;
- gastrointestinal;
- musculoskeletal/pain;
- sleep;
- mood/affect;
- cognitive;
- neurologic;
- cardiovascular;
- craving/reward;
- substance-specific features.

Describe variability rather than presenting one checklist as universal.

### 6. Timeline and pharmacokinetics

Include a comparison table where possible:
- typical onset;
- approximate peak;
- usual acute duration;
- possible lingering symptoms;
- major reasons the timeline shifts.

Explicitly state that half-life is important but does not perfectly predict withdrawal. Active metabolites, tissue distribution, receptor kinetics, formulation, repeated exposure, dose pattern, organ function, and co-exposures can all matter.

Avoid falsely precise countdowns.

### 7. Within-class similarities and differences

For category pages, compare clinically meaningful members of the class.

Examples for opioids:
- heroin/short-acting full agonists;
- prescription short/intermediate-acting full agonists;
- fentanyl;
- methadone;
- buprenorphine;
- kratom/7-hydroxymitragynine products;
- tianeptine;
- novel or semi-synthetic opioids where human data exist.

Do not claim two substances are identical simply because they share a receptor.

### 8. Spontaneous vs precipitated withdrawal

When relevant, distinguish withdrawal caused by falling drug concentrations from withdrawal triggered by an antagonist or high-affinity partial agonist displacing a full agonist.

Explain why the distinction matters clinically without turning the page into a self-directed medication protocol.

### 9. Polysubstance and adulterant reality

Every major page must address how co-exposures can change the picture.

Examples:
- alcohol;
- benzodiazepines and other sedatives;
- stimulants;
- gabapentinoids;
- xylazine or other adulterants;
- multiple opioids.

A “normal” score for one withdrawal scale must not be presented as proof that no other withdrawal or medical problem exists.

### 10. What predicts severity?

Discuss evidence around:
- duration/frequency of exposure;
- dose and potency;
- formulation/route;
- short vs long acting kinetics;
- previous withdrawal episodes;
- medical and psychiatric comorbidity;
- pregnancy;
- sleep deprivation;
- pain;
- co-use;
- environmental instability;
- product uncertainty.

Do not reduce severity to dose alone when evidence does not support doing so.

### 11. Acute management

Cover evidence-based approaches without publishing unsupervised prescription dosing protocols.

For each approach state:
- what symptom/problem it targets;
- evidence level;
- important limitations;
- whether medical monitoring is typically needed;
- whether it treats withdrawal only or the underlying substance-use disorder.

### 12. Recovery is more than detox

A mandatory recovery section must cover:
- reduced tolerance and overdose vulnerability;
- craving and cue reactivity;
- sleep recovery;
- mood/anhedonia/anxiety;
- cognitive recovery when relevant;
- pain sensitivity;
- gastrointestinal recovery;
- hydration/nutrition where supported;
- rebuilding routine and function;
- treatment retention;
- social/environmental supports.

Use “post-acute withdrawal syndrome” cautiously. If discussed, state that PAWS is under-researched, lacks universally accepted diagnostic criteria, and can overlap with sleep disorders, depression/anxiety, pain, medication effects, and the underlying illness.

### 13. Treatment of the underlying disorder

If a well-supported treatment exists, it must be prominent.

For opioid use disorder, for example, withdrawal management alone is not adequate treatment; evidence-based medication treatment and continuing care materially change outcomes.

Do not frame indefinite maintenance treatment as a failure to “finish detox.”

### 14. Relapse/return-to-use and overdose prevention

For classes where tolerance changes overdose risk, explain it directly.

Include:
- loss of tolerance;
- contamination/potency uncertainty where relevant;
- co-use with depressants;
- naloxone/overdose reversal where relevant;
- treatment retention;
- transitions out of inpatient/residential/incarceration settings;
- why a previous amount may become dangerous after abstinence.

### 15. Special populations

Address when relevant:
- pregnancy/postpartum;
- adolescents;
- older adults;
- chronic pain;
- kidney/liver disease;
- serious psychiatric illness;
- people leaving incarceration or residential treatment.

### 16. Myths vs evidence

Include at least five high-value misconceptions when the evidence permits.

Examples:
- “Detox cures opioid addiction.”
- “If withdrawal is mild, dependence was not real.”
- “A long half-life always means more severe withdrawal.”
- “All opioids have the same timeline.”
- “Feeling bad for weeks proves permanent brain damage.”

### 17. Evidence ledger

Use a compact table:

| Claim | Evidence strength | Best evidence | Important limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Example claim | High / Moderate / Low / Unknown | Guideline, meta-analysis, cohort, case series | What the evidence cannot establish |

The page should make uncertainty inspectable rather than burying it in prose.

### 18. Evidence gaps

End with concrete unanswered questions:
- missing prospective timelines;
- under-studied products;
- treatment-comparison gaps;
- special-population gaps;
- long-term recovery outcomes;
- product/adulterant uncertainty;
- diagnostic-measurement problems.

## Citation standard

A flagship page should generally aim for **20–40 high-value references**, not citation padding.

Reference mix should normally include:
- current clinical guidance;
- at least two systematic reviews/meta-analyses when available;
- major outcome cohorts;
- pharmacokinetic or mechanistic human studies;
- substance-specific human evidence;
- current safety/regulatory sources for emerging products.

PMIDs and DOIs should be included whenever available.

Citations must support the sentence they are attached to. Do not cluster a dozen references at the end of a paragraph if only one supports the key claim.

## Safety and scope

These pages are educational and harm-reduction oriented.

They should:
- explain treatment options and evidence;
- encourage appropriate medical care;
- identify emergencies;
- explain why professional assessment matters;
- help readers understand questions to ask clinicians.

They should **not** become individualized detox prescriptions, home taper schedules, or unsupervised prescription-drug dosing instructions.

## Category coverage backlog

Every major category should receive a flagship withdrawal + recovery page, even when the conclusion is that a classic physical withdrawal syndrome is weak or absent.

Priority set:

1. Opioids
2. Alcohol
3. Benzodiazepines and Z-drugs / sedative-hypnotics
4. Stimulants (amphetamine, methamphetamine, cocaine)
5. Nicotine
6. Cannabis
7. Kratom / 7-OH and kratom-derived opioids
8. Gabapentinoids
9. Antidepressant discontinuation
10. Ketamine and dissociatives
11. GHB / GBL / 1,4-butanediol
12. Kava and other dependence-relevant botanicals
13. Tianeptine
14. Novel synthetic opioids / nitazenes and emerging opioid-like NPS
15. Psychedelics / entactogens — explicitly distinguishing after-effects, comedown, and psychiatric complications from a classic physical withdrawal syndrome when evidence does not support one.

High-risk individual substances can receive dedicated pages in addition to the category page.

## Definition of “finished”

A withdrawal/recovery article is Masterclass-complete only when it:
- satisfies the architecture above;
- has source-level citations;
- distinguishes established evidence from uncertainty;
- covers both acute withdrawal and longer recovery;
- includes treatment and mortality/harm-reduction context;
- handles within-class differences;
- links into the Substance Use evidence hub and relevant monographs;
- has passed normal content/build/link checks.

Until then, the page is **baseline coverage**, not finished.
