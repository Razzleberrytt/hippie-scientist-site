# RC / NPS Masterclass Monograph Standard — 2026-10-02

## Mission

The RC/NPS section is not a conventional drug blog. Its target is a **reference-grade harm-reduction and safety library**: the page a reader, family member, clinician, toxicologist, journalist, or search/answer engine can use to understand what a substance is, what the evidence actually establishes, where uncertainty remains, and what to do when use becomes dangerous.

A route existing is **not** the same thing as the article being finished.

A monograph is "masterclass complete" only when it answers the major identity, pharmacology, toxicology, dependence, withdrawal, support, history, regulatory, and evidence-quality questions that a serious reader is likely to have.

## Core editorial principles

1. **Harm reduction over fascination.** Explain enough chemistry and pharmacology to understand risk, never to optimize recreational use.
2. **No dosing guides.** Do not publish recreational dose ranges, redosing schedules, route-optimization, synthesis, sourcing, concealment, or potency-conversion instructions.
3. **Separate evidence levels.** Controlled human studies, observational clinical data, poison-center surveillance, forensic/postmortem evidence, animal studies, in-vitro receptor assays, and community anecdotes must never be blended into one level of certainty.
4. **Community reports are radar.** Reddit and other forums may identify aliases, emerging products, common misconceptions, and reader questions. They do not establish identity, purity, potency, pharmacokinetics, withdrawal severity, or safe use.
5. **Product identity is a scientific question.** Pressed pills, "botanical" products, powders, vapes, gummies, and branded extracts should be treated as chemically unknown unless analytical testing supports the claimed contents.
6. **Numbers need provenance.** Detection counts, blood concentrations, receptor affinities, fatality counts, prevalence percentages, and legal dates must be tied to a specific source and population.
7. **Do not create false precision.** A blood concentration from a death does not automatically define a lethal concentration. A receptor assay does not define a human dose. An animal potency ratio does not define human potency.
8. **Dependence is not the same as addiction.** Explain physical dependence, tolerance, withdrawal, compulsive use, craving, loss of control, and substance use disorder separately.
9. **Withdrawal advice must be safety-first.** Explain syndrome, danger signs, and evidence-based treatment pathways. Do not create DIY taper schedules for poorly characterized RCs.
10. **Legal claims are dated facts.** Use primary regulatory sources and state the jurisdiction and effective date. Never imply that one jurisdiction determines legality everywhere.
11. **Reference density should follow evidence density.** Do not pad a sparse compound with weak citations, but do not leave a well-studied compound with four references when twenty high-value primary sources exist.
12. **Every monograph should tell the reader what is still unknown.**

## Required monograph architecture

The exact order can vary for readability, but a complete page should normally cover the following.

### 1. Emergency safety box
For overdose-capable classes, open with the signs that require emergency care.

Examples:
- opioid: slow/abnormal breathing, blue/gray lips, unresponsiveness, naloxone context;
- benzodiazepine/sedative: profound sedation, airway/respiratory risk in combinations, seizure-prone withdrawal;
- stimulant: chest pain, seizure, hyperthermia, severe agitation, dangerous psychosis;
- synthetic cannabinoid: seizure, collapse, coma, severe confusion, cardiovascular instability;
- dissociative: unconsciousness, seizure, severe agitation, major injury, abnormal breathing.

### 2. Quick answer
In 2–5 paragraphs:
- what it is;
- drug class;
- why it matters now;
- strength of the human evidence;
- biggest safety issue;
- biggest misconception.

### 3. Identity card
When evidence exists, include:
- canonical name;
- aliases and common spellings;
- chemical class;
- CAS number;
- related compounds;
- whether it is a metabolite, analogue, prodrug, positional isomer, or distinct molecule;
- approved medical use: yes/no/where;
- current evidence grade;
- current U.S. federal status and international status, with dates;
- important state/local variability when material.

### 4. History and market emergence
Cover:
- origin in medicinal chemistry, pharmaceutical research, patents, or clandestine design;
- first scientific description when known;
- first forensic/government detection;
- geographic spread;
- major market substitutions;
- major warnings, scheduling actions, or outbreaks;
- how the compound became relevant to current users.

### 5. Chemistry and relationship to familiar drugs
Explain:
- parent scaffold;
- structural relationship to better-known drugs;
- what the structural comparison does and does not imply;
- isomer confusion;
- analytical identification problems.

Avoid synthesis instructions.

### 6. Pharmacodynamics
Use the strongest available evidence:
- receptor/transporter targets;
- agonist/antagonist/PAM/substrate/inhibitor mechanism;
- selectivity;
- efficacy vs affinity;
- important active metabolites;
- mechanistic explanation of major risks.

Clearly label in-vitro and animal findings.

### 7. Pharmacokinetics and metabolism
When available:
- absorption/route evidence in clinical or forensic literature;
- metabolism;
- CYP/UGT involvement;
- active metabolites;
- elimination information;
- analytical detection windows only when clinically/forensically justified and not framed to evade testing;
- major unknowns.

Do not invent onset/duration from forums.

### 8. Human effects: what is actually documented
Prioritize:
1. controlled human studies;
2. clinical case series;
3. poison-center cohorts;
4. prospective toxicology surveillance;
5. forensic impaired-driving data;
6. postmortem casework.

Separate subjective/therapeutic claims from analytically confirmed clinical findings.

### 9. Acute toxicity and overdose
Include:
- typical toxidrome;
- severe complications;
- case-series sizes;
- fatality evidence;
- co-drugs;
- why causal attribution can be difficult;
- emergency response principles;
- class-specific antidote/reversal context where appropriate.

### 10. Interactions and polysubstance risk
Cover high-value combinations by pharmacology:
- opioids + benzodiazepines;
- alcohol + sedatives;
- serotonergic combinations;
- stimulant stacking;
- MAOI interactions;
- multiple opioids;
- multiple synthetic cannabinoids;
- unknown gray-market mixtures.

Avoid presenting combinations as a menu of effects.

### 11. Tolerance
Explain:
- whether tolerance is established directly or inferred from class;
- likely cross-tolerance when evidence supports it;
- why loss of tolerance can increase overdose risk.

### 12. Physical dependence
State:
- whether direct human dependence is documented;
- whether it is class-inferred only;
- how dependence differs from compulsive use/addiction.

### 13. Withdrawal
This section is mandatory for dependence-capable substances.

Cover:
- documented or expected syndrome;
- approximate timing only when supported by clinical literature;
- common vs severe features;
- seizure/delirium/autonomic/respiratory dangers when relevant;
- why abrupt discontinuation may be dangerous;
- what evidence does **not** exist for that specific RC.

Never publish an improvised recreational taper schedule.

### 14. Addiction / substance use disorder education
Explain in non-stigmatizing language:
- craving;
- inability to cut down;
- escalating use;
- using despite harm;
- tolerance and withdrawal;
- time spent obtaining/recovering;
- interference with work, relationships, or responsibilities.

Make clear that physical dependence alone is not identical to a substance use disorder.

### 15. Treatment and support
Give actionable but non-prescriptive support:
- when emergency care is appropriate;
- when medically supervised withdrawal is appropriate;
- addiction-medicine / substance-use treatment pathways;
- evidence-based treatment principles relevant to the class;
- SAMHSA FindTreatment.gov and National Helpline for U.S. readers;
- peer/family support as an adjunct, not a substitute for urgent medical care.

When compound-specific treatment evidence is absent, say so and use established class-level guidance without pretending it was studied for that RC.

### 16. Drug checking, toxicology, and detection
Explain:
- whether routine immunoassays detect it;
- mass-spectrometry methods used;
- major metabolites used as markers;
- false-negative/false-positive or cross-reactivity concerns;
- isomer differentiation issues;
- why a certificate of analysis is limited to analytes actually tested.

Do not write test-evasion guidance.

### 17. Counterfeit and mislabeled product risk
When relevant:
- counterfeit pharmaceutical appearance;
- wrong-drug cases;
- contamination;
- batch variability;
- tablet/powder quantification studies;
- unintentional exposure evidence.

### 18. Forensic interpretation
Explain:
- postmortem redistribution when relevant;
- concentration overlap between fatal/nonfatal cases;
- role of tolerance and co-drugs;
- why a detected compound is not automatically "the cause of death."

### 19. Epidemiology and current trends
Use dated populations:
- poison-center calls;
- ED surveillance;
- seized-drug detections;
- mortality datasets;
- geographic trends.

Never imply a local dataset is globally representative.

### 20. Legal and regulatory history
At minimum:
- U.S. federal status with effective date;
- relevant DEA/Federal Register history;
- WHO/CND international status;
- selected national/state controls only where useful;
- explicit note that laws change and jurisdiction matters.

### 21. Special populations
Where evidence supports it, discuss:
- pregnancy/breastfeeding;
- adolescents;
- older adults;
- respiratory disease;
- seizure disorders;
- liver/kidney disease;
- people taking opioids, sedatives, stimulants, serotonergic drugs, or other interacting medications.

Do not invent special-population data when none exist.

### 22. Myths and common misconceptions
Use short evidence-correcting sections, for example:
- "It feels like alprazolam, so the dose is equivalent."
- "No fentanyl on the label means there is no opioid."
- "A negative routine drug screen rules it out."
- "A fatal blood level defines a lethal dose."
- "Plant/botanical branding means non-opioid."

### 23. What we know vs what remains unknown
End the scientific body with a compact evidence ledger:
- established;
- probable;
- uncertain;
- not established.

### 24. FAQ / citation questions
Answer the questions people actually search, but preserve evidence limits.

### 25. Related evidence
Link:
- parent family hub;
- close analogues;
- comparison pages;
- dependence/withdrawal resources;
- emergency/harm-reduction hubs.

## Citation standard

### Minimum expectation
There is no arbitrary quota, but a masterclass monograph should normally have:
- **well-developed compound:** 15–30+ strong references;
- **moderate evidence:** 10–20;
- **emerging sparse compound:** every meaningful primary source plus authoritative monographs/alerts, even if that is only 5–10.

### Preferred source hierarchy
1. PubMed-indexed controlled human studies
2. Prospective clinical toxicology
3. Poison-center / public-health surveillance
4. Peer-reviewed forensic toxicology
5. Peer-reviewed analytical/metabolism studies
6. Systematic reviews / high-quality narrative reviews
7. WHO / UN / DEA / FDA / CDC / SAMHSA / CFSRE and equivalent authoritative bodies
8. High-quality drug-checking datasets
9. Community reports only as clearly labeled discovery signals

### Reference metadata
When available, include:
- full title;
- authors;
- year;
- PMID;
- DOI;
- canonical URL.

## Evidence-label language

Use explicit phrases such as:
- **Controlled human evidence**
- **Clinical observational evidence**
- **Poison-center surveillance**
- **Forensic/postmortem evidence**
- **Animal evidence**
- **In-vitro/receptor evidence**
- **Community signal only**

This prevents answer engines from flattening evidence quality.

## Category-specific mandatory modules

### Benzodiazepines / sedatives
Must include:
- amnesia and behavioral impairment;
- opioid/alcohol interaction;
- physical dependence;
- seizure/delirium withdrawal risk;
- distinction between dependence and benzodiazepine use disorder;
- class-level guideline support when compound-specific withdrawal data are absent.

### Opioids
Must include:
- respiratory depression;
- naloxone;
- dependence and withdrawal;
- tolerance/loss of tolerance;
- fentanyl/counterfeit overlap;
- opioid use disorder treatment context;
- routine testing limitations.

### Stimulants / cathinones
Must include:
- cardiovascular toxicity;
- hyperthermia;
- seizure;
- psychosis/agitation;
- sleep deprivation and compulsive redosing as harm amplifiers;
- stimulant use disorder support.

### Psychedelics
Must include:
- identity/mislabeling;
- serotonin-related interactions;
- severe agitation/seizure/hyperthermia when relevant;
- distinction between classic-psychedelic assumptions and compound-specific evidence.

### Dissociatives
Must include:
- impaired consciousness;
- accidents/injury;
- agitation/delirium;
- duration uncertainty;
- ketamine/PCP assay limitations;
- urinary toxicity only when evidence supports transfer.

### Synthetic cannabinoids
Must include:
- distinction from THC;
- high-efficacy CB1 agonism when established;
- seizure/coma/psychosis/cardiovascular risk;
- uneven spraying/concentration and mislabeled vape/herbal products.

### Retail / botanical-labeled gray-market products
Must include four separate evidence lanes:
1. laboratory-confirmed contents;
2. poison-center/public-health suspicion;
3. manufacturer label claims;
4. self-reported effects.

Never collapse those categories.

## Definition of done

A monograph is **not complete** until:
- the major sections above are either answered or explicitly marked "not established";
- current legal status has a dated primary source;
- dependence/withdrawal/addiction/support are addressed for dependence-capable drugs;
- serious interaction risks are addressed;
- reference metadata is rich;
- internal links are in place;
- duplicate intent has been checked;
- factual claims survive content lint / claim-drift / source verification;
- a reader can understand both the danger **and** the limits of the evidence without needing Reddit to fill the gaps.
