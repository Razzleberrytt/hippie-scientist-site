# Withdrawal & Recovery Masterclass Standard

> Permanent editorial standard for The Hippie Scientist withdrawal and recovery coverage.
> Adopted: 2026-10-03

## Purpose

Withdrawal pages are not short symptom lists. Each major psychoactive-drug category should have a flagship withdrawal-and-recovery resource that explains the biology, time course, acute risks, treatment evidence, and the longer recovery period with the same source discipline used for Masterclass monographs.

The goal is to make these pages useful to a reader trying to understand **what is happening, what differs by substance, what requires medical care, what treatment evidence is strongest, and what recovery actually looks like after the acute syndrome ends**.

## Required coverage

Every category flagship must cover, when evidence exists:

1. **Scope and definitions**
   - physical dependence vs tolerance vs withdrawal vs substance use disorder;
   - spontaneous vs precipitated withdrawal;
   - medically supervised taper/discontinuation vs withdrawal after nonmedical use;
   - acute withdrawal vs longer post-acute recovery symptoms.

2. **Mechanism**
   - receptor/transporter adaptations;
   - autonomic and neuroendocrine changes;
   - why discontinuation produces the characteristic syndrome;
   - what mechanistic evidence can and cannot prove clinically.

3. **Timeline**
   - onset, peak, acute duration, and possible prolonged symptoms;
   - short-acting vs long-acting agents;
   - formulation, route, active metabolites, tissue redistribution, and repeated-exposure effects;
   - an explicit statement that timelines are population ranges, not personal countdown clocks.

4. **Within-class comparison**
   - similarities shared by the drug class;
   - clinically meaningful differences among common members;
   - special cases with mixed mechanisms or unusual pharmacokinetics;
   - novel compounds where evidence is too sparse to infer a timeline.

5. **Severity and complications**
   - common symptoms;
   - dehydration, aspiration, cardiovascular stress, seizures/delirium where relevant;
   - mental-health and suicide risk;
   - pregnancy;
   - polysubstance withdrawal and why another drug class may dominate medical risk.

6. **Assessment**
   - validated withdrawal scales when available;
   - what the scales measure;
   - limitations, especially for novel compounds or mixed withdrawal states.

7. **Evidence-based management**
   - first-line treatment approaches;
   - medications with regulatory approval where applicable;
   - off-label options supported by trials or systematic reviews;
   - supportive care;
   - treatment-evidence hierarchy;
   - **no unsupervised prescription dosing recipes or copyable detox schedules**.

8. **Recovery after acute withdrawal**
   - sleep, mood, anhedonia, fatigue, craving, pain, cognition, and stress reactivity;
   - what evidence supports a persistent/post-acute syndrome and where the concept is poorly defined;
   - expected variability;
   - functional recovery and quality of life.

9. **Relapse and mortality**
   - loss of tolerance;
   - overdose risk after abstinence or treatment discontinuation;
   - evidence for maintenance treatment where applicable;
   - naloxone/overdose-prevention context for opioids;
   - why “completed detox” does not equal treated substance use disorder.

10. **Special populations**
    - pregnancy;
    - adolescents/older adults when data exist;
    - chronic pain or prescribed-use populations;
    - hepatic/renal disease when relevant;
    - co-occurring psychiatric illness;
    - polysubstance use.

11. **Myths and evidence ledger**
    - common internet claims;
    - verdict limited to what the evidence establishes;
    - direct vs indirect evidence;
    - uncertainty made visible.

12. **Help and escalation**
    - emergency red flags;
    - U.S. treatment resources where appropriate;
    - poison-center resources for uncertain product exposures;
    - crisis resources when suicide risk is discussed.

13. **References**
    - prioritize systematic reviews, controlled trials, prospective cohorts, major guidelines, government/public-health sources, and primary human studies;
    - include PMID/DOI whenever available;
    - distinguish guideline/consensus evidence from trials;
    - include recent evidence and classic foundational sources when still relevant.

## Page-quality requirements

- Target **25+ strong sources** for a mature category flagship when the literature supports it.
- Prefer primary human evidence and systematic reviews over tertiary summaries.
- Use exact dates for regulatory or guideline status.
- Never transfer a withdrawal timeline from a related compound to a novel compound without labeling it as extrapolation.
- Never convert receptor potency, animal data, or half-life alone into a claimed human withdrawal severity ranking.
- Explicitly distinguish **withdrawal management** from **ongoing treatment/recovery**.
- Avoid stigmatizing language.
- Keep emergency guidance visible without making the entire page alarmist.
- Add internal links to relevant monographs and drug-specific withdrawal pages.
- Update the page when treatment guidance, illicit-market composition, or major evidence changes.

## Category flagship backlog

### P0 — highest clinical value / strongest withdrawal hazard

- **Opioids** — heroin, fentanyl, prescription full agonists, methadone, buprenorphine, tramadol/tapentadol, kratom/7-OH, tianeptine, novel synthetic opioids.
- **Alcohol** — acute withdrawal, seizures/delirium tremens, kindling, benzodiazepine treatment evidence, thiamine, recovery.
- **Benzodiazepines & Z-drugs** — short- vs long-acting agents, seizure risk, protracted symptoms, medically supervised tapering, cross-tolerance.
- **GHB / GBL / 1,4-butanediol** — rapid severe withdrawal, delirium/seizures, ICU-level risk, limited protocol evidence.
- **Gabapentinoids** — gabapentin and pregabalin dependence/withdrawal, seizure risk, opioid co-use.

### P1 — common and high-value

- **Stimulants** — cocaine, amphetamine, methamphetamine, prescription stimulants, cathinones; crash vs withdrawal, sleep, depression, anhedonia, craving, suicide risk.
- **Nicotine** — cigarettes, vaping, pouches; withdrawal trajectory, NRT/varenicline/bupropion evidence, relapse prevention.
- **Cannabis** — sleep, irritability, appetite, anxiety, timing, treatment evidence.
- **Kratom / mitragynine / concentrated 7-OH** — dedicated flagship separate from conventional opioids because the evidence base and product chemistry differ.
- **Tianeptine** — dedicated MOR-active atypical-drug withdrawal/recovery page.

### P2 — specialized / emerging

- **Phenibut / baclofen / GABA-B agonists**.
- **Barbiturates and other sedative-hypnotics**.
- **Synthetic cannabinoids**.
- **Dissociatives** including ketamine and PCP-like NPS.
- **Antidepressant discontinuation** — explicitly separated from addiction framing.
- **Antipsychotic and other psychotropic discontinuation** where site scope justifies coverage.
- **Psychedelics / serotonergic hallucinogens** — primarily a “why classic physical withdrawal is not typical” evidence page rather than forcing a syndrome that is not established.
- **Novel psychoactive substances by pharmacologic class** — create only when evidence permits; otherwise link the class flagship and state the evidence gap.

## Drug-specific child pages

A category flagship should link to narrower pages when one member materially differs in:

- half-life or receptor kinetics;
- withdrawal medical danger;
- treatment approach;
- mixed pharmacology;
- market composition;
- pregnancy considerations;
- regulatory status;
- or quality of direct human evidence.

The child page should not duplicate the flagship. It should answer the substance-specific questions and send readers back to the category page for the shared physiology and recovery framework.

## First implementation

The first flagship implementing this standard is:

- `/articles/opioid-withdrawal-recovery-comparison/`

It should become the canonical shared background page linked from heroin/fentanyl, prescription-opioid, methadone, buprenorphine, tramadol, kratom/7-OH, tianeptine, and novel-opioid content.
