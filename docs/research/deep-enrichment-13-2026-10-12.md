## DE13-01 — Extended-release niacin + background statin: surrogate gains are not fewer events

**Question:** Does raising HDL and lowering triglycerides by adding prescription-strength niacin reduce coronary events in statin-treated vascular-disease populations?

**Primary study A:** AIM-HIGH Investigators (2011), [PMID 22085343](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22085343/), DOI [10.1056/NEJMoa1107579](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1107579). Randomized, double-blind clinical trial, **3,414** adults with established vascular disease and LDL controlled with statin-based therapy. Niacin versus comparator for mean **3 years**, stopped for lack of efficacy.

**AIM-HIGH results:** Primary composite vascular outcome **16.4% niacin vs 16.2% comparator**; HR **1.02**, 95% CI **0.87–1.21**, p=.79 (null). At two years HDL increased, triglycerides decreased, and LDL decreased significantly in niacin group, **without clinical-event improvement**. Neither favorable HDL change nor a numerical endpoint difference can be recast as heart-attack protection.

**Primary study B:** HPS2-THRIVE Collaborative Group (2014), [PMID 25014686](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25014686/), DOI [10.1056/NEJMoa1300955](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1300955). Randomized **25,673** participants with atherosclerotic vascular disease already receiving statin-based LDL lowering; **niacin plus laropiprant** vs placebo, median **3.9 years**. Major vascular events **13.2% vs 13.7%**; RR **0.96**, 95% CI **0.90–1.03**, p=.29 (null). Significantly increased serious diabetes-control problems, new diabetes diagnoses, gastrointestinal, musculoskeletal, infection, and bleeding event categories.

**Key safety/identity distinction:** HPS2-THRIVE tested a **combination (niacin + laropiprant)**; its harm magnitude must not automatically be attributed to isolated niacin, food niacin, or a routine multivitamin. AIM-HIGH and HPS2 are **different trials and participant populations** and may be described as convergent null clinical-outcome evidence, without pooling hazards from mismatched formulations.

**Research verdict:** Human RCT, strong negative support against an unqualified claim that raising HDL with high-exposure niacin plus background statins prevents cardiovascular events. **P0 candidate**, but no current-page defect assumed until live content is inspected.

## DE13-02 — Calcium carbonate + vitamin D in the WHI: fracture endpoints vs kidney stones

**Question:** In generally healthy postmenopausal women, does combined supplementation reduce fractures without harm?

**Primary parent trial:** Jackson et al. (2006), [PMID 16481635](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16481635/), DOI [10.1056/NEJMoa055218](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa055218). Randomized placebo-controlled WHI trial in **36,282 postmenopausal women**, follow-up approximately **7 years**. Calcium carbonate plus vitamin D vs placebo.

**Parent results:** Hip bone mineral density **1.06% higher** with active supplementation, but ITT hip-fracture HR **0.88**, 95% CI **0.72–1.08** (not significant); total-fracture HR **0.96**, 95% CI **0.91–1.02** (not significant). Urinary calculus incidence **increased** (HR **1.17**, 95% CI **1.02–1.34**).

**Same-cohort secondary paper:** Wallace et al. (2011), [PMID 21525191](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21525191/), DOI [10.3945/ajcn.110.003350](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.110.003350). This is an analysis of **the same WHI randomization**, **not** an independent safety replication: **449** participants reported urinary stones in active group vs **381** in placebo, HR 1.17 (95% CI 1.02–1.34). Outcome was **reported clinician-diagnosed urinary stone**, not laboratory-confirmed composition or proof of individual causation.

**Important constraints:** WHI is combined calcium + vitamin D, **not** a vitamin-D-only test. The 7-year kidney-stone signal should not be extrapolated to food calcium, all supplement forms, men, people with diagnosed deficiency, or a different dose. Adherence-adjusted hip-fracture comparisons differed from ITT and cannot displace prespecified ITT evidence without explanation.

**Research verdict:** **P0** benefit-risk precision and combination firewall. Avoid blanket “no benefit for anyone” and avoid “bone-density increase = proven fracture prevention.”

## DE13-03 — Vitamin D-only fracture prevention in VITAL general population

**Question:** Does vitamin D by itself reduce incident fractures in people not selected for deficiency or osteoporosis?

**Primary:** LeBoff et al. (2022), [PMID 35939577](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35939577/), DOI [10.1056/NEJMoa2202106](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2202106). Ancillary randomized analysis of parent VITAL trial (two-by-two factorial design); **25,871** participants, median **5.3 years**. Randomized vitamin D3 vs placebo; not enrolled based on low vitamin D, osteopenia, or osteoporosis.

**Prespecified outcomes:** **Total fractures:** 769/12,927 vs 782/12,944; HR **0.98**, 95% CI **0.89–1.08**, p=.70. **Nonvertebral** fracture HR **0.97** (95% CI .87–1.07; p=.50); **hip** fracture HR **1.01** (95% CI .70–1.47; p=.96). No clear modification by measured baseline 25-hydroxyvitamin D in this study, but limited deficiency sampling does not settle efficacy in profound deficiency.

**Contrast and guardrail:** WHI (DE13-02) studied **calcium plus vitamin D in postmenopausal women**; VITAL studied **vitamin D randomized independently** among older men and women. Do not pool or call them same intervention. Neither result rules out clinical treatment of confirmed deficiency or osteoporosis-specific prevention plans.

**Research verdict:** P1 population-specific null. “Vitamin D prevents fractures in everyone” is unsupported by VITAL, whereas deficiency-treatment advice requires a different evidence frame.

## DE13-04 — Ginkgo biloba GEM study: dementia prevention null

**Question:** Does a standardized Ginkgo extract lower incident all-cause dementia or Alzheimer disease in adults aged 75+?

**Primary:** DeKosky et al. (2008), [PMID 19017911](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19017911/), DOI [10.1001/jama.2008.683](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2008.683). Multi-center double-blind placebo-controlled **3,069-person** GEM trial; adults at least 75, some with MCI, median **6.1-year** follow-up.

**Results:** **523** participants developed dementia (277 Ginkgo, 246 placebo); all-cause dementia HR **1.12**, 95% CI **0.94–1.33**, p=.21; Alzheimer disease HR **1.16**, 95% CI **0.97–1.39**, p=.11. Baseline MCI progression to dementia HR **1.13**, 95% CI **0.85–1.50**, p=.39. None statistically significant and **none shows prevention benefit**.

**Null nuance:** Numerical HRs above 1 do **not** establish statistically significant increased dementia risk; do not overread as proven harm. Prevention over years is different from short-term working-memory/attention claims, which require other outcome-specific trials. Studied extract must not generalize to every leaf powder or dose.

**Research verdict:** P1 null. Existing repository article `content/articles/ginkgo-biloba.md` already references PMID **19017911**: reconcile/reuse source instead of adding duplicate.

## DE13-05 — Omega-3 fish oil for diagnosed dry-eye disease: DREAM trial

**Question:** Can popular omega-3 cardiovascular or anti-inflammatory rationale establish a dry-eye symptom benefit?

**Primary:** Dry Eye Assessment and Management Study Research Group / Asbell et al. (2018), [PMID 29652551](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29652551/), DOI [10.1056/NEJMoa1709691](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1709691). Randomized placebo-controlled **535** adults with moderate-to-severe dry-eye disease, twelve months. A high-EPA/DHA **fish-derived** combination was compared with an **olive-oil placebo**.

**Primary result:** Mean Ocular Surface Disease Index change −13.9 active vs −12.5 placebo; imputation-adjusted mean difference **−1.9**, 95% CI **−5.0 to +1.1**, **p=.21** (null). Prespecified ocular signs — conjunctival/corneal staining, tear breakup time and Schirmer's test — also **not significantly different**.

**Limitations:** A null trial of specific fish-derived high-dose preparation in defined dry-eye patients does not rule out all omega-3 benefits for other endpoints (e.g. blood triglycerides), all subgroups, or different interventions. Olive oil was the comparator, not necessarily a pharmacologically inert oil; do not suggest omega-3 made the disease worse.

**Research verdict:** P1 efficacy-indication firewall. No automatic change to cardiovascular omega-3 pages based on an ocular trial.

## DE13-06 — PLACIDE four-strain probiotics: antibiotic-associated diarrhea null

**Question:** Does one multi-strain capsule prevent antibiotic-associated diarrhea in older hospitalized patients?

**Primary:** Allen et al. (2013), PLACIDE RCT report [PMID 24309198](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24309198/), DOI [10.3310/hta17570](https://doi.org/10.3310/hta17570). Randomized double-blind placebo controlled **2,981** people age ≥65 admitted to UK hospitals and exposed to antibiotics; **2,941** in allocated-treatment analysis. Four defined Lactobacillus/Bifidobacterium strains in specific study product; not an entire probiotic class comparison.

**Results:** Antibiotic-associated diarrhea (AAD) **159/1,470 (10.8%) probiotic vs 153/1,471 (10.4%) placebo**; RR **1.04**, 95% CI **0.84–1.28**, p=.71 (null). C. difficile diarrhea **12 vs 17**, RR **0.71**, 95% CI **0.34–1.47**, p=.35 (nonsignificant, rare event). No meaningful group difference in reported severity, duration, serious adverse events or cost.

**Boundaries:** AAD and C. difficile-associated diarrhea are distinct endpoints; do not claim C. difficile risk reduction from a numerical but nonsignificant ratio. A specific combination in older inpatients does not refute every strain/species studied in different settings, and lack of excess events does not prove safety in high-risk immunocompromised patients (these were excluded).

**Research verdict:** P1 null/strain-population specificity; this RCT is already described in another long-form institutional report, but all PLACIDE publications are **one clinical trial, not independent replications**.

## DE13-07 — Echinacea angustifolia root in experimental rhinovirus challenge

**Question:** Does prophylactic or early-treatment use of distinct standardized Echinacea root extracts prevent rhinovirus infection or shorten experimentally induced cold illness?

**Primary:** Turner et al. (2005), [PMID 16049208](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16049208/), DOI [10.1056/NEJMoa044441](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa044441). Randomized **437 volunteers** across prophylaxis-versus-treatment designs and three chemistry-defined **Echinacea angustifolia root extracts**; analysis of **399** successfully challenged with rhinovirus type 39 in isolated setting.

**Results:** **No statistically significant benefit** for infection rates, cold-symptom severity, nasal-secretions volume, nasal lavage neutrophils/interleukin-8, or viral titer for the tested extracts. Outcomes were **not** proof that every Echinacea species, plant part, or preparation has an identical effect.

**Boundary:** Experimentally inoculated virus and precisely controlled extracts differ from community respiratory infections and proprietary multi-herb blends. Null observed trial is still material: cannot claim these tested extracts “stop cold viruses” based on in-vitro immune-modulation speculation. No recommended amounts.

**Research verdict:** P1 evidence-correction candidate with phytochemistry/species firewall.

## DE13-08 — Magnesium oxide for nocturnal leg cramps: both arms got better

**Question:** Does reduced cramp frequency from baseline constitute superiority over placebo?

**Primary:** Roguin Maor et al. (2017), [PMID 28241153](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28241153/), DOI [10.1001/jamainternmed.2016.9261](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2016.9261). Community randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial: **94 randomized** adults with frequent nocturnal leg cramps, four-week oral magnesium oxide intervention vs placebo.

**Primary outcome:** Cramp reductions **−3.41/week** magnesium vs **−3.03/week** placebo; between-group difference **0.38 cramps/week**, p=.67 (null). No significant group differences in cramp duration/severity, sleep or quality of life. Magnesium exposure did not beat placebo despite within-group changes.

**Boundaries:** Study is about **nonpregnant adults with idiopathic nighttime leg cramps** and **magnesium oxide**. Null does not rule out correcting diagnosed magnesium deficiency or magnesium interventions in pregnancy, and cannot be generalized as “magnesium does nothing medically.” Manufacturer supplied product/support; evaluate potential conflict.

**Research verdict:** P1 before/after versus between-group precision, no dose guidance.

## DE13-09 — Iron supplementation and fatigue: only for documented low-ferritin population

**Question:** Can iron help fatigue without anemia; does a trial justify universal daily iron for unexplained tiredness?

**Primary:** Vaucher et al. (2012), [PMID 22777991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22777991/), DOI [10.1503/cmaj.110950](https://doi.org/10.1503/cmaj.110950). Multicenter randomized, observer-blinded trial (**closed-label** — not double-blinded) in **198 menstruating women** aged 18–53 with reported unexplained fatigue, hemoglobin >12 g/dL, and ferritin **below 50 µg/L**, 12 weeks. Studied iron form ferrous sulfate.

**Results:** Fatigue scale decreased **47.7% iron vs 28.8% placebo**, difference **−18.9 percentage points** (95% CI −34.5 to −3.2, p=.02). Blood ferritin and hemoglobin improved. **Quality of life (p=.2), depression (p=.97), and anxiety (p=.5) did not differ significantly** between groups.

**Critical limit:** Findings apply only to the selected menstruating, low-ferritin population; fatigue is not a diagnostic test for iron deficiency. Do not generalize to men, children, normal-ferritin adults, iron-overload disorders, or iron self-treatment. Trial studied quantity is exposure documentation, not advice.

**Research verdict:** **P0** population/diagnostic gate. A source-integrity research record already exists at `ops/enrichment-submissions/sessions/session-c/2026-08-30-iron-fatigue-source-integrity.json` for this PMID; reuse it only if the canonical source mapping is valid.

## DE13-10 — S-adenosylmethionine (SAMe): small adjunctive trial, not an OTC antidepressant equivalence study

**Question:** Does a short-term positive adjunct trial prove an independent antidepressant alternative or safe unsupervised stacking?

**Primary:** Papakostas et al. (2010), [PMID 20595412](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20595412/), DOI [10.1176/appi.ajp.2009.09081198](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2009.09081198). Randomized, double-blind placebo-controlled **73** adult patients with major depressive disorder who were inadequate responders to an existing serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SRI). Participants stayed on stable SRI therapy; six weeks.

**Results:** HAM-D defined **response: 36.1% SAMe adjunct vs 17.6% placebo adjunct**; **remission: 25.8% vs 11.7%**. NNT estimates ~6 for response and ~7 for remission from this small study. Discontinuation proportions due to adverse events did not significantly differ (5.1% vs 8.8%), **which does not establish long-term safety or absence of interactions**.

**Interpretive limits:** Adjunctive strategy in **selected SRI nonresponders** is not SAMe monotherapy, a generic wellness/mood booster, clinical equivalence to antidepressants, or proof that combining any supplement with serotonergic therapy is safe. Short trial underpowered to establish uncommon harms, mania/hypomania risk or long-term disease outcomes. Independent medication/interaction review required before public consumer instructions.

**Research verdict:** **P0** safety-interaction/editorial hold, **P1** replication/efficacy certainty. No advice to alter prescribed therapy.
