# Sleepwalking / NREM Parasomnias — Evidence Dossier

Date: 2026-09-04
Status: publication-ready support for `content/articles/sleepwalking-nrem-parasomnias.md`

## Research question

How should the site explain sleepwalking and other NREM disorders of arousal while preserving the distinction from REM parasomnias, separating supported triggers from case-report folklore, and keeping safety ahead of medication or supplement claims?

## Editorial verdict

Sleepwalking is an **NREM disorder of arousal** characterized by incomplete awakening from deep sleep with complex behavior, impaired judgment and variable recall.

Most episodes are benign or transient, but recurrent or complex episodes can create injury, social, occupational or forensic consequences.

Management hierarchy:
1. safety;
2. adequate sleep opportunity;
3. remove or treat precipitating factors;
4. evaluate comorbid sleep disorders;
5. use targeted behavioral or clinical treatment when episodes remain distressing or dangerous.

Evidence grade: **Moderate** for mechanism/diagnostic framing; **Low-to-Moderate** for specific behavioral treatments.

## Anchor evidence

### 1. 2024 Somnambulism review

PMID: 38368068

Key points:
- sleepwalking is an NREM parasomnia;
- altered state of consciousness with impaired judgment;
- continuum with confusional arousal and night terror;
- usually benign but can have significant personal/social/forensic consequences;
- differential diagnosis is important.

Editorial use:
- primary definition/mechanism source;
- keep NREM vs REM distinction explicit.

### 2. 2023 behavioral-treatment systematic review

PMID: 37716336

- 72 publications;
- 68% case reports;
- 21% case series;
- only 3 RCTs;
- most common approaches: hypnosis, psychotherapy, sleep hygiene, education/reassurance, relaxation, scheduled awakenings, sleep extension/naps, mindfulness;
- some support for multicomponent CBT, sleep hygiene, scheduled awakenings and hypnosis;
- evidence limited by uncontrolled designs and inconsistent outcomes.

Editorial use:
- central evidence-quality guardrail;
- do not count treatment reports as equivalent to RCT evidence.

### 3. 2023 diagnosis/management review

PMID: 37046480

Key points:
- disorders of arousal arise from incomplete NREM arousal;
- amnesia and absence of conscious experience are not sensitive enough as absolute diagnostic requirements;
- home video and video-polysomnography can aid work-up;
- proposes levels of diagnostic certainty;
- emphasizes gaps preventing highly standardized treatment guidelines.

Editorial use:
- do not say “if you remember it, it was not sleepwalking.”

### 4. 2023 NREM parasomnia clinical review

PMID: 37590825

Key points:
- NREM parasomnias range from minor to severe;
- safety should be addressed for all patients;
- nonpharmacologic treatment should be prioritized;
- increasing total sleep time, avoiding triggering substances and treating comorbid sleep disorders is often sufficient.

Editorial use:
- supports management hierarchy;
- safety before medication.

### 5. 2024 sleep-deprivation study

PMID: 38972128

Population:
- 124 consecutively assessed adult sleepwalkers.

Design:
- baseline PSG vs recovery sleep after 25 hours of sleep deprivation.

Key results:
- nearly twice as many somnambulistic events recorded after deprivation;
- proportion with at least one lab episode increased **48% → 63%**;
- 17% had an event only during recovery sleep;
- 2% had an event only at baseline.

Editorial use:
- unusually strong support for sleep deprivation as a facilitating factor;
- do not suggest home sleep-deprivation testing.

### 6. 2025 psychopathology systematic review

PMID: 39721218

- psychopathological and neurodevelopmental issues more common in NREM parasomnia populations than comparison populations;
- NREM parasomnias more common across several mental-disorder groups;
- relationship remains multifactorial.

Editorial use:
- stress/mental health associations matter;
- do not claim psychiatric illness is the single cause.

### 7. 2025 hypnosis systematic review

PMID: 41478063

- 24 studies;
- 348 individuals described;
- 148 received hypnosis;
- about 82.6% positive clinical response reported;
- most evidence case reports/small series;
- only one controlled study;
- overall evidence quality low.

Editorial use:
- “promising” is appropriate;
- “82.6% effective” without study-quality context is not.

### 8. Predisposing / priming / precipitating factors review

PMID: 17208473

Important points:
- proposed priming factors include sleep deprivation, stress, fever, alcohol, medications and others;
- experimental support is stronger for sleep deprivation than for many medication/alcohol claims;
- many alcohol/medication associations derive from case reports or forensic reports;
- sleep-disordered breathing, periodic leg movements, noise and touch can act as proximal arousal triggers;
- treating triggers can resolve events in some cases.

Editorial use:
- prevent case-report folklore from being presented as settled causality.

### 9. Adult NREM parasomnia update

PMID: 33089156

Use for:
- adult presentation;
- differential diagnosis;
- pathophysiology;
- precipitating factors;
- medico-legal context.

## Core distinctions to preserve

### Sleepwalking ≠ sleep paralysis

Sleepwalking:
- NREM disorder of arousal;
- motor behavior emerges.

Sleep paralysis:
- REM-wake dissociation;
- motor atonia persists despite awareness.

### Sleepwalking ≠ REM sleep behavior disorder

Sleepwalking originates from NREM sleep.
RBD involves loss of normal REM atonia and dream enactment.

### Sleepwalking ≠ any complex nighttime behavior

Differential diagnoses include:
- nocturnal seizures;
- RBD;
- intoxication/medication impairment;
- sleep-related eating disorder;
- other parasomnias.

## Safety rule

For recurrent or complex episodes, safety is part of treatment even when no medication is used.

Allowed safety framing:
- clear obvious environmental hazards;
- secure stairs/windows/exits when warranted by episode severity;
- avoid dangerous sleeping setups during active severe episodes;
- household response should remain calm and non-confrontational.

Do not sensationalize rare forensic cases as typical sleepwalking.

## Sleep-deprivation rule

Strong evidence supports sleep deprivation as a facilitator in susceptible sleepwalkers.

Never recommend intentional sleep deprivation outside diagnostic research/clinical context.

Preferred wording:
> Correct chronic sleep restriction; do not use sleep deprivation as a home provocation test.

## Alcohol / medication rule

Temporal association does not establish a genuine parasomnia mechanism.

Some complex behaviors under alcohol or medication exposure may reflect intoxication or impairment rather than sleepwalking.

Do not publish:
- “Alcohol causes sleepwalking” as universal fact;
- “Drug X caused the episode” without clinical context.

## Treatment-evidence hierarchy

### Better supported as first-line management principles
- safety;
- adequate sleep opportunity;
- addressing triggers;
- treating comorbid sleep disorders;
- education/reassurance.

### Some evidence but limited RCT base
- multicomponent CBT;
- scheduled awakenings;
- hypnosis;
- other behavioral approaches.

### Weak direct evidence
- broad pharmacologic treatment;
- supplements/herbs;
- generic “deep sleep” products.

## Hypnosis guardrail

The 82.6% response figure is derived mostly from uncontrolled reports.

Always pair it with:
- low evidence quality;
- mostly case reports/series;
- only one controlled study in the 2025 review.

## Supplement inheritance rule

Do not transfer insomnia evidence into sleepwalking.

Examples:
- magnesium sleep-quality data ≠ NREM parasomnia treatment;
- melatonin timing data ≠ sleepwalking prevention;
- sedating herb data ≠ disorders-of-arousal evidence.

## Claims that must not reappear

- “Sleepwalking is acting out REM dreams.”
- “If someone remembers part of the event, it cannot be sleepwalking.”
- “Stress alone causes sleepwalking.”
- “Alcohol or a medication automatically proves sleepwalking.”
- “Hypnosis is proven 82.6% effective.”
- “Supplements prevent NREM parasomnias.”
- “Medication is always the first treatment.”
