# Migraine and Sleep — Evidence Dossier — 2026-09-04

## Research question

What can The Hippie Scientist defensibly say about migraine and sleep without flattening a bidirectional neurologic/sleep relationship into “poor sleep causes migraines” or implying that morning headache diagnoses sleep apnea?

## Editorial verdict

**Moderate confidence:** Migraine and sleep disturbance interact bidirectionally. Subjective sleep quality is consistently worse in migraine, especially chronic migraine, while objective sleep architecture findings are heterogeneous. Treating comorbid insomnia with CBT-I clearly improves insomnia and shows promising but less definitive migraine benefit. Migraine prophylaxis can improve subjective sleep quality, but treatment effects vary and questionnaire improvement is not equivalent to normalized sleep architecture. Morning headache is nonspecific; OSA/headache association data do not support treating headache alone as evidence of OSA.

## High-value evidence anchors

### 2026 migraine/sleep intervention systematic review

- PMID: 41874004
- 23 studies, 1,941 participants.
- Included migraine-targeted and sleep-targeted interventions.
- Migraine drugs including erenumab, amitriptyline, propranolol and onabotulinumtoxinA reduced migraine outcomes with variable sleep effects.
- Melatonin showed no significant impact in the included migraine/sleep intervention evidence.
- Digital CBT-I significantly reduced headache days and improved sleep parameters; evidence for standard CBT-I migraine outcomes was mixed.
- Heterogeneity, small samples and outcome inconsistency limit generalizability.
- Guardrail: the melatonin result is specific to this review's migraine/sleep intervention evidence; do not generalize it to every melatonin sleep or circadian question.

### 2026 objective sleep architecture systematic review

- PMID: 41578174
- 30 studies using PSG/actigraphy and related objective measures.
- Major finding: substantial heterogeneity.
- In adult migraine, 50% of studies assessing sleep efficiency reported a significant reduction.
- Majority of studies reporting number of awakenings found more awakenings.
- Microstructure/arousal findings varied; no clear universal hypo- or hyperarousal pattern.
- Some evidence that arousal profiles change across attack phases.
- Editorial value: supports objective disruption while rejecting a single universal migraine sleep-stage signature.

### 2021 subjective/objective migraine sleep meta-analysis

- PMID: 34551985
- 32 eligible studies.
- Adults with migraine had worse subjective PSQI scores than controls: g 0.75.
- Subjective effect larger in chronic than episodic migraine.
- Adults with migraine showed a small lower REM percentage in pooled PSG studies: g -0.22.
- Guardrail: newer objective review shows substantial heterogeneity; do not elevate REM difference into a biomarker.

### 2025 migraine prophylaxis and subjective sleep meta-analysis

- PMID: 38845376
- Seven studies, 989 participants; 85.3% female.
- Six of seven studies improved monthly migraine days.
- Five of six studies reporting sleep outcomes showed relevant subjective sleep improvement.
- Pooled sleep-quality effect: Cohen's d 1.09 (95% CI 0.57–1.62).
- Some treatments were not current international-guideline options.
- Outcomes were self-reported PSQI/ISI.
- Guardrail: sleep-quality improvement can follow migraine improvement without proving objective sleep normalization or treatment of an independent insomnia disorder.

### 2016 CBT-I migraine pilot RCT

- PMID: 26813845
- 31 adults with chronic migraine + insomnia.
- Brief CBT-I versus behavioral control.
- CBT-I significantly improved actigraphy total sleep time, sleep efficiency and self-reported insomnia.
- Headache frequency improved at follow-up, but primary headache comparison did not remain significant under Bonferroni correction.
- Editorial conclusion: strong insomnia signal, promising migraine signal.

### 2018 sequential Bayesian CBT-I analysis

- PMID: 29732536
- Combined completer data from two prior randomized trials.
- Estimated CBT-I headache-frequency reduction 6.2 days/month greater than control (95% credible interval -9.7 to -2.7).
- Completer analysis and small underlying studies matter.
- Do not present as definitive replacement for standard migraine prevention.

### 2024 prospective randomized multidisciplinary trial

- PMID: 38934675
- 96 patients with chronic migraine + chronic insomnia.
- CBT for pain/insomnia added to standard migraine treatment versus standard treatment/lifestyle/sleep-hygiene guidance.
- Reported better sustained insomnia and migraine-related outcomes across follow-up in CBT arm.
- Published with English abstract from a Russian-language report; use as supportive rather than sole anchor.

### 2024 OSA/headache systematic review and meta-analysis

- PMID: 38056382
- 23 studies, 15,402 patients with OSA.
- Pooled prevalence:
  - all headaches 33%;
  - morning headaches 33%;
  - sleep-apnea headache 25%;
  - migraine 16%.
- Overall headache RR in OSA versus non-OSA: 1.43 (95% CI 0.92–2.25), not statistically significant.
- Guardrail: headache is common in OSA but headache alone is not evidence that OSA is present or causally responsible.

### 2018 clinical migraine/sleep review

- PMID: 30095163
- Frames sleep and migraine as clinically intertwined.
- Insomnia is especially common among chronic-migraine treatment populations.
- Sleep evaluation/treatment should complement rather than delay or replace standard headache care.
- Useful decision-layer framing source.

## Claim guardrails

1. Do not write “poor sleep causes migraine” as a universal one-way claim.
2. Distinguish trigger reports, longitudinal prediction, attack consequences and comorbid insomnia.
3. Keep subjective sleep improvement separate from PSG/actigraphy normalization.
4. Preserve the 2026 objective-review heterogeneity; do not claim a universal REM/deep-sleep signature.
5. Treat CBT-I as established for insomnia and promising but less definitive for migraine reduction.
6. Do not use the small CBT-I trials to replace standard migraine prevention.
7. Do not overgeneralize the 2026 melatonin null signal to circadian disorders/general insomnia.
8. Do not imply that a sedating migraine preventive is automatically the best choice for insomnia.
9. Do not diagnose OSA from morning headache or migraine alone.
10. Pair OSA/headache prevalence with the nonsignificant pooled headache-risk estimate.
11. Avoid causal interpretations of oversleeping attacks because prodrome/recovery can reverse the direction.
12. Keep sleep treatment complementary to neurologic migraine care.
