# Teen and Adolescent Sleep — Evidence Dossier — 2026-09-04

## Research question

What can The Hippie Scientist defensibly say about adolescent sleep, puberty-related circadian delay, early school schedules, insomnia, CBT-I, DSWPD, melatonin and light without collapsing those into one generic “teenagers stay up late” story?

## Editorial verdict

**Moderate confidence:** Adolescence shifts preferred sleep timing later on average, and early required wake times can create chronic sleep restriction and social jet lag. Later school start times generally increase sleep opportunity. A late chronotype is not automatically DSWPD, and insufficient sleep is not automatically insomnia. When adolescent insomnia is present, recent randomized-trial meta-analyses support CBT-I across multiple delivery formats. Melatonin and bright light are best framed as phase-timing tools for selected circadian problems rather than universal bedtime sedatives.

## High-value evidence anchors

### 2024 school start time / chronotype meta-analysis

- PMID: 39098151
- Systematic review and meta-analysis.
- Later school start times were generally associated with longer sleep duration and lower social jet lag.
- Effect magnitude varied across studies.
- The effect of school start time on underlying chronotype itself was less settled.
- Guardrail: later start time can improve opportunity without curing insomnia or eliminating all behavior/environment contributors.

### 2022 school start time and youth outcomes meta-analysis

- PMID: 35593065
- 28 studies; 1,774,509 participants.
- Later school start times associated with longer sleep duration, better overall developmental outcomes, and less negative mood.
- Observational/quasi-experimental evidence contributes substantially; do not convert pooled associations into a guaranteed individual benefit.

### 2025 adolescent CBT-I RCT meta-analysis

- PMID: 39680961
- Five RCTs in qualitative synthesis; four RCTs / 527 participants quantitatively pooled.
- CBT-I improved subjective sleep quality: Hedges' g 0.40.
- CBT-I improved insomnia symptoms: Hedges' g 1.04.
- Delivery modality varied.
- Authors call for additional high-quality trials.

### 2024 adolescent CBT-I meta-analysis

- PMID: 39628800
- Eight RCTs, 599 participants.
- Post-treatment pooled outcomes:
  - insomnia severity: SMD -1.06 (95% CI -1.65 to -0.47);
  - sleep-onset latency: SMD -0.99 (95% CI -1.65 to -0.32);
  - total sleep time: SMD +0.50 (95% CI 0.10 to 0.90);
  - sleep efficiency: SMD +0.57 (95% CI 0.26 to 0.87).
- Insomnia improvement remained significant at follow-up: SMD -0.79 (95% CI -1.42 to -0.17).
- Guardrail: effect sizes differ by outcome and synthesis; do not present them as universal expected changes for one patient.

### 2026 CBT-I delivery-format network meta-analysis

- PMID: 42439579
- 22 RCTs across seven CBT-I modalities.
- Web-based CBT-I ranked highly across several outcomes.
- Compared with usual care in the network, reported effects included:
  - TST +33 min;
  - SOL -23 min;
  - sleep efficiency +7 percentage points;
  - insomnia severity -5 points.
- No significant WASO difference.
- Guardrail: network rankings combine direct and indirect evidence and depend on network composition.

### 2025 school-based CBT-I review/meta-analysis

- PMID: 40668370
- Focused on CBT-I delivered in school settings to adolescents with insomnia symptoms/disorder.
- Supports school delivery as an access pathway.
- Do not equate a generic sleep-hygiene lesson with full CBT-I.

### 2025 puberty melatonin meta-analysis

- PMID: 39823958
- Systematic review/meta-analysis of endogenous melatonin secretion across puberty.
- Supports developmental change in melatonin physiology.
- Does **not** establish a “melatonin deficiency” diagnosis in normal adolescents or prove supplement efficacy.

### 2023 updated DSWPD adolescent review

- PMID: 38054481
- DSWPD frequently affects adolescents/young people and overlaps with neurodevelopmental and psychiatric conditions.
- Diagnosis depends on history, sleep timing and impairment rather than late bedtime alone.
- Reviews multimodal treatment including light, behavioral timing and melatonin.
- Avoid exact universal protocols from narrative-review level evidence.

### 2025 DSWPD and depression meta-analysis

- PMID: 40129277
- Systematic review/meta-analysis in young individuals.
- Supports an association between DSWPD and depression.
- Guardrail: association is not proof of one-way causality; mood and circadian disruption can plausibly interact bidirectionally and share risk factors.

### AASM circadian guideline

- PMID: 26414986
- 2015 guideline remains a key evidence-framework source for circadian disorders.
- Supports strategically timed melatonin in selected circadian conditions and light therapy with behavioral interventions for children/adolescents with DSWPD.
- Evidence quality/population specificity matter.
- Do not convert this into one universal dose or clock time.

## Claim guardrails

1. Do not call adolescent late timing laziness.
2. Do not call every night owl DSWPD.
3. Do not call every tired teen an insomniac; insufficient sleep opportunity is a distinct problem.
4. Do not imply later school starts cure insomnia.
5. Do not equate school sleep education with full CBT-I.
6. Preserve multiple CBT-I meta-analyses rather than cherry-picking the largest effect size.
7. Treat 2026 network rankings as comparative evidence, not a guarantee that web CBT-I is universally superior.
8. Do not infer supplement deficiency from puberty-related endogenous melatonin changes.
9. Do not publish a universal adolescent melatonin dose/timing rule.
10. Preserve phase dependence for both light and melatonin.
11. Do not claim DSWPD causes depression from association data.
12. Keep screen displacement/arousal/social engagement distinct from wavelength-only claims.
