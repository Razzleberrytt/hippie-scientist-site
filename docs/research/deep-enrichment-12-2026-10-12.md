## DE12-01 — Butterbur: migraine benefit does not cancel pyrrolizidine-alkaloid risk

**Question:** Is the clinically effective experimental butterbur root extract interchangeable with any retail butterbur product, including unverified “PA-free” products?

**Sources and study classes:** Lipton et al., Neurology 2004, RCT, [PMID 15623680](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15623680/), DOI `10.1212/01.wnl.0000147290.68260.11`; [NIH LiverTox butterbur monograph](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK547997/), adverse-event case assessment; [NCCIH butterbur safety summary](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/butterbur), evidence/guideline context. Case-series attribution is not an efficacy RCT.

**Observed trial evidence:** Three-arm, 245 adults with migraine, four months. In the trial **per-protocol** analysis, 75-mg-twice-daily *specific extract* reduced migraine attacks by 48% from baseline vs 26% placebo (**p=.0012**); the tested lower 50-mg-twice-daily arm's 36% reduction **was not significant** versus placebo (**p=.127**). Fifty-percent responder rates were 68% for the higher-exposure arm vs 49% placebo; do not confuse within-arm percentage reductions with a 48-percentage-point placebo-adjusted effect.

**Safety boundary:** Butterbur plant parts can contain hepatotoxic pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs); preparation identity and removal/verification matter. LiverTox reviews rare clinically significant hepatitis associated with commercial preparations, while causality/contamination attribution is disputed. NCCIH notes the AAN withdrew its favorable recommendation in 2015 because of serious safety concerns. A “PA-free” label is not an independent laboratory certificate and does not prove every current lot is safe.

**Null/limits:** Lower-exposure arm did not outperform placebo; 4-month efficacy does not establish years-long safety, prevention of stroke, or product equivalence. Do **not** convert studied amounts into general dosing instructions.

**Disposition:** **P0** product-identity and liver safety editorial hold; no affiliate recommendation or efficacy promotion without independent review.

## DE12-02 — Feverfew: a positive replication attempt and an earlier primary null

**Question:** Can a 2005 trial establish broad migraine prophylaxis efficacy without mentioning the 2002 multi-dose failure?

**Primary sources:** Diener et al., Cephalalgia 2005, [PMID 16232154](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16232154/), DOI `10.1111/j.1468-2982.2005.00950.x`; Pfaffenrath et al., Cephalalgia 2002, [PMID 12230594](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12230594/), DOI `10.1046/j.1468-2982.2002.00396.x`. Both human randomized placebo-controlled trials of **CO2-extract MIG-99**; do not generalize to feverfew leaf teas or heterogeneous supplement powder.

**2005 trial:** 218 participants randomized; **170 in efficacy ITT set** (89 active, 81 placebo) after protocol-eligibility exclusions. Over defined months, migraine attacks fell ~1.9 per month active vs ~1.3 placebo (**p=.0456** on primary model). Some secondary outcomes were significant; adverse events possibly related to treatment 8.4% vs 10.2% placebo (no significant difference).

**2002 contrast:** Adaptive 147-person dose-ranging study over 12 weeks **failed to show significant efficacy overall and failed its dose-response hypothesis**; an approximately 49-person baseline-severity subgroup showed a positive signal not sufficient to reverse the overall null.

**Boundary:** Apparent benefits are extract-specific, study-design dependent, and modest; 2005 exclusions and borderline primary p-value affect certainty. Do not imply every available feverfew product, acute migraine treatment, or pregnancy safety is proved.

**Disposition:** P1 contradiction-observatory candidate; surface both trials and their inclusion populations before assigning evidence tier.

## DE12-03 — American ginseng for cancer-related fatigue: primary 4-week null

**Question:** Does the 8-week result justify saying the primary endpoint succeeded or substituting Asian ginseng?

**Source:** Barton et al., J Natl Cancer Inst 2013, [PMID 23853057](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23853057/), DOI `10.1093/jnci/djt181`, phase-III randomized double-blind placebo trial, **364 participants** with cancer-related fatigue, eight-week **American ginseng (*Panax quinquefolius*)** treatment.

**Primary endpoint:** Week-four change in general Multidimensional Fatigue Symptom Inventory score **14.4** vs **8.2** placebo, **p=.07 (not statistically significant)**. **Week eight (secondary time point)** change **20.0** vs **10.3**, **p=.003**; the published subgroup suggests larger effect during active cancer treatment, but avoid treating subgroup as independent replication. Recorded adverse events did not significantly differ during this short trial.

**Evidence boundaries:** American (*P. quinquefolius*) is not interchangeable with Asian (*P. ginseng*) or retail mixed-ginsenoside products. Week-eight effect is not a successful week-four primary outcome, treatment of cancer, proven survival impact, long-term safety, or a recommendation for a patient receiving chemotherapy.

**Disposition:** P1 outcome-hierarchy and species firewall. Cancer-care interactions require independent review before live safety copy.

## DE12-04 — Passionflower: preoperative anxiety versus chronic anxiety disorder

**Question:** Is an acute pre-anesthesia signal sufficient for claims about chronic anxiety or sleep therapy?

**Source:** Movafegh et al., Anesth Analg 2008, [PMID 18499602](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18499602/), DOI `10.1213/ane.0b013e318172c3f9`, randomized double-blind placebo-controlled **60-person** ambulatory surgery premedication trial.

**Results:** Anxiety numerical rating scores significantly lower during 90-minute preoperative observation in trial Passiflora group vs placebo (**p<.001**); measured sedation, postoperative psychomotor recovery and discharge times did **not** significantly differ.

**Null/boundaries:** A nonsignificant sedation finding in 60 people and one preoperative exposure does **not** prove lack of sedation or drug interactions in larger populations, nor equivalence to established therapies. The exact studied product was **Passipy**; passionflower tea/extract concentrations, context of anesthesia, GAD, insomnia and chronic dosing are separate evidence domains.

**Disposition:** P1 formulation, outcome and setting-specific research; do not promote as a generic anxiolytic safety guarantee. **Existing repository coverage includes PMID 18499602**, so plan source reuse, not novelty.

## DE12-05 — Alpha-lipoic acid: short-term symptom scores vs four-year progression composite

**Question:** Does symptom relief in five weeks demonstrate long-term nerve-disease modification?

**Sources:** Ziegler et al., Diabetes Care 2006 SYDNEY 2 RCT [PMID 17065669](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17065669/), DOI `10.2337/dc06-1216`; Ziegler et al., Diabetes Care 2011 NATHAN 1 RCT [PMID 21775755](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21775755/), DOI `10.2337/dc11-0503`.

**Short-term:** SYDNEY 2 enrolled **181** people with symptomatic diabetic distal symmetric polyneuropathy. At five weeks, symptom-score reductions in three active oral groups ranged about **48–52%** vs **32%** placebo (each active arm **p<.05** versus placebo). GI/vertigo adverse effects rose with higher studied exposures.

**Long-term contrast:** NATHAN 1 randomized **460** people for four years. **Prespecified primary composite endpoint did not differ significantly**, p=.105. Some neuropathy impairment secondary scales favored treatment; serious adverse events were **38.1% active vs 28.0% placebo**, an observed imbalance that cannot independently establish treatment causation.

**Null/boundaries:** Symptoms, secondary impairment tests, composite neurophysiology, nerve regeneration, and foot-ulcer prevention are *different* outcomes. Long-term primary null cannot be converted into “reverses neuropathy.” Exposure amounts are trial descriptors, not consumer dosing guidance.

**Disposition:** P1 precision correction; separate route (oral vs intravenous) and condition-specific population.

## DE12-06 — Aged garlic extract and blood pressure: product- and baseline-dependent

**Question:** Does a positive subgroup among treated uncontrolled hypertensive patients translate to lowering blood pressure in everyone?

**Source:** Ried et al., Maturitas 2010, [PMID 20594781](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20594781/), DOI `10.1016/j.maturitas.2010.06.001`, placebo-controlled randomized **50-person** study across 12 weeks, one *aged garlic extract* product as **adjunct to existing treatment**.

**Results:** In the baseline subgroup with systolic BP **at least 140 mm Hg**, systolic BP was **10.2 ± 4.3 mm Hg lower than controls** (p=.03) averaged across treatment period. In participants with lower baseline systolic BP, between-group changes were **not statistically significant**.

**Limits:** The 50-person study's baseline-BP subgroup result does not establish a generic garlic-food or garlic-powder effect; endpoint was BP, **not** strokes/heart attacks. Does not establish drug equivalence, safety of replacing antihypertensives, or effect in normotensive adults.

**Disposition:** P1 formulation and baseline-stratification enrichment; do not build product effectiveness rankings from a single studied preparation.

## DE12-07 — Green tea extract: significant ALT risk in randomized safety data

**Question:** Does a concentration-specific liver risk apply to ordinary brewed tea, and is the clinical risk measured?

**Sources:** Dostal et al., Food Chem Toxicol 2015, [PMID 26051348](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26051348/), DOI `10.1016/j.fct.2015.05.019`, randomized double-blind **1,075 postmenopausal women**, one year, concentrated green tea extract versus placebo. Yu et al., Cancer Prev Res 2017, [PMID 28765194](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28765194/), DOI `10.1158/1940-6207.CAPR-17-0160`, additional ALT analysis from **the same Minnesota Green Tea Trial**, **not** a second independent RCT.

**Primary safety observation:** ALT elevation occurred in **36 participants (6.7%) extract vs 4 (0.7%) placebo**, p<.001. Overall any-adverse-event reports were 75.6% vs 72.8% (not significantly different); serious adverse events 2.2% vs 1.5% (not significant), while GI symptoms differed by type. ALT-associated serious adverse events were described in 1.3% of the extract arm.

**Interpretation:** Liver enzyme elevation **is a biomarker/safety signal, not a measured liver-failure rate**. This exposure was a standardized high-catechin **extract** containing EGCG, not ordinary brewed green tea. Neither numeric rate is a current-market incidence estimate across products, ages, sexes or meal conditions.

**Null/boundaries:** Overall AE and serious AE comparisons null; do not portray an ALT increase as clinical liver injury in every exposed person, and do not infer safety of all extracts if low exposure differs.

**Disposition:** **P0** risk-tier/formulation quality review; prevent green-tea-beverage vs high-EGCG-extract confusion. No consumption/dose recommendation.

## DE12-08 — Beetroot dietary nitrate and hypertension: opposite controlled-trial findings

**Question:** Does a BP effect persist across treated and untreated hypertension and across follow-up duration?

**Sources:** Kapil et al., Hypertension 2015, randomized 68-person trial, [PMID 25421976](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25421976/), DOI `10.1161/HYPERTENSIONAHA.114.04675`; Bondonno et al., Am J Clin Nutr 2015, 27-person double-blind crossover, [PMID 26135348](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26135348/), DOI `10.3945/ajcn.114.101188`.

**Positive RCT:** Kapil randomized **68 hypertensive participants**, stratified **34 drug-naive and 34 treated**, to a nitrate-rich juice preparation versus nitrate-depleted juice over four weeks. Placebo-adjusted clinic BP lower by mean **7.7/2.4 mm Hg**; 24-hour ambulatory BP **7.7/5.2 mm Hg lower**, with reported favorable endothelial surrogate tests.

**Contrary RCT:** Bondonno enrolled **27 treated-hypertensive participants** in one-week crossover: nitrate-rich juice markedly raised nitrate/nitrite in blood/saliva/urine, but **neither home nor 24-hour ambulatory BP differed** versus nitrate-depleted juice.

**Evidence boundaries:** Both are RCTs with different treatment populations and durations. Biomarker evidence of nitrate delivery is **not** synonymous with BP efficacy. Do not infer heart attack/stroke prevention, replacement of prescriptions, universal effect, or all beetroot powders contain the tested nitrate content.

**Disposition:** P1 contradiction-observatory and formulation/endpoint mapping.

## DE12-09 — L-methylfolate added to SSRIs: first randomized study null

**Question:** Was the same benefit established across both randomized sequential-comparison studies?

**Source:** Papakostas et al., Am J Psychiatry 2012, [PMID 23212058](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23212058/), DOI `10.1176/appi.ajp.2012.11071114`, report of **two separate** randomized double-blind sequential-parallel comparison trials in people with major depressive disorder not responding adequately to SSRIs.

**Results:** **First trial, n=148**, lower-dose initial augmentation strategy: **no significant treatment-group outcome differences**. **Second trial, n=75**, used 15-mg/day L-methylfolate throughout the periods and reported significant improvements in **two primary response/symptom outcomes** versus SSRI+placebo; reported number needed to treat approximately **six** for response. Trial dosing is historical trial exposure, **not** advice.

**Interpretation:** The first null must accompany the second positive. This was an **adjunct** in a selected psychiatric population, not a replacement for antidepressants, correction of demonstrated folate deficiency, treatment of every depression subtype, or evidence for standard multivitamins. The higher-strength intervention is not equivalent to generic folic acid or food folate. Short follow-up, trial sponsorship/conflict assessment and rare harms require independent review.

**Disposition:** P1 efficacy/formulation precision; no changes to user medication or medical advice.

## DE12-10 — Oral Silexan lavender oil and GAD: branded formulation firewall

**Question:** Does an oral lavender-oil trial justify claims for aromatherapy or equivalence to paroxetine?

**Source:** Kasper et al., Int J Neuropsychopharmacol 2014, [PMID 24456909](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24456909/), DOI `10.1017/S1461145714000017`, **539 adult** GAD randomized double-blind double-dummy trial, ten weeks, **oral Silexan** two exposure levels, paroxetine active control, and placebo.

**Results:** Hamilton Anxiety score reductions were **14.1** and **12.8** for active Silexan levels, **11.3** for paroxetine, and **9.5** for placebo. Two Silexan vs placebo comparisons p<.01 in full-analysis set; paroxetine versus placebo **p=.10** in full set, but observed-cases analysis did indicate significance. This trial did **not** demonstrate Silexan is clinically superior to paroxetine or interchangeable with all prescribed therapies.

**Evidence boundaries:** Trial interventions were **specific standardized oral lavender-oil capsules**, not diffuser use, lavender perfume, essential-oil ingestion generally, or topical preparations. Sponsor affiliations include product manufacturer; effectiveness/harms beyond ten weeks, pediatric use, pregnancy, and long-term outcomes are not established by this trial.

**Disposition:** P1 branded/route outcome correction; PMID **24456909 already occurs in repository citation exports/guide material**, so reconcile and reuse.
