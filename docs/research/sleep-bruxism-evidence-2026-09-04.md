# Sleep Bruxism / Teeth Grinding — Evidence Dossier

Date: 2026-09-04
Status: publication-ready research support for `content/articles/sleep-bruxism-teeth-grinding.md`

## Research question

What can be said confidently about sleep bruxism, its assessment, relationship to sleep quality and obstructive sleep apnea, oral consequences, occlusal splints, botulinum toxin and substance associations without treating all tooth grinding as a sleep disorder or turning limited treatment evidence into universal advice?

## Editorial verdict

Sleep bruxism is **masticatory-muscle activity during sleep**, not automatically a disease state in an otherwise healthy person.

The clinically relevant questions are separate:
- Is current sleep bruxism actually present, and how was it assessed?
- Is it causing tooth damage, pain or functional problems?
- Does another sleep disorder such as OSA need evaluation on its own merits?
- Is an intervention protecting against consequences, reducing pain, changing muscle activity, or actually reducing bruxism events?

Evidence grade: **Moderate** overall.

Why not High:
- modern definitions and instrumental-assessment principles are comparatively strong;
- treatment studies remain heterogeneous;
- sleep-bruxism/OSA evidence is conflicting and often low quality;
- self-report, dental signs, EMG and PSG are not interchangeable measures.

## Anchor evidence

### 1. International consensus on bruxism assessment

PMID: 29926505

Key points:
- sleep bruxism and awake bruxism are distinct circadian manifestations;
- sleep bruxism is rhythmic or non-rhythmic masticatory-muscle activity during sleep;
- awake bruxism involves repetitive/sustained tooth contact or jaw bracing/thrusting while awake;
- in otherwise healthy individuals, bruxism is better framed as a behavior that may be harmless, a risk factor or occasionally protective rather than automatically a disorder;
- self-report and instrumental assessment answer different questions;
- rigid universal cutoffs are discouraged in otherwise healthy people.

Editorial rule:
> Never equate “I grind my teeth” with a formal sleep-disorder diagnosis.

### 2. Updated bruxism-definition work

2024 explanatory note: PMID 37994212.
2025 consensus update: cited in the public article via PMC.

Use:
- preserve the behavior/continuum model;
- preserve sleep-vs-awake bruxism separation;
- avoid presenting historic “possible/probable/definite” labels as if all current frameworks use them identically.

### 3. Instrumental assessment systematic review/meta-analysis

PMID: 38295573

Use:
- supports the importance of instrumental approaches when the research question is actual sleep-time muscle activity;
- portable EMG and PSG methods are not equivalent to self-report or old tooth wear;
- methodology and thresholds vary across studies.

Editorial guardrail:
- tooth wear is cumulative evidence of mechanical history, not a precise nightly activity meter.

### 4. Adult sleep quality meta-analysis

PMID: 40043438

Population/evidence:
- 32 studies;
- 4,706 adults;
- sleep disorders, major neurologic/psychiatric disorders and psychotropic medication exposures were excluded by the review eligibility criteria.

Main findings:
- no significant pooled difference between sleep-bruxism and control groups in PSG sleep efficiency, sleep latency, awakenings, WASO or sleep duration;
- subjective PSQI score was worse in sleep bruxism: **MD 1.98, 95% CI 0.96–3.00**;
- awake bruxism also showed worse PSQI: **MD 1.99, 95% CI 0.42–3.57**;
- certainty of evidence rated very low.

Editorial use:
> Preserve the subjective-vs-objective split. Do not claim sleep bruxism universally fragments sleep architecture.

### 5. 2024 OSA association meta-analysis

PMID: 39182463

Evidence:
- 14 studies;
- SB odds in OSA versus control: **OR 1.23, 95% CI 0.47–3.20** — not significant;
- subgroup comparisons by mild/moderate/severe OSA were also not significant;
- major included studies were generally low quality.

Editorial use:
- strongest direct guardrail against “bruxism means sleep apnea”;
- null meta-analysis does not prove the two can never coexist.

### 6. 2026 OSA/bruxism umbrella review

PMID: 42447651

Evidence:
- eight systematic reviews included;
- most reported frequent coexistence and proposed common mechanisms such as micro-arousals/autonomic activation;
- only one included review had a meta-analysis;
- methodological quality mostly low or critically low;
- causal direction unresolved.

Important date note:
- indexed article date July 9, 2026; journal issue publication listed for November 2026.

Editorial use:
> Current literature can support possible coexistence/association, not a universal causal chain.

### 7. Occlusal appliances and sleep — 2026 systematic review/meta-analysis

PMID: 39707941

Evidence:
- two randomized plus eight non-randomized studies;
- meta-analyses found significant changes in slow-wave sleep and sleep-quality measures across studied appliance conditions.

Limits:
- small evidence base;
- heterogeneous designs and appliance types;
- an effect on a sleep measure is not evidence that the underlying bruxism behavior is permanently eliminated.

Editorial use:
- mouthguard/splint language should distinguish mechanical protection from behavioral cure.

### 8. Occlusal appliances and muscle function — 2024 meta-analysis

PMID: 38421263

Use:
- supports that appliances can alter masticatory-muscle outcomes;
- findings depend on design, outcome and measurement approach.

Do not collapse:
- tooth protection;
- pain relief;
- muscle-activity reduction;
- bruxism-event reduction.

### 9. Splint vs botulinum toxin randomized trial

PMID: 39510242

Population:
- 60 adults randomized;
- probable sleep bruxism plus jaw-muscle pain;
- 59 analyzed.

Findings:
- no significant between-treatment difference in primary GCPS pain outcome (p=.627);
- both groups improved at 3 and 6 months;
- splint had advantages on several jaw-function parameters;
- **23/29 (79.3%)** BTX-A participants reported mild chewing discomfort.

Editorial use:
> This trial is about jaw-muscle pain in probable sleep bruxism, not proof that either therapy cures the genesis of sleep bruxism.

### 10. 2026 splint-vs-BTX systematic review/meta-analysis

PMID: 42274300

Evidence:
- 36 studies in qualitative synthesis;
- both interventions improved pain-related clinical sequelae;
- no significant difference in intermediate follow-up pain reduction;
- some faster short-term relief with BTX-A;
- temporary reductions in jaw-muscle activity and bite force reported with BTX-A;
- occlusal splints primarily provide mechanical protection while maintaining function;
- certainty moderate for pain, lower for other outcomes.

Editorial use:
- conservative protection is a different endpoint from neuromuscular suppression.

### 11. Placebo-controlled botulinum toxin physiology trial

PMID: 32182879

Evidence:
- 30 randomized, 23 completed;
- BoNT-A reduced EMG burst amplitude;
- it did **not** significantly reduce the genesis/number of sleep-bruxism episodes.

Editorial use:
> Reducing contraction intensity is not the same as eliminating the central sleep-bruxism behavior.

### 12. Substance association systematic review

PMID: 27522154

Signals included positive associations with alcohol, smoking and very heavy caffeine intake in a small literature base.

Editorial rule:
- association ≠ direct causation;
- do not claim one coffee, drink or cigarette causes a bruxism episode;
- caffeine's direct sleep-timing evidence belongs to the separate caffeine sleep article.

### 13. Critical evaluation of treatment systematic reviews

PMID: 36441158

Evidence:
- six systematic reviews evaluated;
- all rated critically low by AMSTAR 2;
- pharmacologic, oral-appliance and biofeedback findings were diverse/confusing;
- no clear consensus on a single most effective sleep-bruxism therapy.

Editorial use:
- prevents treatment-list inflation;
- newer positive studies should not erase the weak historical evidence base.

## Core distinctions to preserve

### Sleep bruxism ≠ awake bruxism

Sleep bruxism occurs during sleep.
Awake bruxism occurs during wakefulness.

Stress/anxiety associations are often stronger and conceptually different for awake bruxism. Do not inherit awake-bruxism psychology into sleep bruxism without direct evidence.

### Sleep bruxism ≠ tooth wear

Tooth wear may reflect:
- historical grinding;
- erosion;
- age;
- occlusal factors;
- other mechanical exposures.

It does not quantify last night's RMMA.

### Sleep bruxism ≠ OSA

Allowed:
- coexistence is possible;
- shared arousal/autonomic mechanisms are proposed;
- OSA symptoms should be evaluated independently.

Not allowed:
- grinding proves OSA;
- treating OSA will necessarily cure all bruxism;
- a mouthguard is an OSA treatment.

### Splint protection ≠ bruxism cure

A splint can protect teeth even if motor activity continues.
This is a successful clinical outcome without needing to claim the underlying behavior disappeared.

### Pain improvement ≠ event elimination

Jaw pain, bite force, EMG amplitude, RMMA event count, tooth damage and sleep quality are distinct outcomes.

Never rewrite a positive result on one into efficacy on all of them.

## Magnesium/supplement rule

No direct evidence reviewed here establishes magnesium deficiency as a common cause of sleep bruxism or magnesium supplementation as a standard treatment.

Do not inherit generic relaxation or sleep-supplement evidence into bruxism.

Avoid:
- “magnesium stops grinding”;
- “L-theanine treats bruxism”;
- “melatonin fixes teeth grinding”;
- generic sedative stacks as a bruxism treatment pathway.

## Mouthguard safety/OSA rule

A standard occlusal splint is not a mandibular advancement device for OSA.

Do not imply:
- dental protection treats airway obstruction;
- any over-the-counter guard is appropriate for every bite/TMD situation;
- a guard replaces evaluation for loud snoring, witnessed apneas, gasping or marked daytime sleepiness.

## Botulinum toxin rule

Public content may discuss evidence and adverse effects.

Do not provide:
- injection sites;
- units/doses;
- injection technique;
- self-selection protocols.

Core evidence boundary:
> BTX-A can reduce pain or contraction intensity in selected clinical populations without proving that the central genesis of sleep bruxism has been eliminated.

## Safety / referral logic

Formal dental or sleep evaluation becomes more relevant with:
- progressive tooth fracture/wear;
- persistent jaw pain or limited opening;
- damaged restorations;
- loud snoring, witnessed apneas or gasping;
- significant daytime sleepiness;
- suspected medication/substance contribution;
- new severe facial pain or neurologic symptoms.
