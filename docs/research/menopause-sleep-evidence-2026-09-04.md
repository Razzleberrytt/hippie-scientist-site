# Menopause and Sleep — Evidence Dossier — 2026-09-04

## Research question

How should the sleep cluster explain sleep disruption during perimenopause and menopause without collapsing vasomotor symptoms, chronic insomnia, sleep apnea and other sleep disorders into one hormonal story?

## Verdict

Sleep disturbance is common during the menopausal transition, but it is mechanistically heterogeneous. Vasomotor symptoms can fragment sleep, chronic insomnia can become a separate self-sustaining disorder, and obstructive sleep apnea becomes increasingly relevant after menopause.

CBT-I has strong menopause-specific randomized evidence for chronic insomnia. Menopausal hormone therapy produces smaller average improvements in self-reported sleep and appears more relevant when vasomotor symptoms are present; objective PSG changes are less consistent. Exercise should not be overclaimed as a direct insomnia treatment based on current RCT meta-analysis.

## High-value evidence anchors

### 2024 menopause sleep review

- PMID: 38916279
- Sleep disturbance is among the most common and burdensome symptoms of the menopause transition.
- No therapy is specifically approved solely for menopause-associated sleep disturbance.
- Strong anchor for the multi-driver framework.

### 2025 clinical review

- PMID: 39820156
- Summarizes biopsychosocial contributors and pharmacologic/nonpharmacologic options.
- Reinforces separating menopausal status, hot flashes and reproductive-hormone changes rather than treating them as interchangeable explanations.

### 2025 CBT-I meta-analysis

- PMID: 41531400
- 11 RCTs, n=973.
- Sleep quality: SMD -1.01 (95% CI -1.27 to -0.75).
- Insomnia Severity Index: MD -4.49 points (95% CI -6.12 to -2.87).
- Delivery included face-to-face, telephone and internet CBT-I.
- Strongest direct menopause-specific insomnia anchor.

### 2026 chronic-insomnia treatment meta-analysis

- PMID: 42247852
- 24 studies qualitative; 17 meta-analyzed.
- Nonpharmacological treatments significantly improved PSQI and ISI.
- Pharmacological treatments improved PSQI but not all endpoints.
- Neither objective nor subjective total sleep time changed reliably.
- Supports endpoint-specific writing and CBT-I-first framing for chronic insomnia.

### 2022 menopausal hormone therapy sleep meta-analysis

- PMID: 35102100
- 15 randomized studies.
- Small overall improvement in self-reported sleep: SMD -0.13.
- No clear overall polysomnography benefit.
- Regimen and route influenced effects.
- Guardrail: do not translate self-reported benefit into universal objective sleep improvement.

### Earlier MHT + vasomotor symptom meta-analysis

- PMID: 27515805
- Sleep quality improved more clearly among women with vasomotor symptoms at baseline: SMD -0.54.
- No comparable benefit in women without vasomotor symptoms.
- Supports the “treating the driver may improve sleep” model.

### 2025 exercise / vasomotor symptoms meta-analysis

- PMID: 40288155
- 9 studies, n=1,579.
- No significant overall improvement in sleep quality.
- Insomnia improvement did not reach statistical significance.
- Small improvement in broader sleep-problem measures.
- Important null finding to preserve.

### 2025 OSA in women review

- PMID: 40885537
- OSA risk increases with reproductive aging/postmenopause.
- Women may present with insomnia, fatigue and mood disturbance rather than only classic loud snoring/witnessed apnea.
- Strong safety/diagnostic-bottleneck anchor.

## Claim guardrails

1. Do not say menopause itself is the sole cause of every sleep complaint in midlife women.
2. Do not imply hot flashes explain all insomnia.
3. Do not imply hormone therapy is a general insomnia treatment or recommend it without individualized medical context.
4. Preserve the distinction between subjective sleep improvement and PSG changes.
5. Do not overclaim exercise as a proven menopause-insomnia intervention; the 2025 RCT meta-analysis is largely null for sleep quality/insomnia.
6. Treat postmenopausal OSA as an important competing/coexisting explanation, including atypical female presentations.
7. Treat restless legs as a separate pathway rather than assuming “restlessness” means magnesium deficiency.
8. Keep CBT-I central for persistent insomnia because the menopause-specific RCT evidence is comparatively strong.
