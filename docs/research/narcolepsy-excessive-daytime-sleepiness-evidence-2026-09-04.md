# Narcolepsy / Excessive Daytime Sleepiness — Evidence Dossier

Date: 2026-09-04
Status: publication-ready support for `content/articles/narcolepsy-excessive-daytime-sleepiness.md`

## Research question

How should the site distinguish narcolepsy from ordinary fatigue, sleep deprivation, shift work, OSA and other hypersomnolence disorders while accurately explaining cataplexy, REM-intrusion symptoms, PSG/MSLT testing, orexin deficiency and current treatment guidance?

## Editorial verdict

Narcolepsy is a chronic neurologic disorder of sleep-wake regulation.

The core public-health distinction is:
- **pathologic excessive daytime sleepiness** is not the same as generic fatigue;
- sleep paralysis and hypnagogic/hypnopompic hallucinations are clues, not diagnoses;
- cataplexy has much higher specificity for narcolepsy type 1;
- diagnosis requires clinical context plus objective testing;
- testing is only interpretable after addressing insufficient sleep, shift work/circadian confounders and medications.

Evidence grade: **Moderate** overall.

Why not High:
- diagnostic criteria are strong, but MSLT repeatability and type 2 narcolepsy biomarker gaps remain important;
- treatment recommendations are strong for several medications but the article spans heterogeneous symptoms and diagnostic questions.

## Anchor evidence

### 1. 2021 AASM treatment guideline

PMID: 34743789

Strong adult narcolepsy recommendations:
- modafinil;
- pitolisant;
- sodium oxybate;
- solriamfetol.

Conditional adult recommendations:
- armodafinil;
- dextroamphetamine;
- methylphenidate.

Editorial rules:
- these recommendations apply to diagnosed narcolepsy;
- do not convert to self-treatment advice for ordinary fatigue/sleep deprivation;
- no public dosing/escalation schedules;
- treatment choice is symptom-, risk- and patient-specific.

### 2. ICSD-3 / DSM-5 diagnostic criteria review

PMID: 27359185

Key points:
- excessive daytime sleepiness is required;
- objective criteria include PSG/MSLT REM findings and/or CSF hypocretin deficiency;
- ICSD-3 divides narcolepsy into type 1 and type 2;
- type 1 requires cataplexy with required objective findings or documented CSF hypocretin deficiency;
- type 2 lacks cataplexy and has normal/undocumented CSF hypocretin;
- MSLT threshold commonly uses mean sleep latency ≤8 minutes and ≥2 SOREMPs, with qualifying overnight PSG SOREMP potentially counting.

Editorial use:
- preserve NT1 vs NT2 distinction;
- do not diagnose from sleep paralysis/hallucinations.

### 3. Consensus statement on narcolepsy without cataplexy

PMID: 24882898

Key points:
- narcolepsy without cataplexy/type 2 is diagnostically difficult;
- detailed history helps rule out other causes of chronic sleepiness;
- MSLT remains central;
- prior sleep deprivation, shift work and circadian disorders should be excluded using actigraphy or sleep logs when appropriate;
- short REM latency on PSG can support diagnosis but sensitivity is limited.

Editorial use:
- strongest confounder guardrail.

### 4. 2025 biomarkers review

PMID: 40976193

Key points:
- PSG + MSLT remain the clinical objective testing foundation;
- CSF orexin is a useful confirmatory test for type 1 narcolepsy;
- MSLT repeatability is limited;
- no established biomarker for narcolepsy type 2 or idiopathic hypersomnia;
- differentiation of NT2 vs IH remains difficult.

Editorial use:
- prevent “MSLT as truth machine” language;
- type 2 diagnostic uncertainty must stay visible.

### 5. 2025 Narcolepsy: Beyond the Classic Pentad

PMID: 40111737

Key points:
- narcolepsy has 24-hour burden beyond EDS/cataplexy;
- nighttime sleep can be disrupted;
- management should consider age, life stage, dominant symptoms, support, comorbidities and goals.

Editorial use:
- do not flatten treatment to “give a stimulant.”

### 6. 2025 orexin-deficiency review

PMID: 41076550

- NT1 is characterized by orexin/hypocretin deficiency;
- EDS, cataplexy and REM intrusions reflect instability of wake/REM boundaries;
- orexin is central to arousal regulation.

Editorial use:
- explain pathophysiology;
- do not extrapolate into supplement “orexin booster” claims.

### 7. MSLT validation/reliability study

PMID: 9351129

- MSLT/SOREMP results can vary across repeat testing;
- test performs best in conjunction with clinical findings;
- should not be used in isolation to confirm/exclude narcolepsy.

Editorial use:
- historical support for modern repeatability concern.

### 8. 2026 immune/orexin review

PMID: 42006054

Key points:
- detailed sleep-wake history, ESS, sleep diaries and actigraphy can support phenotyping/exclusion of confounders;
- overnight PSG followed by MSLT is the objective testing core;
- CSF orexin and HLA are ancillary rather than generic screening tests;
- final classification requires clinical context and objective findings.

## Core distinctions to preserve

### Sleepiness ≠ fatigue

Sleepiness = tendency/pressure to fall asleep.
Fatigue = low energy/exhaustion that may not include abnormal sleep propensity.

Do not use these as synonyms.

### Narcolepsy ≠ insufficient sleep

A person with chronic sleep restriction may fall asleep rapidly during daytime testing.
Sleep opportunity/schedule must be assessed before interpreting MSLT.

### Narcolepsy ≠ sleep paralysis

Sleep paralysis occurs commonly outside narcolepsy.
It has diagnostic meaning only in the full symptom/testing context.

### Narcolepsy ≠ REM hallucinations alone

Hypnagogic/hypnopompic hallucinations can occur outside narcolepsy.
They are not standalone diagnostic markers.

### Cataplexy ≠ fainting

Cataplexy is emotion-triggered loss of muscle tone with preserved consciousness in the classic presentation.
Collapse/loss-of-consciousness events require a broader differential.

### Narcolepsy type 1 ≠ type 2

NT1 has cataplexy and/or orexin deficiency with required diagnostic context.
NT2 lacks cataplexy and has no established equivalent biomarker.

## MSLT guardrails

Never describe MSLT as meaningful without preparation/context.

Important confounders:
- insufficient sleep;
- shift work;
- circadian delay/misalignment;
- medications affecting REM or alertness;
- untreated sleep disorders.

Preferred wording:
> MSLT is central but must be interpreted after a properly documented sleep schedule and overnight PSG.

## Orexin rule

Allowed:
- severe orexin deficiency is central to NT1 biology;
- CSF orexin can support/establish NT1 diagnosis in the appropriate clinical pathway.

Not allowed:
- generic blood orexin testing as routine diagnosis;
- supplement claims to “restore orexin neurons”;
- implying NT2 has the same orexin biomarker.

## Treatment rule

AASM strong recommendations are for diagnosed adult narcolepsy, not for ordinary fatigue.

Never publish:
- “modafinil is best for tiredness”;
- stimulant comparisons for self-selection;
- medication dosing or DIY stacking;
- wake-promoting therapy as a substitute for OSA treatment or adequate sleep.

## Safety rule

Persistent involuntary sleepiness can affect driving and safety-sensitive work.

Do not provide individualized driving clearance.

Public page can state:
- dangerous involuntary sleep episodes warrant formal evaluation;
- symptom control and clinician assessment matter before safety-sensitive activities.

## OSA differential rule

Profound EDS can come from untreated OSA.

If snoring/witnessed apneas/gasping are present, link to:
- `/articles/sleep-apnea-vs-insomnia/`
- `/articles/snoring-vs-sleep-apnea/`

Wake-promoting treatment does not correct airway obstruction.

## Supplement inheritance rule

Do not transfer:
- magnesium sleep evidence → narcolepsy treatment;
- melatonin circadian evidence → cataplexy treatment;
- “energy” supplement evidence → pathologic sleepiness treatment;
- stimulant-like supplement evidence → AASM narcolepsy therapy.
