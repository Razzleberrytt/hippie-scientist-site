## DE17-01 — Melatonin in medication-free children with ADHD: sleep changed, daytime cognition did not

**Original trial:** Van der Heijden et al., *J Am Acad Child Adolesc Psychiatry* 2007, [PMID 17242627](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17242627/), [DOI 10.1097/01.chi.0000246055.76167.0d](https://doi.org/10.1097/01.chi.0000246055.76167.0d). Double-blind placebo-controlled four-week randomized trial of **105 medication-free children ages 6–12** who had rigorously diagnosed ADHD **and chronic sleep-onset insomnia**. The original protocol used different melatonin quantities by weight; those protocol amounts are **not instructions or recommended doses**.

**Primary outcomes:** Objectively estimated sleep onset advanced **26.9 minutes** in the melatonin arm while delaying **10.5 minutes** with placebo (P < .0001). Actigraphy-estimated total sleep increased **19.8 minutes** versus **decreasing 13.6 minutes** in placebo (P=.01). Circadian dim-light melatonin onset also shifted. **No significant between-arm improvement** was reported for daytime behavior, cognitive performance or quality of life.

**Boundary:** Demonstrating a sleep/circadian effect in selected unmedicated children with sleep-onset insomnia does not demonstrate that melatonin treats core ADHD, improves school outcomes, or is appropriate for all pediatric insomnia or commercial gummy formulations. Small/short trials cannot establish long-term growth/puberty or endocrine safety.

**Disposition:** **P0** ADHD/sleep claim audit: keep direct sleep improvements **and** null cognition/behavior in the same source-linked summary; no pediatric self-administration advice.

## DE17-02 — Prolonged-release melatonin in ASD: formulation and endpoint are essential

**Original trial:** Gringras et al., *J Am Acad Child Adolesc Psychiatry* 2017, [PMID 29096777](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29096777/), [DOI 10.1016/j.jaac.2017.09.414](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaac.2017.09.414). Double-blind placebo RCT of **125 children/adolescents aged 2–17.5**, most with autism spectrum disorder, whose insomnia had not sufficiently responded to a behavioral intervention. The intervention was a specific **pediatric prolonged-release minitablet** preparation, studied for **13 weeks**, not an ordinary immediate-release retail melatonin product.

**Primary:** Caregiver sleep diary total sleep time improved by **57.5 minutes** with prolonged-release melatonin versus **9.14 minutes** with placebo at 13 weeks (prespecified outcome P=.034). Sleep latency also improved, with a reported between-group P=.011. Trial authors described higher meaningful sleep-response rates (**68.9% vs 39.3%**). Somnolence was reported more often with active treatment.

**Boundaries and interests:** The trial does not show improvement in core autism characteristics, cognitive ability, social communication, developmental trajectory or permanent circadian correction. The author list includes pharmaceutical-industry affiliations; inspect full disclosure before any public efficacy grade. This is one product, one population and a limited follow-up, with relevant pediatric safety oversight outside this research intake.

**Disposition:** **P0** isolate a specialist pediatric-insomnia evidence claim from universal "melatonin for autism" language. No caregiver instruction or implied safety clearance.

## DE17-03 — Chamomile for generalized anxiety: the primary relapse-prevention outcome was null

**Original trial:** Mao et al., *Phytomedicine* 2016, [PMID 27912875](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27912875/), [DOI 10.1016/j.phymed.2016.10.012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phymed.2016.10.012). Two-stage GAD study: **179 enrolled** in a **12-week open-label chamomile lead-in**; only **93 responders** subsequently entered the randomized 26-week double-blind placebo-substitution continuation trial. Thus the randomized question is relapse prevention **among selected prior responders**, not new treatment initiation efficacy in all GAD patients.

**Primary:** Relapse occurred in **7/46 continued-chamomile** versus **12/47 placebo-switch** participants. Despite the numerical difference, the prespecified primary time-to-relapse analysis was **not statistically significant** (HR **0.52**, 95% CI **0.20–1.33**, P=.16). Secondary GAD symptom scores were more favorable with continued chamomile (P=.0032). The abstract reports similar low adverse-event rates within this selected and relatively small sample.

**Boundary:** The secondary symptom signal must not be substituted for a successful relapse-prevention endpoint. A placebo-substitution trial of prior open-label responders is different from a blinded initiation trial, and pharmaceutical-grade extract is not interchangeable with chamomile tea, aromatherapy, or other preparations. Potential interactions, pregnancy and long-term safety are separate unresolved reviews.

**Disposition:** **P0** retain negative primary outcome adjacent to favorable secondary symptom score; no medication-replacement framing.

## DE17-04 — Magnesium and migraine: one positive and one null multicenter trial in 1996

**RCT A, positive:** Peikert et al., *Cephalalgia* 1996, [PMID 8792038](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8792038/), [DOI 10.1046/j.1468-2982.1996.1604257.x](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1468-2982.1996.1604257.x). Randomized placebo-controlled **81-person** adult migraine-prevention trial of one **trimagnesium dicitrate** preparation for **12 weeks**. In weeks 9–12, migraine attack frequency had declined **41.6%** from baseline in the magnesium arm versus **15.8%** in placebo (between-group P < .05); migraine days and rescue-medication use also favored active treatment. The paper reported diarrhea and gastric irritation.

**RCT B, null:** Pfaffenrath et al., *Cephalalgia* 1996, [PMID 8902254](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8902254/), [DOI 10.1046/j.1468-2982.1996.1606436.x](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1468-2982.1996.1606436.x). A **different** planned 150-person multicenter RCT in migraine without aura stopped following a protocol-specified interim analysis of **69 participants**. The prespecified responder endpoint was **10/35 (28.6%) with magnesium versus 10/34 (29.4%) with placebo**. Migraine-attack and migraine-day differences were not significant; mostly mild GI adverse effects were more common in the magnesium group (**45.7% vs 23.5%**).

**Reconciliation:** Distinct trial protocols, preparations and success definitions mean one is not simply the other's replication, but the contradictory placebo-controlled findings prohibit cherry-picking an unequivocal preventive benefit. Attack-frequency change and responder thresholds are different endpoints. Neither justifies advice about high-dose magnesium, other magnesium salts or managing prescription migraine treatment.

**Disposition:** P1 contradictory-trial comparison for magnesium/migraine; independent synthesis, risk-of-bias, current safety and preparation identity before claim-grade change.

## DE17-05 — CoQ10 failed to slow Parkinson progression in a large phase III randomized trial

**Original:** Parkinson Study Group QE3 Investigators (Beal et al.), *JAMA Neurol* 2014, [PMID 24664227](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24664227/), [DOI 10.1001/jamaneurol.2014.131](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamaneurol.2014.131). Phase III multicenter, double-blind placebo-controlled trial in **600 early Parkinson disease patients** at **67 sites**. Two high-exposure CoQ10 experimental arms and placebo all received vitamin E, preserving a common co-intervention.

**Primary:** The prespecified outcome was change in UPDRS composite motor/nonmotor scores over 16 months or until dopamine replacement was needed. Futility stopping was triggered. Adjusted UPDRS score worsened **6.9 points with placebo**, **7.5** in the lower CoQ10 arm (P=.49), and **8.0** in the higher CoQ10 arm (P=.21). No evidence of clinical slowing; the trial did not identify a favorable direction on this endpoint.

**Boundary:** A mitochondrial or antioxidant rationale cannot override a failed disease-progression outcome. Migraine prevention, statin-associated muscle symptoms and hypertension involve distinct conditions, outcomes and populations; do not combine earlier CoQ10 dossiers' answers. These trial exposures are neither a dosing recommendation nor evidence that all CoQ10 uses are futile.

**Disposition:** **P0** any Parkinson/neuroprotection claim must account for this large null phase III trial; avoid marketing claims about proven dopamine-neuron preservation in humans.

## DE17-06 — Creatine did not slow Parkinson progression in the NET-PD long-term trial

**Original:** NET-PD LS-1 Investigators (Kieburtz et al.), *JAMA* 2015, [PMID 25668262](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25668262/), [DOI 10.1001/jama.2015.120](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2015.120). Randomized, double-blind multicenter trial in **1,741 people with early, treated Parkinson disease**, assigned to creatine monohydrate or placebo with intended extended follow-up and a clinical global-statistic endpoint across **five** measures of functioning and progression.

**Primary:** A prespecified interim futility analysis of **955** participants after sufficient follow-up resulted in early termination. Median follow-up was four years. The global summed-rank outcome showed **no significant clinical benefit** (P=.45); authors concluded the data did not support creatine to treat Parkinson progression. The experiment measures a clinical disease progression endpoint, not simply brain energy-metabolism biomarkers.

**Boundary:** The null in Parkinson disease **does not negate** creatine's established muscle-performance uses or the narrower sleep-deprivation experiments in DE16-04. Conversely, cognitive-task or energy-phosphate biomarker signals must not be stretched into proven long-term neuroprotection. No reason here to change an individual's treatment regimen.

**Disposition:** **P0** prevent indication-transfer from sports/sleep-loss benefits to Parkinson neuroprotection and document trial-level null outcome.

## DE17-07 — Homocysteine-lowering B vitamins: MRI brain atrophy is not dementia prevention

**Original:** Smith et al., *PLoS One* 2010, [PMID 20838622](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20838622/), [DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0012244](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0012244). VITACOG single-center, two-year double-blind randomized controlled trial of folate, B6 and B12 versus placebo in **271 people over 70 with mild cognitive impairment**. **187** consented to brain MRI; **168** completed the imaging analysis. The main imaging outcome was annual whole-brain volume loss.

**Results:** Mean annual atrophy **0.76%** in the active arm (95% CI 0.63–0.90) versus **1.08%** with placebo (95% CI 0.94–1.22), P=.001. A baseline elevated-homocysteine subgroup exhibited larger relative effects. This is a positive **imaging/surrogate** outcome in a specialized population; the original study explicitly requested future trials to determine whether dementia progression is delayed.

**Boundary:** MRI atrophy is not itself a measured reduction in incident Alzheimer's disease, preserved day-to-day cognition or an effect in healthy younger consumers. B6-related neuropathy safety and folate/B12 deficiency masking require separate evaluation; trial exposure is not a recommended self-supplementation regimen. Subsequent VITACOG papers reusing the same randomized cohort should be linked as one trial lineage, not independent replications.

**Disposition:** P1 enforce surrogate-versus-clinical firewall on B-vitamin brain-health marketing and claim lineage.

## DE17-08 — Sulforaphane in autism: small trial with improvement on scales, not an established therapy

**Original:** Singh et al., *Proc Natl Acad Sci USA* 2014, [PMID 25313065](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25313065/), [DOI 10.1073/pnas.1416940111](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1416940111). Double-blind randomized placebo-controlled research in **44 adolescent/young adult males aged 13–27** with moderate-severe ASD: **29 sulforaphane** from broccoli-sprout-derived source and **15 placebo**, assessed over 18 treatment weeks plus a four-week discontinuation period.

**Findings:** The researchers reported positive between-arm results on behavior ratings (ABC score improvement of **34%**, P < .001; SRS improvement of **17%**, P=.017), plus favorable clinician global impression subsets. After discontinuation, mean ratings rose toward baseline. These outcomes were symptom scales in a small selected male population, not objective demonstration of changed ASD neurodevelopment or improvements in all functional domains.

**Boundary:** Hypotheses about oxidative stress, mitochondria, Nrf2 and neuroinflammation were rationale, not proof of the mechanism causing the clinical results. A broccoli-sprout-derived preparation is not equivalent to raw broccoli, all sulforaphane supplements, or a consumer "detox" product. Independent randomized replication and child/adolescent safety, dosing and interaction checks remain incomplete here. No extrapolation to broad ASD treatment instructions.

**Disposition:** **P0** high-stakes population guard; retain positive measured scales but with sample size, sex, preparation and unreplicated-effect limitations.

## DE17-09 — Multivitamins in COSMOS: cognitive test signals versus null MCI/dementia incidence

**Original cognitive substudy:** Yeung et al., *Am J Clin Nutr* 2023, [PMID 37244291](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37244291/), [DOI 10.1016/j.ajcnut.2023.05.011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajcnut.2023.05.011). COSMOS-Web ancillary randomized research enrolled **3,562 older adults** allocated to a multivitamin/mineral formulation or placebo, assessed by internet cognitive tests over up to three years. The **prespecified one-year immediate word-recall outcome** favored multivitamins (**P=.025**); other cognitive-task **secondary outcomes were null**. The paper's "equivalent to three years" comparison is a cross-sectional age-norm model, **not three years of observed reduction in dementia risk**.

**Separate outcome within shared COSMOS research program:** Sachs et al., *Alzheimers Dement* 2023, [PMID 37035889](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37035889/), [DOI 10.1002/alz.13078](https://doi.org/10.1002/alz.13078). COSMOS-Mind **N=2,262** studied incident mild cognitive impairment and probable dementia over three years. **110 MCI cases and only 14 dementia cases** were adjudicated; **incidence did not significantly vary** by multivitamin or cocoa-extract assignment. The small dementia case count means this is also an imprecise test of rare clinical incidence, not conclusive no-effect proof.

**Trial hierarchy caution:** COSMOS-Web and COSMOS-Mind are named substudies in the same parent trial; check non-overlapping participant sets, original allocation and combined estimates before interpreting independence. Direct episodic-memory test improvement is **not** equivalent to demonstrated clinical dementia prevention; null incidence must remain visible.

**Disposition:** P1 revise any "multivitamins prevent dementia" statements; annotate exact cognitive endpoint and shared parent trial identity.

## DE17-10 — Ginger for chemotherapy nausea: two different RCTs gave different answers

**More recent positive trial:** Crichton et al., *J Acad Nutr Diet* 2024 (online 2023), [PMID 37699474](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37699474/), [DOI 10.1016/j.jand.2023.09.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jand.2023.09.003). A double-blind multicenter placebo-controlled trial of **103 adults** receiving moderately/highly emetogenic chemotherapy, with a standardized ginger product **added to regular antiemetic care**. Prespecified main outcome was nausea-related **quality of life**, favoring ginger (P=.003); other favorable outcomes included delayed nausea severity (P=.003) and selected cycle-specific incidence. The experiment found no significant benefit on acute/anticipatory nausea, depression or anxiety. Several cycle-specific comparisons were secondary and multiplicity-sensitive; review the precise protocol before promotion.

**Earlier negative trial:** Zick et al., *Support Care Cancer* 2009, [PMID 19005687](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19005687/), [DOI 10.1007/s00520-008-0528-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00520-008-0528-8). Different double-blind placebo RCT of **162** oncology patients with **prior chemotherapy-induced nausea**, receiving standard antiemetics including 5-HT3 antagonists and/or aprepitant. The **primary outcome**, prevalence of delayed nausea/vomiting, was **null**; acute symptom measures were also null. Ginger combined with aprepitant was associated with worse acute nausea than aprepitant alone in this trial; further interaction interpretation is needed.

**Why results differ:** These are separate randomized populations using different standardized/bulk preparations, exposure schedules, antiemetic backgrounds, outcome definitions and chemotherapy contexts. A nausea-related QoL signal does not automatically establish prevention of emesis, while an earlier symptom null does not prove the newer QoL effect impossible. In no case is ginger a replacement for evidence-based antiemetic treatment.

**Disposition:** **P0** oncology-relevant evidence card must show both RCTs, segregate QoL, anticipatory/acute/delayed nausea and vomiting outcomes, inspect concomitant medication and preparation details, and withhold unsupervised combination guidance.
