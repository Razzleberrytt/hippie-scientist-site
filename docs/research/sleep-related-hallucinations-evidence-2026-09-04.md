# Sleep-Related Hallucinations — Evidence Dossier

**Updated:** 2026-09-04  
**Public article:** `content/articles/sleep-related-hallucinations.md`  
**Primary question:** How should hypnagogic and hypnopompic perceptual experiences be separated from narcolepsy, sleep paralysis, nightmares, exploding head syndrome, daytime psychosis, severe sleep deprivation, and complex nocturnal visual hallucinations?

## Editorial thesis

Sleep-transition hallucinations are a **timing-defined perceptual phenomenon**, not a diagnosis of narcolepsy or psychosis by themselves.

The central editorial rule is:

> A vivid perception occurring while falling asleep or waking can occur within normal sleep. Diagnostic weight comes from the surrounding syndrome, timing, daytime state, functional impact, and associated neurological/sleep features—not from the word “hallucination” alone.

## Non-negotiable distinctions

1. **Hypnagogic** = around sleep onset.
2. **Hypnopompic** = around awakening.
3. Isolated sleep-transition hallucinations can be non-pathological.
4. Hypnagogic/hypnopompic hallucinations are associated with narcolepsy but are not specific enough to diagnose it alone.
5. Sleep paralysis can include hallucinations but the two can occur separately.
6. Nightmare ≠ sleep-transition hallucination.
7. Exploding head syndrome ≠ generic auditory hallucination.
8. Complex nocturnal visual hallucinations (CNVH) are not automatically equivalent to ordinary hypnagogic/hypnopompic experiences.
9. Persistent daytime hallucinations require a broader differential than isolated sleep-boundary experiences.
10. Prolonged severe sleep-deprivation hallucinations must not be generalized to ordinary one-night sleep loss or normal hypnagogia.
11. Case reports of melatonin response in CNVH do not establish melatonin as a general treatment.

## Core sources

### 1. Waters et al. — Sleep-Related Hallucinations (2024)
- PMID: **38368061**
- Type: Clinical review
- Most important source for modern classification.
- Key boundary: hypnagogic and hypnopompic hallucinations occurring without another disorder and within normal sleep are most likely non-pathological.
- Separates ordinary HHH from complex nocturnal visual hallucinations.
- Explicitly notes that CNVH conceptualization remains relatively new and mechanistic relationships with HHH need more research.

**Allowed claim:** Isolated sleep-transition hallucinations can occur within normal sleep and are not automatically pathological.

**Forbidden upgrade:** “All hallucinations at night are harmless.”

### 2. 2023 hypnagogic-state questionnaire
- PMID: **37812995**
- n = **4,456**
- Broad hypnagogic states reported by **80.2%**.
- Experiences most commonly kinaesthetic and visual; auditory/tactile/olfactory-gustatory less common.

**Critical measurement warning:** The 80.2% estimate concerns a broad self-reported **hypnagogic state** construct. Do not rewrite as “80.2% have clinically significant hallucinations.”

### 3. Hypnagogic state brief update (2022)
- PMID: **36017720**
- Review of terminology, prevalence, phenomenology, and research gaps.
- Reinforces that definitions vary and hypnagogia should be differentiated from nearby phenomena.

### 4. Twilight Zone epidemiology study (2021)
- PMID: **34062377**
- Auditory HHH prevalence reported at **6.8%**; multimodal HHH **12.3%** in the studied sample.
- People with only sleep-related auditory hallucinations had less disturbance/interference and better health-related profiles than people with daytime auditory hallucinations.

**Allowed claim:** Sleep-boundary-only hallucination groups can differ meaningfully from groups with daytime hallucinations.

**Do not infer:** sleep-related hallucinations can never coexist with psychiatric illness.

### 5. Ohayon et al. representative UK study (1996)
- PMID: **8894197**
- n = **4,972**, ages 15–100.
- Hypnagogic hallucinations: **37%**.
- Hypnopompic hallucinations: **12.5%**.
- Narcolepsy prevalence in the study: **0.04%**.
- HHH were far more common than narcolepsy could explain.

**Strength:** Representative community sampling for its era.

**Limit:** Old study; telephone definitions and diagnostic frameworks differ from modern work. Use to establish non-specificity, not a timeless universal prevalence constant.

### 6. REM sleep in narcolepsy (2024)
- PMID: **39186901**
- Review of REM dysregulation in narcolepsy.
- Hypnagogic/hypnopompic hallucinations belong to a broader REM-state-instability syndrome.

**Editorial implication:** A hallucination can be a narcolepsy feature without being a narcolepsy test.

### 7. Narcolepsy and psychosis systematic review (2021)
- PMID: **33779983**
- Full-text review of 100 papers.
- Separates:
  - typical narcolepsy sleep-transition psychotic-like experiences;
  - atypical dream/reality confusion; and
  - separate comorbid schizophrenia-spectrum illness.

**Key boundary:** Do not equate typical sleep-transition narcolepsy hallucinations with persistent psychosis, and do not dismiss true daytime psychosis as narcolepsy.

### 8. Severe sleep-deprivation synthesis (2018)
- PMID: **30042701**
- 21 eligible studies; **760 participants**.
- Wake deprivation ranged **24 h to 11 nights**.
- Perceptual distortions and hallucinations became more prominent as time awake increased.

**Critical boundary:** Much of this literature concerns extreme/prolonged sleep deprivation. It does not support “one bad night commonly causes hallucinations.”

### 9. Melatonin-responsive CNVH case report (2018)
- PMID: **29609711**
- **3 patients**.
- Reported dramatic improvement in complex nocturnal visual hallucinations with melatonin.

**Evidence class:** case report / hypothesis-generating.

**Forbidden upgrade:** “Melatonin treats sleep-related hallucinations.”

## Prevalence handling

Never collapse the following into one number:

- broad hypnagogic states;
- narrowly defined hypnagogic hallucinations;
- hypnopompic hallucinations;
- auditory-only HHH;
- multimodal HHH;
- complex nocturnal visual hallucinations;
- narcolepsy-associated hallucinations.

Preferred wording:

> Estimates vary substantially with definition and sampling, but sleep-transition perceptual experiences are common enough that their presence alone is not specific for narcolepsy or psychosis.

## Differential matrix

| Phenomenon | Typical timing/state | Core clue | What it does NOT prove |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hypnagogic hallucination | Falling asleep | vivid sleep-transition perception | narcolepsy or psychosis |
| Hypnopompic hallucination | Waking | dreamlike perception persists into waking | narcolepsy or psychosis |
| Sleep paralysis | sleep-wake boundary | awareness + transient REM atonia | narcolepsy by itself |
| Nightmare | within sleep/dream, then awakening | remembered distressing dream narrative | NREM parasomnia or psychosis |
| Exploding head syndrome | sleep transition | abrupt painless perceived bang/explosion | seizure or headache disorder by default |
| CNVH | nighttime awakenings / sleep transition | detailed recurrent visual images | one single etiology |
| Narcolepsy | 24-h sleep-wake disorder | pathological EDS ± cataplexy/REM intrusions/testing | diagnosed by hallucinations alone |
| Daytime psychosis | full waking state | persistent hallucinations/delusions/disorganization outside sleep boundary | explained away as hypnagogia |
| Severe sleep-deprivation phenomenon | prolonged wakefulness | progressive perceptual/cognitive disturbance | ordinary hypnagogia |

## Narcolepsy firewall

The site must not use:

> “Hypnagogic hallucinations are a sign you have narcolepsy.”

Use:

> “They are a recognized narcolepsy symptom, but they are common outside narcolepsy. Persistent irresistible daytime sleepiness, cataplexy, sleep paralysis, disrupted nighttime sleep, and appropriately interpreted diagnostic testing carry more diagnostic weight.”

Canonical internal route:
- `/articles/narcolepsy-excessive-daytime-sleepiness/`

## Psychosis firewall

The article must preserve both sides:

- sleep-boundary hallucinations can occur without psychotic illness;
- persistent daytime hallucinations, impaired reality testing, delusional interpretation, marked mood/disorganization, or neurological/toxic changes require a different frame.

Avoid stigmatizing language and avoid consumer psychiatric diagnosis.

## Sleep paralysis firewall

Hallucinations and sleep paralysis often coexist, especially when dream imagery persists into waking awareness, but neither requires the other.

Canonical internal route:
- `/articles/sleep-paralysis/`

## Exploding Head Syndrome firewall

EHS is classically a sudden **painless** perceived bang/explosion at sleep transition. It is not simply “hearing a hallucination.”

Do not transfer nightmare treatment evidence such as imagery rehearsal to isolated HHH.

## Treatment boundary

Evidence does **not** support a universal supplement or medication treatment for isolated benign HHH.

Prioritize:
- explanation/reassurance when the pattern is clearly isolated and sleep-boundary-limited;
- adequate sleep opportunity and correction of severe sleep disruption where relevant;
- evaluation of an underlying disorder when the syndrome indicates narcolepsy, seizure, neurological disease, medication/substance effect, or persistent daytime psychosis.

Do not give individualized medication stopping, starting, dosing, or retiming advice.

## Testing boundary

Polysomnography is not required merely because a person has had one hypnagogic image.

Testing is driven by the suspected underlying disorder.

For suspected narcolepsy, PSG/MSLT preparation and confounders remain critical; insufficient sleep, circadian mismatch, shift work, and medication effects can distort results.

## Safety / escalation context

The article should encourage broader evaluation when experiences are:
- frequent or escalating;
- highly distressing or functionally impairing;
- occurring during full daytime wakefulness;
- paired with pathological daytime sleepiness/cataplexy;
- associated with stereotyped seizure-like events, confusion, loss of consciousness, injury, or neurological change;
- temporally associated with medication/substance exposure or withdrawal;
- occurring as recurrent complex visual hallucinations in someone with visual impairment, cognitive change, or neurological disease.

This is differential framing, not a diagnosis algorithm.

## Evidence grade rationale

**Moderate** for the broad classification/differential conclusion because multiple epidemiological studies and modern reviews support the distinction between common sleep-transition phenomena and disorder-specific syndromes.

**Low/very low** for a specific treatment of isolated HHH because direct controlled treatment evidence is sparse and the melatonin evidence cited for CNVH is case-report level.

## Bottom-line editorial rule

Sleep-related hallucinations should be normalized **without being trivialized**.

The scientifically defensible message is:

> The sleep-wake boundary can generate vivid perceptions in healthy people. The event becomes diagnostically meaningful through its timing, frequency, daytime context, associated REM/sleepiness features, neurological or psychiatric context, and functional impact—not because the word “hallucination” is inherently pathological.
