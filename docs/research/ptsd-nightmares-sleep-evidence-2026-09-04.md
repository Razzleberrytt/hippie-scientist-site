# PTSD, Nightmares and Sleep — Evidence Dossier

Date: 2026-09-04
Status: publication-ready research support for `content/articles/ptsd-nightmares-and-sleep.md`

## Research question

How should the site distinguish PTSD-related insomnia, trauma-related nightmares, fear of sleep, trauma-focused treatment, CBT-I, imagery rehearsal therapy, pharmacologic nightmare treatment and coexisting sleep-disordered breathing without collapsing them into one “sleep problem”?

## Editorial verdict

PTSD-related sleep disturbance should be treated as a set of overlapping but separable targets.

- **CBT-I** has direct randomized evidence for insomnia in PTSD.
- **Imagery rehearsal therapy (IRT)** has meaningful direct evidence for recurrent trauma-related nightmares.
- IRT is not automatically additive to CBT-I; veteran RCTs have found no clear superiority of CBT-I + IRT over CBT-I alone on primary nightmare outcomes.
- Trauma-focused therapy can improve PTSD without fully resolving insomnia or nightmares.
- Better sleep does not automatically imply a larger PTSD response.
- Prazosin has supportive randomized/meta-analytic evidence for nightmares, but the medication literature remains heterogeneous and does not support site-wide dosing advice.
- Sleep-disordered breathing deserves separate evaluation because airway obstruction is not treated by sedation, CBT-I or imagery rehearsal.

Evidence grade: **Moderate** overall.

The grade is not High because treatment effects vary by endpoint, comparator, population, treatment combination and veteran/civilian setting.

## Anchor evidence

### 1. CBT-I randomized trial in PTSD

PMID: 24497661

Design:
- randomized controlled trial;
- 45 adults with PTSD and research-diagnosed insomnia;
- 8-session CBT-I vs monitor-only waitlist;
- sleep diary, actigraphy, PSG and clinical outcomes.

Key results:
- CBT-I superior across sleep-diary outcomes;
- improved PSG-measured total sleep time;
- full subjective sleep remission: **41% CBT-I vs 0% waitlist**;
- gains maintained at 6 months.

Editorial use:
- direct support for treating insomnia as a real comorbidity in PTSD;
- do not imply CBT-I treats the entire PTSD syndrome.

### 2. 2025 CBT-I + prolonged exposure RCT

PMID: 40488726

Design:
- 94 veterans with PTSD and insomnia;
- CBT-I + prolonged exposure vs sleep hygiene + prolonged exposure.

Key results:
- PTSD symptoms improved in both groups;
- no between-group advantage for PTSD symptoms (`P = .844`);
- CBT-I arm showed significantly larger improvements in ISI, sleep efficiency, total sleep time and quality of life.

Editorial use:
- one of the cleanest endpoint-separation examples in the cluster;
- better insomnia treatment ≠ stronger PTSD-symptom treatment;
- sleep hygiene ≠ CBT-I.

### 3. 2023 combined sleep + PTSD pilot RCT

PMID: 37322836

Design:
- 93 active-duty military personnel;
- CBT-I/nightmare treatment before or after CPT vs CPT alone;
- study terminated early and underpowered.

Reported comparative effect sizes vs CPT alone:
- PTSD symptoms: `d = -0.36`;
- insomnia: `d = -0.77`;
- sleep efficiency: `d = 0.62`;
- nightmares: `d = -0.53`.

Editorial use:
- promising support for combined care;
- preserve underpowered/early-termination limitation;
- do not state that a specific sequence is proven superior.

### 4. Landmark IRT RCT

PMID: 11476655

Population:
- 168 women with chronic nightmares;
- 95% had moderate-to-severe PTSD;
- predominantly sexual-assault survivors.

Key outcomes vs waitlist:
- nights/week with nightmares: `d = 1.24`;
- number of nightmares/week: `d = 0.85`;
- PSQI sleep quality: `d = 0.67`;
- PTSD symptom outcomes also improved;
- gains sustained to 6 months.

Editorial use:
- direct support for imagery rehearsal;
- population must remain visible;
- do not generalize one trial into universal efficacy.

### 5. IRT + CBT-I vs CBT-I in combat veterans

PMID: 31053215

Design:
- 108 U.S. Iraq/Afghanistan veterans;
- current severe PTSD and recurrent deployment-related nightmares;
- IR + CBT-I vs CBT-I alone.

Key results:
- both groups improved;
- IR + CBT-I not superior to CBT-I alone on primary nightmare frequency/distress outcomes;
- 29% had reduction in nightmare frequency;
- 22% reached remission.

Editorial use:
- critical guardrail against “stacking therapies must work better” logic;
- supports comparator-aware interpretation.

### 6. Imagery rehearsal meta-analysis

PMID: 22819998

- 13 studies of imagery rehearsal for post-trauma nightmares;
- large pre/post effects on nightmare frequency, sleep quality and PTSD symptoms;
- CBT-I + imagery rehearsal improved sleep quality more than imagery rehearsal alone;
- combined treatment did not clearly add nightmare/PTSD benefits;
- active-control evidence was much weaker than uncontrolled/pre-post effects.

Editorial use:
- emphasize comparator sensitivity.

### 7. 2024 veteran imagery-rescripting review/meta-analysis

PMID: 39074713

- veteran-focused systematic review/meta-analysis;
- supports improvements in nightmare and sleep outcomes;
- preserve population and study-quality limitations.

### 8. Trauma-related nightmare network meta-analysis

PMID: 35661755

- 29 randomized clinical trials;
- 2,214 trauma survivors;
- IRT and prazosin emerged as supported interventions;
- other interventions had less certain comparative efficacy.

Editorial use:
- supports both behavioral and pharmacological nightmare pathways;
- do not treat rank ordering as permanent or universal.

### 9. PTSD sleep pharmacotherapy network meta-analysis

PMID: 38795401

- 99 RCTs;
- 10,481 participants;
- prazosin ranked favorably for insomnia, nightmares and poor sleep quality;
- authors emphasize underpowered comparisons and very-low to moderate certainty.

Editorial use:
- preserve uncertainty;
- no site-wide dosing;
- distinguish somnolence from therapeutic sleep outcomes.

### 10. 2025 nightmare-treatment review

PMID: 40932590

- IRT and prazosin remain among the most supported approaches;
- effects vary across studies;
- newer CBT-N, digital and integrated approaches are promising but not yet equivalent to a mature evidence base.

### 11. 2023 trauma-focused psychotherapy sleep review

PMID: 37393069

- 16 primary studies;
- sleep disturbance associated with higher PTSD severity;
- most sleep problems did not clearly block trauma-focused psychotherapy response;
- sleep-disordered breathing was the notable concern;
- improvements in insomnia, sleep duration and sleep quality tracked with greater treatment gains, but certainty was low/very low.

Editorial use:
- do not claim insomnia must always be treated first;
- do not claim trauma therapy automatically fixes sleep.

### 12. Brief behavioral insomnia treatment and nightmares

PMID: 35393934

- 91 veterans with insomnia;
- brief behavioral treatment for insomnia vs progressive muscle relaxation;
- trauma-related nightmare frequency improved post-treatment;
- effect was not maintained at 6 months;
- non-trauma bad dreams did not improve.

Editorial use:
- insomnia treatment may reduce some nightmare burden;
- durability and nightmare specificity matter.

### 13. Fear of sleep analysis

PMID: 29991428

- PTSD + insomnia sample from the CBT-I RCT;
- greater fear of sleep associated with PTSD severity, nightmare frequency and hypervigilance;
- CBT-I reduced fear of sleep.

Editorial use:
- supports conditioned/hyperarousal pathway;
- do not convert association into proof that fear of sleep causes PTSD insomnia in every patient.

## Core distinctions to preserve

### Insomnia ≠ nightmares

Nightmares can coexist with insomnia but are a separate endpoint and may need a separate intervention.

### CBT-I ≠ imagery rehearsal

CBT-I primarily targets insomnia mechanisms.
IRT primarily targets recurrent nightmare content/frequency/distress.

### Trauma therapy ≠ sleep therapy

CPT/PE target PTSD.
CBT-I targets insomnia.
IRT targets nightmares.

Overlap in outcomes does not make the interventions interchangeable.

### Sedation ≠ successful sleep treatment

Somnolence is an adverse effect or state, not proof of improved sleep continuity, nightmare remission or PTSD treatment.

### Better sleep ≠ PTSD remission

Use the 2025 CBT-I + PE trial as the strongest guardrail: sleep improved more with CBT-I, but PTSD improvement did not differ by group.

### PTSD improvement ≠ sleep remission

Residual insomnia/nightmares can remain after trauma-focused treatment and deserve direct evaluation.

## Medication guardrails

Prazosin:
- supported by several trials/meta-analyses;
- variable effects across populations and trials;
- individualized clinician assessment required;
- do not publish universal starting doses, escalation schedules or self-treatment recipes in this article.

Other sedating medications:
- do not infer efficacy from sleepiness;
- evidence for sleep-specific PTSD outcomes is heterogeneous;
- avoid class-wide claims from isolated trials.

## Sleep-apnea guardrail

PTSD does not rule out OSA.

If snoring, witnessed apnea, gasping, marked daytime sleepiness or other breathing clues are present:
- link to `sleep-apnea-vs-insomnia`;
- link to `snoring-vs-sleep-apnea`;
- do not imply CBT-I, IRT or sedatives correct airway obstruction.

## Supplement inheritance rules

Do **not** transfer broad evidence into this page as if it were PTSD-specific.

Examples:
- magnesium-for-sleep evidence ≠ PTSD nightmare evidence;
- general melatonin evidence ≠ trauma-nightmare evidence;
- anxiety-reduction evidence ≠ PTSD efficacy;
- a sedating herb ≠ IRT or trauma therapy;
- branded insomnia supplement trials ≠ treatment of PTSD.

## Claims that must not reappear

- “Every PTSD nightmare needs IRT.”
- “IRT always improves CBT-I.”
- “Prazosin is universally effective.”
- “A sedative treats PTSD nightmares because it causes sleepiness.”
- “Treat insomnia before trauma therapy in every case.”
- “PTSD therapy always resolves sleep.”
- “Supplements proven for ordinary insomnia are proven for PTSD.”
- “Nightmares and insomnia are the same disorder.”
