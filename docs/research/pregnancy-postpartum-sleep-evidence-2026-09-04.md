# Pregnancy and Postpartum Sleep — Evidence Dossier — 2026-09-04

## Research question

How should the sleep cluster discuss pregnancy and postpartum sleep without treating all sleep disruption as unavoidable, all postpartum sleep loss as insomnia, or general-adult supplement evidence as pregnancy safety evidence?

## Verdict

Pregnancy and postpartum sleep problems are heterogeneous. Pregnancy-specific CBT-I has randomized-trial evidence for improving insomnia severity and sleep quality. Broader nonpharmacological pregnancy interventions improve self-reported sleep quality but do not consistently increase sleep duration or efficiency, especially on objective measures.

Sleep apnea and restless legs are important pregnancy-specific competing explanations that require separate evaluation. Postpartum sleep fragmentation can reflect loss of sleep opportunity rather than insomnia, although conditioned insomnia can develop on top of caregiving-related sleep loss. Postpartum sleep interventions modestly reduce depressive-symptom severity but are not proven to prevent postpartum depression.

## High-value evidence anchors

### 2023 pregnancy CBT-I meta-analysis

- PMID: 37952481
- 9 RCTs, n=978.
- Insomnia severity improved immediately post-treatment: MD -2.69 (95% CI -3.41 to -1.96), high-quality evidence.
- Sleep quality also improved; short-term follow-up benefits persisted.
- Medium/long-term evidence limited and inconsistent.
- Delivery included face-to-face, digital, group, individual, telephone/email formats.

### 2023 pregnancy CBT-I / sleep-disturbance meta-analysis

- PMID: 36529887
- 8 RCTs, n=743.
- ISI MD -4.25 (95% CI -6.32 to -2.19).
- PSQI MD -3.30 (95% CI -4.81 to -1.79).
- Sleep-onset latency improved.
- No significant pooled difference in total sleep time or sleep efficiency.
- Reinforces endpoint-specific writing.

### 2025 nonpharmacological pregnancy sleep meta-analysis

- PMID: 40165730
- 31 RCTs, n=3,307.
- Self-reported sleep quality SMD -0.99 (95% CI -1.40 to -0.58), but I² 96.3%.
- No consistent significant improvement in sleep duration or sleep efficiency, including objective measures.
- Objective TST pooled SMD 0.36 (95% CI -0.04 to 0.77).
- Objective sleep efficiency SMD 0.17 (95% CI -0.07 to 0.42).
- Preserve the very high heterogeneity.

### 2025 postpartum sleep and health meta-analysis

- PMID: 40011016
- 60 studies, n=20,684, postpartum through 1 year.
- Five RCTs, n=992: sleep interventions reduced depressive-symptom severity, SMD -0.27 (95% CI -0.40 to -0.14), high certainty.
- Sleep interventions did not reduce odds of developing depression.
- No clear effects on anxiety, cardiometabolic outcomes, maternal anthropometrics or exclusive breastfeeding in the pooled evidence.
- Guardrail against overselling sleep intervention as postpartum-depression prevention.

### 2023 pregnancy OSA consensus guideline

- PMID: 37411038
- Pregnancy-specific expert guideline on screening, diagnosis and treatment of obstructive sleep apnea.
- Supports treating snoring/gasping/high-risk symptoms as more than generic pregnancy fatigue.
- Evidence quality varies and recommendations require individualized clinical context.

### 2024 AASM restless legs guideline

- PMID: 39324694
- Explicitly notes RLS is common in pregnancy.
- First step includes addressing exacerbating factors such as alcohol, caffeine, antihistaminergic/serotonergic/antidopaminergic medications and untreated OSA.
- Pregnancy-specific safety profile matters for treatment choice.

### Prenatal sleep / postpartum depression association meta-analysis

- PMID: 36738337
- 16 studies, n=12,614.
- Poor pregnancy sleep associated with higher postpartum depression risk, with high heterogeneity.
- Observational association; do not frame as proof that sleep disturbance causes postpartum depression.

## Claim guardrails

1. Do not say sleep problems are simply unavoidable in pregnancy.
2. Do not equate postpartum sleep deprivation from infant caregiving with insomnia disorder.
3. Do not imply CBT-I necessarily increases total sleep time; major benefits can occur in insomnia severity/quality without TST change.
4. Preserve the extreme heterogeneity in the broad 2025 nonpharmacological intervention meta-analysis.
5. Do not say sleep interventions prevent postpartum depression; evidence supports a small reduction in depressive-symptom severity, not reduced incidence.
6. Do not attribute all pregnancy fatigue to normal pregnancy; OSA and RLS are meaningful alternatives.
7. Do not automatically apply general adult supplement efficacy/safety data to pregnancy or breastfeeding.
8. Do not provide blind iron, magnesium, sedative or herbal recommendations for pregnancy-related sleep symptoms.
9. Do not present severe postpartum mood symptoms as a sleep-optimization problem.
