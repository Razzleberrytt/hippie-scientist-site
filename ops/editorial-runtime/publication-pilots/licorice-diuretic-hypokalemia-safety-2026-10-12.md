> **Bottom line:** Real licorice root containing **glycyrrhizin** can affect potassium and blood pressure. In a small, randomized crossover study, licorice combined with the thiazide diuretic **hydrochlorothiazide** significantly lowered plasma potassium, and **2 of 10** participants developed laboratory-defined hypokalemia during combination exposure. That figure is a tiny experimental sample, **not a population incidence estimate**. Separate human observations and medical references support particular concern when licorice is combined with medicines that affect potassium or in people with hypertension, kidney disease or heart disease. [Hukkanen 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19562574/) · [NIH NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/licorice-root)

## The 10-person crossover study: what happened?

Hukkanen, Ukkola and Savolainen published an **open-label, randomized crossover trial** in 2009. Ten healthy volunteers were assigned brief periods consuming **genuine licorice alone** and **licorice together with hydrochlorothiazide**, a prescription thiazide diuretic. Each studied period lasted approximately **two weeks**. [PMID 19562574](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19562574/)

| Study measure | Licorice alone | Licorice + hydrochlorothiazide |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plasma potassium | No statistically significant change | **Average decrease of 0.32 mmol/L**, p=.0015 |
| People developing hypokalemia | None reported in the licorice-only study phase | **2 out of 10** during combination phase |
| Kidney-function and endocrine measures | No significant change in measured basic serum markers or renin/aldosterone in the solo phase | Plasma renin activity increased; weight also decreased over the phase |

Both cases of hypokalemia occurred during the **first week** of combination exposure and led to early discontinuation. This supports a **real potential interaction** in the tested conditions. The study is too small to determine how commonly it occurs in typical patients, which patients are at highest risk, whether events occur with every licorice product, or what exposure level is always safe.

The fact that potassium did not significantly fall during the licorice-only phase is not proof that licorice on its own is harmless. This trial could miss rare events or slower cumulative effects, and the combined intervention specifically explored one diuretic. Other medicines and patient conditions were not tested here.

## Why could licorice and a diuretic have overlapping effects?

Thiazide diuretics can cause potassium depletion in some people. Certain licorice constituents, particularly the component **glycyrrhizin** and related metabolites, can alter steroid metabolism and mineralocorticoid signaling; biologically this can cause sodium retention, blood-pressure elevation and potassium loss. The enzyme often discussed in the literature is **11-beta-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 (11β-HSD2)**.

That mechanism is useful context; it does **not** mean a label saying “licorice” establishes the amount of active constituent, or that every affected person experiences the same clinical effect. Actual evidence comes from observed potassium and blood-pressure measurements, clinical cases and preparation analyses. It is a mistake to promote a plausible pathway into a universal harm incidence.

## Additional human evidence about high blood pressure

A separate 2003 intervention study by Sigurjónsdóttir and colleagues compared responses to licorice ingestion among participants with **essential hypertension** and those with normal blood pressure. Over four weeks, office-measured systolic blood pressure increased on average **15.3 mmHg in the hypertensive group (p=.003)** versus **3.5 mmHg in normotensive volunteers (p<.06)**. The difference in systolic response between groups was statistically significant (**p=.004**). [PMID 12574791](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12574791/)

This is not a placebo-controlled trial of heart attack prevention or harm and should not be used to predict blood-pressure changes from a different everyday food. It does reinforce the clinical reason to treat baseline hypertension as an important risk modifier. Participants received the *experimental preparation* and amount described in the study, **not a broadly transferable serving suggestion**.

The [NIH NCCIH licorice safety review](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/licorice-root) describes potentially serious effects of glycyrrhizin, including heart-rhythm disturbances, especially after substantial or prolonged exposure or in people with elevated blood pressure, heart or kidney conditions. Serious outcomes described in surveillance and case histories are **not** incidence numbers for current retail supplements.

## Licorice candy is not always licorice root

Flavor names create confusion. Some products sold as **black licorice** are flavored with anise rather than real *Glycyrrhiza* root. An anise-flavored candy is not necessarily a source of licorice glycyrrhizin. Conversely, products described as herbal tea, extract, powder, candy or a complex botanical blend may contain genuine licorice material, sometimes with unclear constituent amounts.

Even two products based on the same plant species may vary with extraction, source part, manufacturer processing and lot. A dietary-supplement package is not evidence that a trial's chemical exposure applies to that particular product.

**Product-identity rule:** Distinguish **actual glycyrrhizin-containing licorice**, merely licorice-*flavored* foods, and deglycyrrhizinated licorice before making any claim about potassium.

## What about DGL (deglycyrrhizinated licorice)?

DGL products are processed to remove much of the glycyrrhizin associated with potassium and blood-pressure effects. They are therefore chemically and clinically **different** from the licorice exposure used in many hypertension/hypokalemia studies. But a product description saying “DGL” does not itself prove a particular finished product is free of glycyrrhizin, or that all other ingredients are safe.

An evidence summary should not transfer the exact Hukkanen potassium drop to verified DGL without direct exposure data; it also should not declare all DGL products free from all drug interactions. Evidence for stomach symptoms or other proposed uses must be assessed separately from the electrolyte hazard.

## Which combinations need special clinical attention?

[Memorial Sloan Kettering's licorice monograph](https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/licorice) highlights diuretic-related hypokalemia and additional potential interactions. The 2009 experiment concerns **hydrochlorothiazide specifically**; that does not establish identical effect sizes for every loop or thiazide-like diuretic, but gives a credible human interaction signal for clinicians to evaluate.

The NIH also cautions that **heart and kidney conditions, existing hypertension and other medicines** can change the risk-benefit picture. If someone takes potassium-affecting prescription medication, the safe clinical course is to review **all relevant products and treatments with a clinician or pharmacist**, rather than making independent medicine or supplement changes based on a blog.

The symptoms of low potassium may include weakness, muscle cramps, palpitations and more serious rhythm problems, but symptoms alone cannot identify the cause or potassium level. Suspected severe adverse effects need prompt medical evaluation.

## Frequently asked questions

### Did the study show 20% of licorice consumers develop low potassium?

**No.** Two of ten volunteers in one short licorice-plus-hydrochlorothiazide trial developed hypokalemia. With only ten people and a particular combination exposure, **2/10 must not be represented as a general population risk percentage**.

### Is there a reliable universally safe licorice dose?

Not established by these studies. Glycyrrhizin content varies, exposure context matters, and some groups are more susceptible. Amounts used experimentally are **not** an evidence-based recommendation for individual consumers.

### Does the trial show licorice alone is always safe?

No. The solo phase had a nonsignificant potassium change in ten healthy volunteers; this cannot detect every possible exposure and does not override broader blood-pressure and potassium-risk data.

### Can DGL be treated as chemically identical to whole-root licorice?

No. The reason it is marketed as a distinct preparation is that removal of glycyrrhizin materially changes the exposed constituents. The specific product and any residual glycyrrhizin still require verification.

### Does licorice have proven benefits for blood pressure or general health?

There is insufficient evidence to broadly endorse licorice root for health conditions, and glycyrrhizin-containing licorice may **raise**, not lower, blood pressure. Some separate topical and oral preparations have been studied for other indications; efficacy for those does not negate potassium-safety concerns.

## Bottom line for the evidence record

The high-confidence *direction of concern* is that glycyrrhizin-containing licorice **can intensify potassium-lowering effects**, especially in a susceptible person or medication combination. The tested licorice-hydrochlorothiazide crossover gives a clear human physiological result, but the sample is too small to quantify real-world frequency. The prior trial's solo-phase null and the difference between root, flavored candy and DGL belong next to any safety statement.

Before this information is used for public therapeutic recommendations or product rankings, the site needs **human approval**, canonical source reconciliation and independent evaluation of the exact intended product/exposure. Nothing here supports changing a prescription or self-managing suspected electrolyte abnormalities.
