# Four DMT-related molecules, explained without a chemistry degree

**Quick answer:** When most people say **DMT**, they mean **N,N-DMT**. But related chemicals include **5-MeO-DMT**, **5-HO-DMT** (bufotenine), and **4-HO-DMT** (psilocin). They belong to a related chemical family, yet they are not simply four strengths of the same drug. Even a small change to a molecule can change what it does in the body. [1–5]

## First, what is a molecule?

A **molecule** is a tiny group of atoms joined together. Atoms are the small building blocks that make up ordinary matter. A molecule's arrangement matters, not just the number of atoms in it.

Imagine four keys cut from a similar blank. They may look related, but each can fit a different set of locks. This is an **analogy**, not a literal picture of how a drug attaches to a brain cell.

**Tryptamines** are a chemical family with a shared core structure. Several psychedelic compounds are in this family. Their similar names tell us something about their structure, but they do not guarantee the same effects or risks.

## 1. N,N-DMT: the chemical most people mean

**N,N-DMT** is short for *N,N-dimethyltryptamine*. It is a chemical found in some plants. It is also a classic psychedelic, meaning it can strongly alter perception and conscious experience.

In this article, **perception** means how the brain puts together what you see, hear, feel, and think. Reports of intense changes in perception are not measurements of what is happening at every single brain connection.

DMT interacts with the **serotonin system**. Serotonin is one of the brain's chemical messengers. A **receptor** is a spot on or in a cell that reacts when a particular chemical reaches it. Different receptors can produce different responses. [4]

## 2. 5-MeO-DMT: a small change with important effects

**5-MeO-DMT** has a *methoxy group* attached at a particular location on its chemical structure. A **methoxy group** is simply a small piece made of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen atoms.

5-MeO-DMT is not another spelling of N,N-DMT. Laboratory research finds that it can interact with serotonin-related receptors differently. A 2024 structural study gave scientists a closer look at its activity at a receptor called **5-HT1A**—another kind of serotonin signal receiver. [1,2]

People sometimes describe 5-MeO-DMT experiences differently from N,N-DMT experiences. Those stories can tell researchers what people report, but they cannot reliably tell us how a specific person will respond. Direct, carefully controlled human comparisons remain limited.

Importantly, combining some serotonin-active substances can cause dangerous interactions, including **serotonin toxicity**—a potentially serious reaction involving excessive serotonin-related effects. Chemical similarity is not evidence of safety. [1]

## 3. Bufotenine: also called 5-HO-DMT

**Bufotenine** has another chemical name: **5-HO-DMT**. The letters "HO" point to a *hydroxy group*, a small arrangement of oxygen and hydrogen atoms. Its position in the molecule helps distinguish it from other DMT relatives.

Bufotenine occurs in some plants, including species associated with traditional **yopo** practices in South America. It also activates certain serotonin receptors in laboratory research. [3,5]

One complication is the **blood–brain barrier**. Think of it as a selective border that controls which substances can travel from the blood into brain tissue. Research has suggested that bufotenine may cross this barrier differently from some related compounds. That is one reason a strong result in a receptor experiment may not translate into an equally strong effect in people. [3,5]

For the botany and history, see [Yopo: the seed, the science, and the history](/articles/yopo-bufotenine-science-history/).

## 4. Psilocin: also called 4-HO-DMT

**Psilocin** is **4-HO-DMT**. It has a hydroxy group in a different location from bufotenine.

The better-known word **psilocybin** refers to a substance found in certain mushrooms. Inside the body, psilocybin is converted into psilocin. Scientists call a substance that turns into an active compound a **prodrug**.

That distinction matters: a mushroom, psilocybin, and psilocin are related, but they are not identical labels for the same thing. Psilocin interacts with serotonin receptors involved in classic psychedelic effects. [4,5]

## What do the names actually tell you?

| Name | Plain-language difference | What not to assume |
| --- | --- | --- |
| N,N-DMT | The compound normally called DMT | That all DMT-like names mean this exact compound |
| 5-MeO-DMT | Has a methoxy group at position 5 | That a small change makes it safer |
| Bufotenine / 5-HO-DMT | Has a hydroxy group at position 5 | That receptor activity predicts human effects |
| Psilocin / 4-HO-DMT | Has a hydroxy group at position 4 | That it is identical to psilocybin |

**Position** means where a small group of atoms attaches to the shared chemical structure. It is a chemistry address, not a measure of strength.

## Why don't researchers just rank them from weakest to strongest?

Because "strongest" could mean several things. A compound may attach tightly to a receptor in a laboratory, produce a particular brain signal, cross into the brain more easily, or produce intense subjective effects. Those are **different measurements**.

A **subjective effect** is something a person experiences and reports, such as a change in the sense of time or self. Those reports deserve careful study, but they are not interchangeable with laboratory measurements.

The evidence also comes from different kinds of research: chemistry, cells, animal studies, and human studies. Researchers cannot convert one simple receptor result into a universal human potency or safety ranking. [1–5]

## Common questions

**Is 5-MeO-DMT just stronger DMT?** No. It has a different chemical structure and a different pattern of interaction with receptors. A single "stronger" label hides too many differences.

**Is psilocin technically related to DMT?** Yes. Its chemical name is 4-HO-DMT, but people generally use the everyday name psilocin.

**Is bufotenine the same as yopo?** No. Bufotenine is a molecule. Yopo refers to a plant-linked cultural preparation and traditions, not a purified single chemical.

**Can the molecule's name predict a safe experience?** No. Chemistry helps explain possible mechanisms, but human effects and risks depend on more than the molecule's family name.

## How to check the scientific sources

The numbers in square brackets, such as **[1]** and **[3]**, refer to the numbered studies in the References section below. A **PMID** is a paper's identifying number in **PubMed**, a public database of medical and life-science research. A **DOI** is a long-lasting identifier used to find a publication, even if its website address changes. Neither number tells you whether the study was well designed or whether a substance is safe.

For these four molecules, check **what researchers actually tested**. A chemical-structure study tells us how atoms are arranged. A receptor experiment measures how a chemical affects a cell's receiving spots. A study in people measures real human outcomes. These are useful but different kinds of evidence, so we should never turn a cell result into a promise about a person's experience. [1–5]

## Bottom line

The most interesting lesson is not that four molecules share part of a name. It's that **tiny changes in chemistry can lead to meaningful changes in biology**. The limits of that knowledge are equally important: related substances are not interchangeable, and laboratory effects do not automatically predict human experiences.
