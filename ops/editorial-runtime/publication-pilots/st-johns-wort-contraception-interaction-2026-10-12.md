> **Bottom line:** St. John's wort (*Hypericum perforatum*) can interact with hormonal birth control. In small human studies of combined oral contraceptives, some St. John's wort preparations increased hormone metabolism and breakthrough bleeding; one study also found signs of follicular development and probable ovulation. That creates a clinically meaningful concern about reduced contraceptive protection, although these experiments did **not** directly measure a reliable pregnancy-failure rate. A low-hyperforin extract produced no relevant pharmacokinetic interaction in one small study—but that does **not** establish contraceptive safety for all low-hyperforin products. [Hall 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14663455/) · [Murphy 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15914127/) · [NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/st-johns-wort)

## What did the human studies actually measure?

| Study | Study design | Finding | Important limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hall 2003 | 12 women using combined oral contraceptives; sequential treatment cycles | Norethindrone clearance increased, ethinyl estradiol half-life fell, breakthrough bleeding **7/12 vs 2/12** | Small sample and specific pill/extract exposure; not a pregnancy-outcome trial |
| Pfrunder 2003 | 18 women, randomized cycles, low-dose combined pill | Greater breakthrough bleeding and lower exposure to active desogestrel metabolite | No significant measured change in ovarian activity in that short study |
| Murphy 2005 | 16 women in sequential contraceptive cycles | Reduced contraceptive-hormone exposure, greater breakthrough bleeding, follicle growth and **probable ovulation** | Probable ovulation is not directly observed unplanned pregnancy |
| Will-Shahab 2009 | 16 women given **low-hyperforin Ze 117** | No clinically relevant pharmacokinetic loss for the **tested** combined pill and product | No demonstration of contraceptive effectiveness across all formulations |

The studies disagree on **ovarian activity**, but the interaction concern is not negated by a single study without observed ovulation. It is important to separate four different outcomes: hormone concentrations, breakthrough bleeding, evidence of ovulation, and **actual pregnancy**. They are related but not interchangeable.

## The Hall trial: drug levels and bleeding, not an observed failure rate

In 2003, Hall and colleagues enrolled 12 healthy premenopausal oral-contraceptive users in a pharmacokinetic study. With St. John's wort co-administration, oral clearance of **norethindrone increased from 8.2 to 9.5 L/h (p=.042)** and the **ethinyl estradiol half-life decreased from 23.4 to 12.2 hours (p=.023)**. Breakthrough bleeding occurred in **7 of 12 women** during co-use versus **2 of 12** in the control phase. Follicle-stimulating hormone, luteinizing hormone and progesterone were not significantly affected in the sampled windows. [PMID 14663455](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14663455/)

That is direct human evidence of altered hormone disposition and menstrual bleeding. It is not a measurement of how many people became pregnant, and the 7/12 figure must **not** be presented as a pregnancy rate. The differences were observed under a particular sequence, duration, contraceptive product and botanical exposure.

## Other human trials give necessary nuance

Pfrunder and colleagues studied **18 women** taking a low-dose combined contraceptive, using controlled treatment cycles with a St. John's wort extract. They reported more breakthrough bleeding and lower **3-ketodesogestrel** exposure; their measures of follicle maturation, estrogen and progesterone did not show a significant change. [PMID 14616430](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14616430/)

Murphy and colleagues studied **16 women** in a sequential design and observed lower hormonal exposure alongside increased breakthrough bleeding and evidence of follicular growth and **probable ovulation**. [PMID 15914127](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15914127/)

The latter study matters because a claim such as “no trials found ovulation signals” would be inaccurate. Nevertheless, ovarian observations from a few small studies cannot be converted into an accurate incidence of contraception failure in the general population.

A [2016 systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27444983/) examined four comparative studies. It noted that most showed increased bleeding, that studies differed on ovarian markers, and that some demonstrated altered contraceptive-hormone exposure. It re-examined overlapping primary experiments; it did **not** represent four fresh clinical replications.

## Why can a low-hyperforin extract seem different?

Hyperforin content and extract standardization can affect the magnitude of drug-metabolism induction. In a 2009 study, **16 women** took one named **low-hyperforin Ze 117** preparation for 14 days while using a low-dose combined pill. Its researchers did not observe a clinically significant reduction in exposure to the measured hormonal components. [PMID 19015839](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19015839/)

This is a valuable **product-specific null finding**, but it is not a contraceptive-failure or pregnancy-outcome trial. It cannot establish that every low-hyperforin product is comparable to Ze 117, that commercial labels accurately identify active constituents, or that all methods of hormonal contraception are safe to combine with St. John's wort. Nor can it overrule regulator warnings.

## What do regulators say?

The UK's [Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA)](https://www.gov.uk/drug-safety-update/st-john-s-wort-interaction-with-hormonal-contraceptives-including-implants) warns that St. John's wort can reduce the effectiveness of hormonal contraception, including some methods beyond the combined oral pill. The agency described spontaneous reports of suspected contraceptive interactions and unplanned pregnancies. Those reports warrant attention but **cannot** be treated as a controlled clinical failure rate: voluntary reporting lacks the denominator needed to calculate incidence.

The MHRA recommends that people using hormonal contraception for pregnancy prevention **not combine it with St. John's wort**. It separately notes that the warning's implications for intrauterine devices differ because those were not supported by the same data. A clinician or pharmacist should address specific contraceptive formulations, method choice and past exposure rather than relying on a generic herbal product label.

[NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/st-johns-wort) independently identifies birth-control pills among medications whose actions can be weakened by St. John's wort. The same ingredient can alter other medicines; the presence or absence of contraceptive interaction is not an all-purpose medication-safety evaluation.

## Why a “natural” label is not a compatibility certificate

St. John's wort is sold as capsules, teas, extracts and mixed botanical products. Many reports simply label them “St. John's wort” without enough details about constituent content, co-ingredients or independent assay verification. Human evidence from an extract standardized in one study does not necessarily transfer to a different formulation or delivery route.

The proposed mechanism for reduced hormonal exposure is the induction of drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters, including **CYP3A** pathways. That helps explain the pharmacokinetic results, but it is not a direct measurement of pregnancy protection for a specific product. Similarly, timing and washout questions require medication-specific guidance rather than assuming an instant reversal of enzyme induction.

## Questions readers commonly ask

### Did the studies prove St. John's wort causes unplanned pregnancy?

No randomized study cited here measured a reliable population pregnancy-failure rate. The clinical concern rests on human hormonal-exposure, bleeding and possible ovarian-activity findings, supported by regulatory reports of suspected contraceptive failures. That is enough to justify serious interaction warnings without inventing a percentage probability.

### Does breakthrough bleeding prove contraception has failed?

No. Bleeding is a signal that may accompany changed hormonal exposure, but **bleeding alone neither proves ovulation nor confirms or excludes pregnancy**. Interpreting a specific event requires individualized clinical assessment.

### What if a particular “low-hyperforin” brand says it does not interact?

One small study of Ze 117 was reassuring for a measured pharmacokinetic outcome with one combined pill. That should not be generalized to all brands, strengths, pregnancy outcomes or different hormonal methods.

### Are implants and progestogen-only contraception affected?

Regulatory advice extends beyond the exact combined pills tested in several early trials. The clinical exposure and evidence quality can differ by method, so a pharmacist, contraceptive prescriber or specialist service is the appropriate source for method-specific guidance.

### Should a person stop an antidepressant or birth control on their own?

This evidence does not authorize changing prescribed treatment or relying on a supplement as a substitute. Medication changes, contraception planning, and assessment after an exposure should be addressed with an appropriate licensed professional.

## How this question should be represented in an evidence database

- **Question:** interaction between *H. perforatum* and hormonal contraceptive exposure/effectiveness.
- **Human PK evidence:** positive in Hall and other studies, with exact formulation and menstrual-cycle timing.
- **Clinical signals:** breakthrough bleeding and probable-ovulation evidence in different small trials.
- **Pregnancy outcome:** not estimated from these randomized trials; spontaneous regulator reports are **surveillance**, not controlled incidence data.
- **Contrary evidence:** one low-hyperforin product's 14-day PK null, **not proof of general safety**.
- **Most important editorial caution:** never transform a pharmacokinetic or bleeding result into an invented risk of pregnancy, or the low-hyperforin null into guaranteed contraceptive protection.
