# 2F-DCK: Poisoning Cases, Dissociation & Safety Evidence

## Quick answer

**2F-DCK (2-fluorodeschloroketamine) is a ketamine-related arylcyclohexylamine dissociative.**

Unlike many newer RC dissociatives, it has an analytically confirmed human poisoning series.

That evidence shows that severe intoxication can involve impaired consciousness, agitation, abnormal behavior, hypertension, tachycardia, convulsions, and loss of consciousness.

## What the 20-case series found

Researchers identified 20 confirmed 2F-DCK exposures in 2019.

The dominant clinical effects were:

- impaired consciousness;
- agitation;
- abnormal behavior;
- hypertension;
- tachycardia.

Five patients had either loss of consciousness or convulsion.

The crucial limitation: **19 of 20 patients also had another ketamine-type drug detected.**

So the study confirms that 2F-DCK occurs in clinically significant intoxications, but it cannot cleanly assign every symptom to 2F-DCK alone.

## Is it “basically ketamine”?

Chemically and pharmacologically, 2F-DCK is clearly related to ketamine.

That does not mean:

- equal potency;
- equal duration;
- identical metabolites;
- identical toxicity;
- a valid milligram-for-milligram conversion.

Analogue relationships are useful for understanding the drug family, not for creating a dosing chart.

## Fatal and severe forensic reports

Later forensic literature described two deaths and a severe self-injury case involving 2F-DCK.

As with many NPS deaths, co-exposures and case circumstances matter.

The appropriate conclusion is that severe and fatal outcomes are possible—not that every detected concentration has the same meaning.

## Dissociation creates indirect risks

Even when respiratory or cardiovascular toxicity is not the dominant problem, dissociation can create danger through:

- falls;
- traffic exposure;
- drowning;
- self-injury;
- poor pain awareness;
- confusion;
- inability to recognize deteriorating symptoms.


## Why 2F-DCK has more evidence than many RC dissociatives

2F-DCK is unusual in this category because published literature includes a multi-patient clinical series rather than only receptor assays or anonymous experience reports. Analytically confirmed cases give clinicians a clearer view of the kinds of presentations that can occur in real people.

Even so, a clinical case series has important limits. Patients who reach medical attention are not a random sample of everyone exposed, and co-use or uncertain product composition can complicate interpretation. The literature therefore helps define **possible toxicity**, not a safe recreational range.

Later forensic deaths and severe cases strengthen the reason for caution without creating a simple blood-concentration threshold that applies to every person.

## Ketamine similarity has limits

The “ketamine analogue” label is chemically useful but clinically incomplete.

Fluorine substitution can alter metabolism, elimination, receptor interactions, and the relationship between subjective effects and blood concentration. A person familiar with ketamine therefore cannot assume that timing or impairment will transfer cleanly to 2F-DCK.

The same caution applies to urinary and bladder toxicity. Chronic ketamine exposure can damage the urinary tract, but the long-term human risk from repeated 2F-DCK exposure has not been quantified well enough to claim an identical rate.

## What severe intoxication can look like

Published reports of RC dissociatives describe loss of consciousness, marked confusion, agitation, abnormal behavior, seizures, hypertension, and injuries that occur while judgment and pain perception are impaired.

A person who is unconscious, having a seizure, severely agitated, or unable to protect their airway needs medical assessment. If breathing is abnormally slow or opioid exposure is possible, naloxone is reasonable while emergency services are contacted.

The most useful safety message is not a potency comparison. It is that **an RC dissociative can become medically serious before the user can reliably judge how impaired they are**.

## Bottom line

2F-DCK has more real-world human toxicology than many RC dissociatives.

That evidence does not justify treating it as a known-dose ketamine substitute. It supports the opposite lesson: **analogue similarity does not erase substantial uncertainty about exposure, combinations, and severe toxicity.**
