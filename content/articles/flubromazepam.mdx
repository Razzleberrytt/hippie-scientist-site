# Flubromazepam: Complete Evidence, Long Persistence, Dependence, Withdrawal & Safety Monograph

> **Emergency safety:** Inability to wake, abnormal or slow breathing, blue/gray lips, collapse, seizure, aspiration or severe confusion after flubromazepam or an unknown sedative requires emergency care. **Naloxone reverses opioids, not flubromazepam**, but should be used when opioid exposure is possible.

## Quick answer

**Flubromazepam is a designer 1,4-benzodiazepine whose defining evidence-based feature is unusually prolonged persistence.**

A 2013 human self-experiment estimated an elimination half-life **greater than 100 hours** in that participant and detected a major urinary metabolite for up to **28 days**.

That is unusually informative for an RC benzodiazepine, but it is still a one-person pharmacokinetic estimate—not a universal half-life.

A later fatal mixed intoxication involving the opioid U-47700 produced serial blood measurements consistent with roughly 100-hour flubromazepam elimination, strengthening the conclusion that the drug can remain clinically relevant for days.

## Identity

**Canonical name:** Flubromazepam  
**CAS:** 2647-50-9  
**Class:** 1,4-benzodiazepine / designer benzodiazepine  
**Molecular formula:** C15H10BrFN2O  
**Relationship:** fluorinated/brominated benzodiazepine analogue; distinct from flubromazolam  
**Approved U.S. medical use:** none established  
**Evidence grade:** low-to-moderate

UNODC classifies flubromazepam within the benzodiazepine/sedative-hypnotic NPS group.

## History and market emergence

Flubromazepam appeared in internet “research chemical” markets in the early 2010s, shortly after pyrazolam helped establish the modern designer-benzodiazepine market.

Its emergence was driven partly by a recurring regulatory cycle: when familiar benzodiazepines were controlled, poorly characterized analogues moved into the market.

Because flubromazepam had limited prior human use, early toxicology work focused on **identification, metabolism and detectability**.

---

## Flubromazepam is not flubromazolam

The names are dangerously easy to confuse.

**Flubromazepam** is a 1,4-benzodiazepine analogue with evidence for very slow elimination.

**Flubromazolam** is a triazolobenzodiazepine with a different structure, potency profile and regulatory status.

No conversion between the two should be inferred from their names.

---

## Pharmacodynamics

Flubromazepam is expected to act as a benzodiazepine-site **positive allosteric modulator at GABA-A receptors**.

Class-consistent effects include:

- sedation;
- anxiolysis;
- psychomotor slowing;
- impaired coordination;
- amnesia;
- muscle relaxation;
- reduced consciousness at high exposure.

Direct human receptor-response data are sparse, so the strongest molecule-specific evidence is pharmacokinetic and analytical rather than receptor-subtype clinical pharmacology.

---

## Metabolism and pharmacokinetics: why persistence dominates the risk profile

### The 2013 human study

A controlled self-experiment collected serum and urine for weeks after a single exposure.

The study estimated a terminal elimination half-life of **more than 100 hours** in that participant.

It also identified:

- a predominant monohydroxylated metabolite;
- a debrominated metabolite;
- urinary detectability of the major metabolite for up to 28 days in that case.

### A later clinical case supported prolonged elimination

In a fatal U-47700/flubromazepam intoxication, serial hospital samples showed flubromazepam concentrations declining very slowly over several days. The derived curve supported the earlier ~100-hour persistence estimate.

### What this does not mean

It does **not** mean:

- everyone has the same half-life;
- impairment lasts exactly a fixed number of days;
- urine detection equals ongoing intoxication;
- waiting one half-life makes a later depressant combination safe.

The robust conclusion is simply: **persistence can dramatically outlast subjective peak effects.**

---

## Accumulation

Long elimination means repeated exposure can stack across days.

A person can feel less sedated while still carrying a large residual body burden.

That can increase:

- next-day or multi-day psychomotor impairment;
- unexpected oversedation after repeated use;
- interaction risk when alcohol/opioids are used later;
- falls or driving risk;
- delayed recognition of dependence.

---

## Human toxicity

### Poison-center evidence

U.S. poison-center surveillance documented exposures to designer benzodiazepines including flubromazepam.

These datasets are valuable for showing clinically important exposure, but individual compounds may have small counts and co-exposure can complicate interpretation.

### Mixed opioid fatality

The U-47700 case is especially important because it illustrates a realistic hazard: a short(er)-acting synthetic opioid combined with a benzodiazepine that remains for days.

The fatal outcome cannot be reduced to one drug alone. It is a **mixed-depressant toxicity** case with strong evidence that flubromazepam contributed prolonged CNS depressant burden.

---

## Acute toxicity

Expected benzodiazepine-type toxicity includes:

- drowsiness;
- ataxia;
- slurred speech;
- amnesia;
- poor judgment;
- confusion;
- stupor or coma.

The special hazard is that toxicity may be **prolonged**, and later sedatives may overlap with residual drug.

---

## Interactions

### Opioids

Opioids suppress breathing; flubromazepam adds prolonged sedation and reduced arousal.

Naloxone treats the opioid component only.

### Alcohol

Alcohol can worsen sedation, memory impairment, falls and aspiration.

### Other benzodiazepines and sedatives

Adding a second sedative when flubromazepam remains in the body can create more total depressant exposure than the person realizes.

---

## Tolerance and physical dependence

Repeated benzodiazepine exposure can produce tolerance and physical dependence.

With a very long-persisting drug, dependence can be difficult to recognize because:

- interdose withdrawal may be less obvious;
- residual drug can mask early symptoms;
- withdrawal may not appear immediately after stopping.

Tolerance is not protection from polysubstance overdose.

---

## Withdrawal

There is no prospective flubromazepam withdrawal trial and no validated flubromazepam-specific taper schedule.

Benzodiazepine withdrawal can include:

- rebound anxiety;
- insomnia;
- tremor;
- autonomic symptoms;
- perceptual disturbance;
- confusion;
- seizures;
- delirium.

### Why withdrawal may be delayed

If the parent drug declines slowly, the nervous system may remain exposed for a prolonged period before concentrations fall enough to trigger obvious withdrawal.

That is why “I stopped yesterday and feel okay” is not a reliable test for absence of dependence.

---

## Dependence vs use disorder

Physical dependence is adaptation to repeated exposure.

A benzodiazepine/sedative use disorder involves a broader pattern such as:

- inability to cut down;
- craving;
- escalating use;
- continued use despite harm;
- hazardous behavior;
- disruption of work or relationships.

Both can coexist, but they are not identical.

---

## Treatment and recovery support

There is no evidence-based DIY flubromazepam conversion or home taper protocol.

When dependence is likely, clinicians should use established benzodiazepine-withdrawal principles and account for the compound's possible prolonged persistence.

Urgent assessment is warranted for:

- seizure;
- delirium;
- severe confusion;
- respiratory compromise;
- major opioid/alcohol co-exposure.

## Drug testing and detection

The 2013 study tested immunoassays and found that cross-reactivity was **not sufficient for reliable detection** at the studied exposure.

This is a high-value practical point: routine benzodiazepine screens can fail even when a benzodiazepine-type drug was taken.

Definitive approaches include:

- LC-MS/MS;
- LC-QTOF/HRMS;
- metabolite-aware targeted analysis.

A negative routine screen is not proof of absence.

---

## Forensic interpretation

Long persistence complicates toxicology.

A positive finding may reflect exposure days earlier. Conversely, prolonged residual drug can still contribute to impairment or mixed toxicity.

Interpretation should consider:

- parent/metabolite concentrations;
- specimen timing;
- repeated use;
- co-detected opioids/alcohol/sedatives;
- tolerance;
- clinical course.

No single concentration from a fatality defines a universal lethal level.

---

## Counterfeit and product-identity risk

An RC-labelled powder or tablet is not pharmaceutical confirmation.

Potential problems include:

- wrong benzodiazepine;
- multiple active sedatives;
- inaccurate concentration;
- contamination;
- incomplete certificates of analysis.

With a long-persisting drug, a mislabeled high-strength product can create risk that continues well after the initial exposure.

---

## Legal and regulatory context

UNODC maintains flubromazepam in its NPS Early Warning Advisory system.

At the WHO 46th Expert Committee on Drug Dependence review in 2023, flubromazepam was **recommended for continued surveillance rather than international scheduling** at that time.

National and state laws vary and can change independently of WHO scheduling.

As of October 3, 2026, this article does not treat the absence of a named U.S. federal scheduling action as a blanket statement of legality; state and other legal controls may apply.

---

## Special populations

Direct flubromazepam data are extremely limited for:

- pregnancy/breastfeeding;
- older adults;
- liver/kidney disease;
- respiratory disease;
- adolescents.

Long persistence may be especially concerning when drug clearance is reduced or when fall/respiratory risk is already high.

---

## Myths vs evidence

### “The strongest effects are gone, so the drug is gone.”

**False.** Persistence can outlast subjective intoxication by days.

### “A long-acting benzo is automatically safer.”

**False.** Slow elimination can reduce fluctuation but increases accumulation and delayed interaction risk.

### “A negative benzo screen rules it out.”

**False.** Published immunoassay testing showed detection limitations.

### “Flubromazepam and flubromazolam are basically the same.”

**False.** They are distinct drugs.

### “No symptoms the next day means no dependence.”

**False.** Long elimination can delay withdrawal.

## Major evidence gaps

Major gaps include:

- population pharmacokinetics;
- controlled dose-response data;
- isolated-overdose cohorts;
- precise impairment duration;
- pregnancy/breastfeeding outcomes;
- frequency of dependence;
- withdrawal timing and duration;
- current illicit-market prevalence;
- current product concentration variability.

## Bottom line

Flubromazepam's defining risk is **persistence**.

The best human evidence shows that the drug and its metabolites can remain for an unusually long time, creating a safety profile dominated by **accumulation, delayed interaction risk, prolonged impairment and delayed withdrawal**.

The literature is good enough to establish that pattern, but not good enough to justify a universal half-life, self-taper conversion, or recreational safety threshold.
