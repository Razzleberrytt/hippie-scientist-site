> **Use this page as a map, not a shopping list.** Sleep interventions only make sense after the bottleneck is defined. Chronic insomnia, a delayed circadian clock, insufficient sleep opportunity, shift work, sleep apnea, restless legs, environmental disruption, and substance-related sleep problems can all produce “I sleep badly” while requiring very different solutions.

## How to read the matrix

The **evidence position** below is deliberately broad. It is not a universal medical grade and should not be compared mechanically across completely different outcomes.

A “moderate” signal for subjective sleep quality is not automatically stronger than a “limited” signal for an objective endpoint. Likewise, an intervention can have strong evidence for **one indication** and weak evidence for another.

The most useful columns are:

- **Best-supported role:** what the intervention is actually good at answering.
- **Main evidence signal:** the outcome most consistently supported in human research.
- **Biggest limitation:** the reason not to turn the result into a universal claim.

## Chronic insomnia and disorder-level interventions

| Intervention | Evidence position | Best-supported role | Main signal | Biggest limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [CBT-I](/articles/cbt-i-vs-sleep-supplements/) | **Strong for chronic insomnia** | First-line chronic insomnia treatment | Insomnia symptoms and sleep-related functioning | Access, adherence, and implementation vary; sleep hygiene alone is not CBT-I |
| [Sleep apnea evaluation/treatment](/articles/sleep-apnea-vs-insomnia/) | **Strong when OSA is present** | Treat airway obstruction and sleep fragmentation | Disorder-specific respiratory and sleep outcomes | Sedation and supplements do not replace airway treatment |
| [Restless legs + iron evaluation](/articles/restless-legs-iron-and-sleep/) | **Guideline-supported in RLS** | Identify iron-related treatment pathway | RLS symptom management | Iron is not a generic sleep aid; ferritin/transferrin context matters |
| [Delayed sleep phase framework](/articles/delayed-sleep-wake-phase-vs-insomnia/) | **Moderate circadian framework** | Shift a delayed biological clock | Circadian phase and sleep timing | Timing of light/melatonin matters; not every night owl has a disorder |
| [Shift work sleep disorder](/articles/shift-work-sleep-disorder/) | **Moderate by intervention** | Manage work-linked circadian misalignment and sleepiness | Alertness, sleep opportunity, timing | Fixed and rotating schedules need different strategies |
| [Mouth taping](/articles/mouth-taping-for-sleep/) | **Limited / safety-sensitive** | Narrow selected airway contexts | Some apnea/snoring signals in selected patients | Viral claims outrun evidence; nasal obstruction and undiagnosed OSA matter |

| Intervention | Evidence position | Best-supported role | Main signal | Biggest limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Melatonin timing](/articles/melatonin-timing-vs-dose/) | **Moderate; strongest for circadian timing** | Circadian phase / sleep timing | Sleep onset and phase shifting | Dose, timing, indication, and formulation are not interchangeable |
| [Magnesium](/guides/sleep/magnesium-for-sleep/) | **Limited** | Selected poor-sleep or low-status contexts | Small insomnia/sleep-quality signals | Small, heterogeneous trials; no universal best form |
| [L-theanine](/guides/sleep/l-theanine-for-sleep/) | **Limited to promising** | Subjective sleep quality / pre-sleep calming | Questionnaire-based sleep outcomes | Not established as chronic-insomnia treatment |
| [Ashwagandha](/guides/sleep/ashwagandha-for-sleep/) | **Limited positive pooled signal** | Stress-related poor sleep over weeks | Sleep-quality / insomnia-scale outcomes | Extract-specific, short-term studies; not a same-night sedative |
| [Saffron](/articles/saffron-for-sleep/) | **Moderate short-term** | Poor sleep / insomnia symptoms | PSQI, ISI and related subjective outcomes | Product-specific, mostly short trials; modest practical effects |

## Environmental and behavioral tools

| Intervention | Evidence position | Best-supported role | Main signal | Biggest limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Warm bath or shower](/articles/warm-bath-shower-before-bed/) | **Moderate for sleep onset** | Pre-sleep thermoregulation | Shorter sleep-onset latency; better efficiency/quality | Whole-body heating evidence does not transfer to every heat ritual |
| [Bedroom temperature / cooling](/articles/sleep-temperature-and-cooling/) | **Strong heat-disruption signal; weak product signal** | Avoid excess thermal load | Hot environments worsen sleep | Cooling-bedding trials do not show a universal product benefit |
| [Eye masks + earplugs](/articles/eye-masks-earplugs-and-sleep/) | **Moderate in bright/noisy clinical settings** | Reduce environmental light/noise | Subjective sleep quality | Evidence is ICU-heavy; earplugs alone are less consistent |
| [White noise](/articles/white-noise-and-sleep/) | **Mixed to moderate in selected settings** | Mask unpredictable environmental noise | Subjective sleep quality in newer RCT meta-analysis | Older review was very low certainty; setting and sound level matter |
| [Music](/articles/music-for-sleep/) | **Moderate subjective signal** | Bedtime relaxation / subjective sleep quality | PSQI improvement | Objective sleep changes are much weaker; no proven magic frequency |
| [Weighted blankets](/articles/weighted-blankets-for-sleep/) | **Limited to promising in adults** | Comfort / subjective insomnia symptoms | PSQI improvement in adult pilot RCT | Objective changes weaker; pooled estimates remain unstable |
| [Mindfulness](/articles/mindfulness-for-insomnia/) | **Mixed / adjunctive** | Arousal and stress management | Better outcomes versus inactive controls | Advantages shrink against active controls or CBT-I components |
| [Sleep regularity](/articles/sleep-regularity-health/) | **Strong observational signal** | Stabilize sleep timing | Health/sleep associations | Most long-term evidence is observational, not causal |
| [Exercise timing](/articles/exercise-timing-and-sleep/) | **Moderate exercise benefit; timing nuance** | General sleep support | Broad sleep-quality benefit | No universal morning advantage; late intense exercise may differ |
| [Naps](/articles/naps-and-nighttime-sleep/) | **Context-dependent** | Acute alertness / shift work | Cognition and sleepiness | Long/late naps can reduce nighttime sleep pressure; sleep inertia matters |

## Circadian timing and light

| Intervention | Evidence position | Best-supported role | Main signal | Biggest limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Morning light](/articles/morning-light-and-sleep-timing/) | **Moderate timing evidence** | Advance circadian phase | Earlier sleep timing / insomnia-scale changes | Wrong-time light can shift the clock in the opposite direction |
| [Blue-light reduction](/articles/blue-light-screens-and-sleep/) | **Mechanism strong; intervention evidence mixed** | Reduce unnecessary bright evening light | Circadian/melatonin effects; mixed sleep outcomes | Screens also affect sleep through arousal and delayed bedtime |
| [Weekend catch-up sleep](/articles/weekend-catch-up-sleep/) | **Partial-recovery evidence** | Recover some acute sleep loss | Fatigue/mood/cognitive recovery | Does not fully erase chronic sleep debt; large shifts add social jet lag |
| [Time-restricted eating](/articles/time-restricted-eating-and-sleep/) | **Limited / inconsistent** | Chrononutrition research question | Mixed within-group sleep changes | Controlled trials do not establish a dependable sleep benefit |

## Substances and OTC sleep products

| Exposure | Evidence position | Main sleep effect | Why the obvious interpretation fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Caffeine](/articles/caffeine-and-sleep-timing/) | **Strong sleep-disruption evidence** | Longer latency, shorter/poorer sleep depending on dose/timing | “I can fall asleep after coffee” does not mean sleep is unaffected |
| [Alcohol](/articles/alcohol-and-sleep/) | **Strong architecture-disruption signal** | Can shorten initial latency while disrupting REM and later sleep | Sedation is not restorative sleep |
| [Nicotine / vaping](/articles/nicotine-vaping-and-sleep/) | **Consistent disruption association** | Sleep fragmentation, timing and withdrawal effects | Temporary withdrawal insomnia does not mean nicotine improves sleep |
| [Cannabis / cannabinoids](/articles/cannabis-cannabinoids-and-sleep/) | **Mixed and formulation-dependent** | Some insomnia signals, inconsistent objective architecture | THC/CBD ratios, dose, tolerance, withdrawal and product composition differ |
| [Diphenhydramine / doxylamine](/articles/otc-antihistamines-for-sleep/) | **Limited chronic-insomnia value** | Acute sedation | Tolerance, anticholinergic burden, next-day effects and older-adult risk matter |

## The most important decision rule

A supplement question should come **after** the mechanism question.

If a person has chronic insomnia, CBT-I has a stronger evidence base than supplement escalation. If sleep is normal only on a much later schedule, circadian timing may be the real issue. If there is loud snoring, choking, or witnessed breathing pauses, an airway problem belongs higher in the decision tree. If the person simply has only five hours available for sleep, no compound can manufacture the missing time.

Likewise, if the bedroom is hot, bright, or noisy, correcting the environment can be a cleaner experiment than adding another ingredient.

## Bottom line

The sleep evidence becomes much less confusing when every intervention is forced to answer five questions:

1. **What problem is it supposed to solve?**
2. **Which endpoint actually improved?**
3. **Was the result subjective, objective, or both?**
4. **How directly does the study match the product or situation being considered?**
5. **What important outcome failed to improve or what safety boundary changes the decision?**

That is the purpose of this matrix. It is not a ranking of what to buy. It is a map of **what the evidence can and cannot currently support**.
