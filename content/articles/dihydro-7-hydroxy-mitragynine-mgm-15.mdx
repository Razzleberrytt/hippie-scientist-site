# MGM-15 (Dihydro-7-Hydroxymitragynine): Complete Pharmacology, Dependence, Withdrawal & Safety Monograph

> **Emergency safety:** MGM-15 is an opioid-active semi-synthetic compound with no established safe human dose. If someone is difficult or impossible to wake, has slow or irregular breathing, has blue/gray lips, is choking/gurgling while unresponsive, or collapses after a product that may contain MGM-15, call emergency services and give naloxone if available.

## Quick answer

**MGM-15 is dihydro-7-hydroxymitragynine, a semi-synthetic derivative of 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH).**

It is not traditional kratom leaf, not mitragynine, and not 7-OH itself.

The scientific evidence is unusually lopsided:

- **chemistry and receptor pharmacology:** meaningful evidence;
- **commercial product confirmation:** meaningful evidence;
- **forensic detection:** emerging evidence;
- **controlled human pharmacokinetics:** essentially absent;
- **controlled human safety:** absent;
- **human dependence/withdrawal studies:** absent;
- **long-term human outcomes:** absent.

That mismatch is precisely why MGM-15 deserves a serious harm-reduction monograph.

The compound entered the consumer market before clinical science caught up.

## Identity: what MGM-15 actually is

**Common names:**

- MGM-15
- MGM15
- dihydro-7-hydroxymitragynine
- dihydro-7-hydroxy mitragynine
- DH-7OH-MIT
- DHM in some product/market contexts

**Chemical class:** semi-synthetic indole/kratom-alkaloid derivative with opioid activity

**CAS number:** 1158901-38-2

**Reported molecular formula:** C23H32N2O5

**Reported molecular mass:** about 416.5 g/mol

**Relationship to 7-OH:** a dihydro derivative produced by stereospecific saturation of a double bond in the 7-OH scaffold.

CFSRE describes MGM-15 as a synthetic derivative of 7-hydroxymitragynine and has documented it in drug materials and toxicology casework. [CFSRE monograph](https://www.cfsre.org/nps-discovery/monographs/dihydro-7-hydroxy-mitragynine)

---

## MGM-15 is not traditional kratom

Traditional kratom leaf is a complex botanical material dominated by **mitragynine** and containing many additional alkaloids.

MGM-15 is a chemically modified derivative encountered as a concentrated/semi-synthetic product.

Those are different exposure categories.

| Feature | Traditional kratom leaf | MGM-15 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Origin | Botanical leaf | Semi-synthetic derivative |
| Main alkaloid context | Mitragynine-dominant mixture | MGM-15-focused commercial/RC product |
| Human research | Some human kratom/mitragynine studies exist | No controlled MGM-15 clinical program |
| Standardization | Variable botanical | Variable unregulated novel product |
| Legal status | Jurisdiction-dependent and distinct | U.S. federal Schedule I as of Aug. 26, 2026 |

Evidence from traditional kratom cannot simply be transferred to MGM-15.

## History: from medicinal chemistry to smoke-shop / RC market

MGM-15 originated in medicinal-chemistry efforts to modify 7-hydroxymitragynine and explore opioid analgesic activity.

Research on MGM-15 and the related fluorinated analogue MGM-16 appears in preclinical opioid/pain literature well before their modern retail emergence. A 2014 paper evaluated MGM-15 and MGM-16 as mu/delta opioid agonist derivatives in receptor assays and mouse pain models. [PMID 24345467](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24345467/)

For years, this chemistry remained primarily a research topic.

That changed in the mid-2020s.

### 2025–2026 market emergence

CFSRE first identified dihydro-7-hydroxymitragynine in modern forensic material in 2025 and subsequently issued a public alert.

A peer-reviewed 2025 study independently analyzed commercially sold MGM-labeled tablets and confirmed MGM-15 using quantitative analytical methods. [PMID 40936282](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40936282/)

By July 2026, Utah authorities reported MGM-15 in **Buzzers-branded Cat’s Claw tablets** associated with overdose reports and a death investigation. [UDAF](https://ag.utah.gov/news/illegal-kratom-product-sold-in-utah-county-linked-to-death-in-utah/)

That sequence matters:

> MGM-15 moved from medicinal chemistry → online/retail novel opioid product → forensic/public-health concern before controlled human research existed.

---

## Chemistry: what the modification changes

MGM-15 is produced conceptually from the 7-OH scaffold by reducing/saturating a specific imine double bond.

That apparently modest structural change alters opioid-receptor binding.

The 2025 MGM-15 paper found **greater human mu- and delta-opioid receptor binding affinity than 7-OH** in the assays used. [PMID 40936282](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40936282/)

This is important pharmacology.

It is **not** a human potency conversion.

---

## Pharmacodynamics

### Mu-opioid receptor

Mu-opioid receptor (MOR) activation is the most clinically important pathway for classic opioid effects such as:

- analgesia;
- euphoria;
- sedation;
- miosis;
- respiratory depression;
- tolerance;
- physical dependence.

Preclinical evidence supports meaningful MOR activity for MGM-15.

### Delta-opioid receptor

MGM-15 also shows delta-opioid receptor activity in preclinical assays.

The clinical importance of that delta activity in humans is not established.

Community discussions sometimes claim that delta activity explains a uniquely prolonged or severe withdrawal syndrome.

That is **not proven human pharmacology**.

The receptor finding is real; the withdrawal interpretation is speculative.

### Why efficacy and affinity matter

A drug can bind strongly to a receptor without producing the exact same functional response as another ligand.

Different studies may measure:

- binding affinity;
- G-protein signaling;
- beta-arrestin signaling;
- tissue response;
- animal antinociception.

These are related but not interchangeable outcomes.

That is why this site avoids ranking MGM-15, 7-OH, pseudoindoxyl and prescription opioids using one headline number.

---

## Is MGM-15 stronger than 7-OH?

### Laboratory answer

In the 2025 commercial-product study, purified MGM-15 showed higher binding affinity at human mu- and delta-opioid receptors than 7-OH in the tested assays. [PMID 40936282](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40936282/)

### Human answer

**Unknown.**

There is no controlled human study establishing:

- milligram-for-milligram subjective potency;
- respiratory-depression potency;
- analgesic potency;
- overdose threshold;
- dependence liability;
- duration;
- safe dose.

So the evidence-based answer is:

> MGM-15 appears highly opioid-active preclinically, but there is no validated human potency ratio versus 7-OH, morphine, oxycodone, fentanyl or another opioid.

---

## Commercial product evidence

A major strength of the MGM-15 evidence base is that it is no longer limited to a reference compound in a laboratory.

A 2025 study purchased commercially available MGM-labeled tablets, chemically analyzed them, confirmed MGM-15 as the principal targeted compound, and then evaluated purified MGM-15 at opioid receptors. [PMID 40936282](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40936282/)

CFSRE has separately documented MGM-15 in drug materials and toxicology casework.

### Why this matters

The question is no longer “could MGM-15 theoretically exist?”

It is:

> What happens when a potent, scarcely studied opioid derivative is sold to humans before human safety data exist?

That is the central public-health problem.

## MGM-15 in Buzzers Cat’s Claw

Utah authorities reported in July 2026 that tested **Buzzers Cat’s Claw tablets contained MGM-15 and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl**.

The products were associated with severe health events and a death under investigation.

This is especially important because Buzzers was presented as a botanical Cat’s Claw product, not as a research-chemical opioid.

See the [Buzzers hidden-opioid investigation](/articles/buzzers-cats-claw-hidden-opioids/).

This shows that MGM-15 exposure can be **unintentional or poorly informed**.

---

## Human pharmacokinetics: the biggest evidence hole

No controlled human MGM-15 pharmacokinetic study has established:

- oral bioavailability;
- time to peak concentration;
- human half-life;
- active metabolites;
- clearance;
- accumulation;
- CYP/UGT pathways in vivo;
- differences by liver/kidney function;
- exposure-response relationships.

### What not to do with community reports

Online communities often quote specific MGM-15 durations or half-lives.

Those figures should not be presented as measured human pharmacokinetics unless a traceable primary study exists.

At present, community timing reports are **anecdotal**.

---

## Acute toxicity and overdose

Direct MGM-15-only human overdose case series are not yet available.

That does not mean overdose risk is absent.

The concern comes from:

- strong opioid-receptor pharmacology;
- commercial exposure;
- forensic detection;
- public-health overdose reports involving MGM-15-containing products;
- biological plausibility of MOR-mediated respiratory depression.

### Emergency warning signs

Possible opioid toxicity includes:

- severe sleepiness;
- inability to wake;
- slow, shallow or irregular breathing;
- blue/gray lips;
- collapse;
- choking, snoring or gurgling while unresponsive;
- pinpoint pupils.

If severe opioid toxicity is possible, call emergency services.

---

## Naloxone

There is no controlled human trial specifically measuring naloxone reversal of isolated MGM-15 poisoning.

But MGM-15 is a MOR-active opioid derivative, and Utah Poison Control recommended naloxone for suspected overdoses involving products found to contain MGM-15/pseudoindoxyl.

Naloxone is therefore appropriate when an MGM-15-containing opioid product is suspected and the person has opioid-overdose signs.

Emergency care remains necessary.

---

## Interactions

MGM-15-specific interaction trials do not exist.

From opioid pharmacology, the most concerning combinations are other **central nervous system depressants**, particularly:

- other opioids;
- benzodiazepines;
- alcohol;
- sedative-hypnotics;
- other unknown smoke-shop or RC products.

The risk is not merely additive “drowsiness.”

Multiple depressants can make respiratory compromise more likely and harder to recognize.

This page does not provide combination-use guidance.

---

## Tolerance

Direct human MGM-15 tolerance studies have not been published.

Repeated MOR agonist exposure generally produces tolerance.

For MGM-15, tolerance should therefore be considered **biologically plausible and likely**, but the speed and magnitude are not quantified.

Community reports frequently describe escalating use.

Those reports can identify a safety concern but cannot define a population incidence or timeline.

---

## Physical dependence

### What is directly established?

There is **no published prospective MGM-15 dependence cohort** and no clean MGM-15-only withdrawal series.

### What is strongly plausible?

Repeated exposure to a potent MOR-active opioid can produce neuroadaptation and physical dependence.

The broader concentrated 7-OH/pseudoindoxyl market now has direct human dependence and withdrawal evidence. [PMID 41982589](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41982589/)

That evidence supports concern.

It does not allow us to simply copy the pseudoindoxyl withdrawal timeline onto MGM-15.

---

## Withdrawal: what is known and what is not

### Community signal

Online forums contain many reports of opioid-like withdrawal after frequent MGM-15 use.

Commonly described symptoms include:

- restlessness;
- chills/sweating;
- insomnia;
- gastrointestinal distress;
- muscle aches;
- anxiety;
- craving.

These reports are consistent with opioid dependence.

They remain **anecdotal**.

### Not established

There is no validated MGM-15-specific evidence for:

- typical withdrawal onset;
- peak day;
- total duration;
- severity versus 7-OH;
- severity versus prescription opioids;
- a specific taper rate;
- a specific medication induction timeline.

### Why a DIY product taper is especially unreliable

Unregulated MGM-15 products may vary in:

- actual concentration;
- co-ingredients;
- batch consistency;
- formulation;
- chemical identity.

A taper that assumes the front-label amount is exact may therefore be built on a false premise.

---

## Dependence vs opioid use disorder

Physical dependence means withdrawal develops after repeated exposure.

Opioid use disorder (OUD) is broader.

Signs can include:

- inability to cut down;
- craving;
- escalating use;
- spending substantial time obtaining/using/recovering;
- continuing despite overdose or health harm;
- problems at work or home;
- hazardous use;
- tolerance;
- withdrawal.

A person can be dependent without meeting full OUD criteria.

A person exposed through a mislabeled product can also develop opioid dependence **without knowingly deciding to use an opioid**.

---

## Treatment and support

There is no MGM-15-specific FDA-approved treatment protocol.

When someone is clearly experiencing opioid dependence or OUD after an MGM-15-containing product, clinicians can evaluate them using established **opioid withdrawal / opioid use disorder** principles.

Evidence-based OUD medications include:

- buprenorphine;
- methadone;
- extended-release naltrexone in appropriately selected patients.

This page intentionally does not provide self-induction or taper doses.

### When medical assessment is especially useful

Seek medical/addiction support when:

- use is daily or frequent;
- withdrawal is developing;
- stopping has repeatedly failed;
- severe vomiting/diarrhea or dehydration is present;
- there has been an overdose;
- other opioids, alcohol or benzodiazepines are involved;
- the product identity is uncertain;
- there is a history of severe substance withdrawal;
- mental health is deteriorating.

For U.S. treatment resources, use **FindTreatment.gov** or SAMHSA's National Helpline at **1-800-662-HELP (4357)**.

## Drug testing and forensic identification

MGM-15 creates analytical challenges.

CFSRE has noted that **GC-MS conditions can promote analytical conversion of MGM-15**, complicating identification if methods are not designed for the compound.

That means laboratories need:

- appropriate reference material;
- validated LC-MS/HRMS methods;
- awareness of conversion artifacts;
- careful differentiation from related kratom derivatives.

A routine opioid immunoassay should not be assumed to detect MGM-15.

---

## Product identity and certificates of analysis

An MGM-15 product label does not by itself prove:

- MGM-15 is present;
- the claimed amount is correct;
- no pseudoindoxyl is present;
- no 7-OH is present;
- no other opioid is present.

Conversely, a certificate showing “no mitragynine” or “no 7-OH” does not rule out MGM-15 unless MGM-15 was specifically tested.

The analyte list matters more than the marketing headline.

---

## Relationship to 7-OH

7-OH and MGM-15 share chemical lineage and opioid activity.

They differ in:

- chemical structure;
- receptor pharmacology;
- evidence base;
- product history;
- legal status;
- human data.

7-OH now has a larger human safety/dependence literature.

MGM-15 has much stronger **data gaps**.

That means 7-OH should not be used as a complete surrogate for MGM-15.

---

## Relationship to mitragynine pseudoindoxyl

Mitragynine pseudoindoxyl is a different opioid-active kratom derivative.

Unlike MGM-15, pseudoindoxyl now has direct published human withdrawal evidence.

Both compounds were found together in tested Buzzers Cat’s Claw products.

That is clinically important because a person using such a product is not experiencing a single-drug MGM-15 exposure.

---

## Relationship to MGM-16

MGM-16 is a fluorinated derivative developed in the same medicinal-chemistry program.

The 2014 mouse study found very high opioid activity in specific preclinical assays.

Those results are **not** evidence that MGM-15 or MGM-16 has a safe or effective human therapeutic role.

See [MGM-16](/articles/mgm-16/).

---

## Human overdose and forensic surveillance

MGM-15 now has a stronger real-world signal than the older literature implied, although it still lacks controlled human pharmacology.

DEA's August 2026 temporary scheduling record states that **DEA TOX detected MGM-15 in 17 overdose cases from February through April 2026, 16 associated with fatal events**. Separately, federal materials citing CFSRE trend data reported MGM-15 in **21 toxicology specimens and three drug materials**.

Those counts should be interpreted carefully:

- DEA TOX is a surveillance program enriched for suspected overdose cases, not a denominator-based population study;
- a fatal event with MGM-15 detected can include other opioids, sedatives, illness, or multiple kratom-derived compounds;
- detection establishes exposure, not automatic sole causation;
- the data do not establish a fatal concentration, lethal dose, or individual overdose probability.

The correct conclusion is that MGM-15 has moved beyond a purely theoretical laboratory hazard into **documented overdose toxicology**, while causal attribution and exposure-response remain poorly defined.

## Forensic interpretation

MGM-15 creates two distinct forensic problems: **identity** and **causality**.

For identity, CFSRE reports that GC-MS conditions can promote analytical conversion of MGM-15 to mitragynine. Laboratories therefore need validated methods, suitable reference material, and preferably LC-MS/MS or HRMS workflows that preserve and distinguish the target analyte.

For causality, a toxicology result must be interpreted alongside:

- other opioids and sedatives;
- 7-OH, pseudoindoxyl, and related alkaloids;
- specimen type and postmortem changes;
- tolerance;
- underlying disease;
- scene/product evidence;
- time between use, collapse, treatment, and specimen collection.

There is **no validated MGM-15 "fatal blood level."** A concentration observed in a death cannot be reverse-engineered into a personal lethal dose.

## Special populations

No adequate MGM-15-specific studies define safety in pregnancy, breastfeeding, adolescents, older adults, chronic respiratory disease, seizure disorders, or significant liver/kidney disease.

For a potent opioid-active compound with unknown human PK, those missing data should not be interpreted as reassurance. Class-level concern is especially high when other opioids, benzodiazepines, alcohol, gabapentinoids, sedative-hypnotics, or serious respiratory disease are present.

## Legal and regulatory history

### Before 2026

MGM-15 emerged faster than regulation.

CFSRE's 2025 monograph correctly noted that MGM-15 was not then federally scheduled.

That historical statement became outdated in August 2026.

### United States federal status

DEA temporarily placed **MGM-15, mitragynine pseudoindoxyl and MGM-16 in Schedule I effective August 26, 2026**.

The order remains effective through **August 26, 2028** unless extended or replaced. [Federal Register](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-08-26/pdf/2026-17429.pdf)

### Why the date matters

An old monograph can be scientifically useful while its legal-status paragraph becomes stale.

This site therefore treats legal status as a dated field, not a timeless property.

## Myths and misconceptions

### “MGM-15 is just stronger kratom.”

Misleading. MGM-15 is a semi-synthetic opioid derivative, not traditional kratom leaf.

### “It has fewer reports, so it must be safer than 7-OH.”

No. Fewer reports can simply mean the compound is newer and less frequently tested.

### “Its long duration is scientifically established.”

Not from controlled human PK studies.

### “Delta-receptor activity proves withdrawal lasts longer.”

No. The receptor activity is real; the claimed human withdrawal consequence has not been demonstrated.

### “A label saying no 7-OH means no opioid.”

No. MGM-15 is a different molecule.

### “A Schedule I classification proves a specific potency ratio.”

No. Scheduling is a legal/regulatory decision, not a human potency assay.

## What we know vs what remains unknown

### Established

- MGM-15 is a defined semi-synthetic 7-OH derivative;
- commercial MGM-15 products have been chemically confirmed;
- preclinical assays demonstrate strong opioid-receptor activity;
- forensic monitoring has detected MGM-15;
- MGM-15 has appeared in mislabeled Cat’s Claw products;
- it is U.S. federal Schedule I as of August 26, 2026.

### Strongly plausible

- repeated exposure can produce opioid tolerance and physical dependence;
- opioid-type withdrawal can follow dependence;
- other CNS depressants can increase danger;
- naloxone is relevant to suspected opioid overdose.

### Community signal only

- precise subjective duration;
- specific withdrawal onset/peak;
- MGM-15 being “harder to quit” than 7-OH;
- exact cross-tolerance;
- claimed milligram equivalence.

### Not established

- safe human dose;
- controlled human PK;
- human respiratory-depression threshold;
- long-term safety;
- pregnancy safety;
- MGM-15-specific OUD incidence;
- validated taper;
- validated induction timeline for OUD medication;
- human potency ratio versus 7-OH or conventional opioids.

## If use is becoming difficult to control

A person does not need to wait for a formal MGM-15 study before taking dependence seriously.

Seek help if there is:

- escalating use;
- opioid-like withdrawal;
- inability to reduce use;
- an overdose;
- using despite health/work/relationship harm;
- waking to use;
- anxiety about running out;
- switching between 7-OH, pseudoindoxyl and MGM-15 to avoid withdrawal.

Those patterns are more clinically meaningful than the product's branding.

## Bottom line

MGM-15 is a potent, semi-synthetic opioid derivative that entered commercial and gray-market use **before controlled human pharmacology existed**.

The strongest current evidence establishes:

- real chemical identity;
- commercial availability;
- strong opioid-receptor activity;
- forensic/public-health detection;
- appearance in mislabeled consumer products;
- federal Schedule I status.

The most important missing evidence is equally clear:

- human PK;
- respiratory dose-response;
- dependence incidence;
- withdrawal timeline;
- long-term toxicity;
- safe exposure.

That is why the responsible harm-reduction position is neither “MGM-15 is just kratom” nor “internet users already know how it works.”

It is:

> **MGM-15 is a poorly characterized opioid with enough pharmacology and real-world exposure evidence to take dependence and overdose seriously, and too little human research to justify precise safety claims.**
