> **Bottom line:** Better nasal breathing can make sleep feel better and can make CPAP easier to use, but **opening the nose is usually not the same thing as treating obstructive sleep apnea itself**. Recent systematic reviews consistently report improvements in nasal obstruction, subjective sleepiness, sleep quality and sometimes snoring after isolated nasal surgery, while the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) usually changes little or not enough to count as successful OSA treatment. The evidence supports nasal treatment as an **adjunct for selected patients**, not a substitute for objective OSA management.[1-3]

## Why the nose matters during sleep

The upper airway is a connected system.

Air typically enters through the nose before passing through the pharynx and toward the lungs. Nasal obstruction can increase breathing resistance and encourage mouth breathing.

Common contributors include:

- septal deviation;
- turbinate enlargement;
- chronic rhinitis;
- nasal valve collapse;
- polyps; or
- other structural or inflammatory problems.

It is therefore plausible that severe nasal obstruction can worsen sleep comfort, snoring or tolerance of airway treatment.

But OSA commonly involves collapse farther downstream in the pharyngeal airway.

That is why improving the nose can help without eliminating the obstructive events measured during sleep.

## What the 2024 systematic review found

A 2024 systematic review included 25 studies of isolated nasal surgery in adults with OSA.[1]

Across the literature, surgery was associated with significant improvements in:

- subjective sleep quality;
- daytime sleepiness;
- nasal resistance; and
- snoring.[1]

Those are meaningful outcomes.

However, the same review found **no relevant improvement in the major polysomnographic OSA parameters**.[1]

The authors' conclusion was explicit: isolated nasal surgery should **not** be treated as a primary OSA treatment.[1]

Instead, one important role was improving positive-airway-pressure therapy by:

- reducing required CPAP titration pressures; and
- increasing duration of CPAP use.[1]

That distinction—better symptoms and treatment tolerance without curing the airway disorder—is the key evidence boundary.

## The 2022 review reached a similar conclusion

A 2022 systematic review included 21 studies and focused on both subjective outcomes and objective AHI.[3]

Most included studies did **not** show a significant AHI reduction after isolated nasal surgery.[3]

The meta-analysis suggested a small average decrease, but the authors judged the change **not clinically relevant for treatment success**.[3]

By contrast, Epworth Sleepiness Scale scores improved in many studies.[3]

That creates a classic subjective-objective split:

> patients may feel better even when the defining respiratory index of OSA has not normalized.

Both outcomes matter. They simply answer different questions.

See [Subjective vs Objective Sleep](/articles/subjective-vs-objective-sleep/).

## The 2026 functional-rhinoplasty meta-analysis adds newer evidence

A 2026 systematic review/meta-analysis examined eight studies comprising 27 cohorts and 1,192 adults with sleep-disordered breathing undergoing functional rhinoplasty or septorhinoplasty.[2]

The analysis found a **large improvement in nasal-obstruction symptom scores**.[2]

It also found:

- improvement in Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index scores;
- modest functional-status gains; and
- a directionally favorable daytime-sleepiness effect.[2]

But heterogeneity was substantial for most outcomes, small-study effects were detected, and much of the evidence was observational.[2]

The authors framed these procedures as **adjunctive** treatment in selected patients, not a replacement for established sleep-disordered-breathing therapy.[2]

## Why AHI can stay abnormal after the nose improves

AHI counts apneas and hypopneas per hour of sleep.

Those events often result from collapse or narrowing in parts of the upper airway beyond the nose.

Opening the nasal passage may:

- lower resistance;
- reduce mouth breathing;
- improve comfort;
- reduce some snoring; and
- make PAP easier to tolerate,

while leaving the main collapsible pharyngeal segment largely unchanged.

That is not a failed surgery if the surgical goal was nasal breathing. It simply means **nasal success is not identical to OSA cure**.

## CPAP tolerance may be one of the most useful outcomes

Some people struggle with PAP because nasal obstruction makes pressure uncomfortable or forces mouth breathing.

The 2024 review found evidence that nasal surgery can reduce needed PAP pressures and improve duration of use.[1]

The 2022 review likewise highlighted people with nasal obstruction or CPAP-adherence problems as a subgroup most likely to benefit.[3]

This is clinically important because an adjunct can have high value even if it does not directly normalize AHI.

A treatment that makes effective CPAP wearable for more hours may improve the real treatment pathway more than a small isolated change in AHI.

## Snoring can improve without apnea being cured

Snoring is sound generated by vibration in the upper airway.

Changing nasal airflow can reduce snoring in some people.[1]

But snoring and OSA are not equivalent outcomes.

Someone can snore less and still have clinically important apneas or hypopneas.

See [Snoring vs Sleep Apnea](/articles/snoring-vs-sleep-apnea/).

## Why mouth taping is not a substitute for treating nasal obstruction

A person who cannot comfortably breathe through the nose has a very different safety context from someone with clear nasal airflow.

This is one reason viral mouth-taping advice is problematic.

Taping the mouth does not diagnose why nasal breathing is difficult, and it does not establish whether the airway collapses during sleep.

See [Mouth Taping for Sleep](/articles/mouth-taping-for-sleep/).

## Treat the cause of obstruction, not the label alone

“Nasal obstruction” is a symptom category, not one single diagnosis.

Potential management differs substantially for:

- inflammatory rhinitis;
- structural septal deviation;
- turbinate hypertrophy;
- nasal valve collapse;
- polyps; or
- other ENT problems.

The fact that surgery can help a structural phenotype does not mean every congested sleeper needs surgery.

Likewise, a medication-responsive inflammatory problem should not be generalized from a septoplasty trial.

The intervention must match the cause.

## Better sleep quality does not prove OSA resolution

This is a recurring lesson across sleep medicine.

A person can report:

- easier breathing;
- fewer awakenings;
- less snoring;
- less daytime sleepiness; and
- better perceived sleep,

while still having an abnormal AHI.[1,3]

When OSA has already been diagnosed, symptom improvement should not automatically replace objective reassessment or prescribed therapy.

## What the evidence does not support

Current evidence does **not** justify claims that:

- fixing a deviated septum usually cures OSA;
- better nasal breathing proves AHI is normal;
- reduced snoring proves apnea is gone;
- every person with OSA needs nasal surgery;
- nasal surgery is useless because AHI often changes little;
- improved CPAP tolerance is an unimportant outcome; or
- mouth taping can substitute for evaluation of nasal obstruction.

## A practical decision tree

If nasal obstruction and poor sleep coexist:

1. **Identify whether obstruction is structural, inflammatory or mixed.**
2. **Ask whether there are genuine OSA clues**—snoring, witnessed pauses, gasping, sleepiness or an established diagnosis.
3. **Treat the nasal problem for its own indication.**
4. **If OSA is present, judge nasal treatment as an adjunct unless objective testing proves otherwise.**
5. **Use improved PAP comfort/adherence as a meaningful outcome.**
6. **Do not stop effective OSA therapy simply because nasal breathing feels better.**

## Bottom line

The nose matters, but OSA is a whole-upper-airway disorder.

Recent reviews agree on a nuanced conclusion:[1-3]

- nasal treatment can substantially improve nasal obstruction;
- subjective sleepiness and sleep quality can improve;
- CPAP pressures and adherence may improve;
- objective AHI improvement is usually small or insufficient for treatment success; and
- isolated nasal surgery is therefore best viewed as an **adjunctive tool**, not a primary OSA cure.
