> **Bottom line:** A circadian timing problem can look exactly like insomnia when someone tries to sleep at a clock time their body is not ready for. One of the most useful clues is **sleep ability at the preferred schedule**: if sleep becomes much easier when bedtime and wake time are allowed to shift later, circadian delay moves higher on the list.

## “I cannot sleep at 10 p.m.” does not always mean “I cannot sleep”

Imagine someone who lies awake until 2 a.m. every work night, then struggles to get up at 6:30 a.m. On weekends, they sleep from 2:30 a.m. to 10:30 a.m. with little difficulty.

That pattern is different from someone who lies awake for hours whether bedtime is 10 p.m., midnight or 2 a.m.

The first pattern raises the possibility of **circadian misalignment**. The second may fit chronic insomnia more closely, although the two can coexist.

## What the circadian system does

The circadian clock helps organize roughly 24-hour rhythms in:

- sleep and wake propensity;
- melatonin secretion;
- body temperature;
- alertness;
- and many other physiological processes.

Sleep timing therefore depends on more than how tired someone feels. A person can accumulate substantial sleep pressure while their circadian wake signal is still opposing sleep.

## Delayed sleep-wake phase disorder is the clearest example

In delayed sleep-wake phase disorder (DSWPD), the main sleep period is persistently shifted later than desired or required.

A 2025 review emphasizes a practical distinction from insomnia: people with DSWPD may be able to fall asleep and maintain sleep more normally at later times, but struggle when forced into an earlier social schedule.[2]

This can create a brutal cycle:

1. sleep onset occurs late;
2. work or school still requires an early wake time;
3. sleep duration is cut short;
4. daytime fatigue increases;
5. catch-up sleep and late weekend wake times reinforce the delay.

## Sleep diaries are more useful than one bad night

Circadian disorders are patterns across time.

AASM practice parameters support sleep logs and, in appropriate cases, actigraphy as useful tools for evaluating suspected circadian rhythm sleep disorders.[3]

A diary can reveal whether:

- sleep onset consistently drifts late;
- wake time is highly variable;
- sleep becomes more consolidated on free days;
- naps are compensating for curtailed nighttime sleep; and
- social obligations are repeatedly forcing wake-up before the biological night has ended.

One night of staying awake until 3 a.m. after caffeine tells you much less.

## Light is one of the strongest timing cues

Light exposure helps set circadian phase.

The effect depends heavily on **timing**. Bright light at one circadian phase can shift the clock earlier; at another phase it can shift it later.

That is why generic advice such as “get more light” or “avoid all blue light” is incomplete.

For a delayed schedule, early-day light exposure is often used to support an earlier phase, while excessive bright light late in the evening can work in the opposite direction.

## Melatonin is a timing tool before it is a sleeping pill

The 2015 AASM guideline supports strategically timed melatonin for selected circadian rhythm sleep-wake disorders, including DSWPD.[1]

The word **timed** is doing a lot of work there.

Melatonin taken at the wrong phase can be less effective for shifting the clock, even if it causes some subjective sleepiness. Circadian use is therefore conceptually different from taking a large dose at bedtime simply because “melatonin makes you sleepy.”

This is one of the strongest reasons the sleep cluster should not rank supplements without first identifying the sleep problem.

## Circadian delay and insomnia can coexist

A delayed clock can create repeated failed attempts to sleep early. Over time, that can produce:

- frustration in bed;
- clock-watching;
- anxiety about the next day;
- long periods of wakefulness in bed; and
- conditioned arousal.

At that point, moving the circadian phase may fix only part of the problem. CBT-I may be useful for the insomnia layer while light and schedule interventions target the timing layer.

## A practical pattern check

Ask:

- If I had no alarm tomorrow, when would I naturally fall asleep and wake up?
- Is sleep easier and more continuous at that later schedule?
- Does the same delay repeat for weeks or months?
- Do weekends shift several hours later?
- Is morning light exposure minimal while evening light exposure is intense?
- Am I trying to solve a timing problem by taking increasingly sedating products?

The answers do not diagnose a circadian disorder, but they make the pattern easier to recognize.

## When evaluation becomes more useful

Consider professional evaluation when the schedule mismatch is persistent, causes major impairment, threatens school or work functioning, or is complicated by severe daytime sleepiness, mood symptoms, shift work, another sleep disorder, or medication/substance effects.

## Bottom line

Insomnia means more than “I cannot sleep when I want to.” Sometimes the ability to sleep is intact while the **timing** is shifted.

The high-value distinction is whether sleep improves when the person follows their natural later schedule. If it does, circadian timing deserves serious attention — and the intervention should target **light, schedule and properly timed circadian signals**, not simply stronger sedation.
