> **Safety scope:** This is an evidence review, not a personalized detox schedule or medication-dosing protocol. Opioid withdrawal is often intensely uncomfortable and can become medically complicated. Pregnancy, severe dehydration, serious medical illness, suicidal thinking, uncertain product contents, and simultaneous withdrawal from alcohol, benzodiazepines, GHB/GBL, barbiturates, or other sedatives deserve particular caution. If someone is difficult to wake, has slow or abnormal breathing, blue/gray lips, a seizure, severe confusion, chest pain, fainting, or another medical emergency, seek emergency care.

## Executive summary

**Opioid withdrawal is one syndrome with many clocks.**

Heroin, morphine, oxycodone, fentanyl, methadone, buprenorphine, tramadol, kratom-derived products, and tianeptine can all produce opioid-like physical dependence because mu-opioid-receptor signaling is central to their effects. Yet a shared receptor does **not** make their withdrawal courses interchangeable.

The biggest determinants of the withdrawal pattern are:

- how rapidly effective opioid signaling falls;
- half-life and active metabolites;
- receptor affinity and intrinsic activity;
- formulation and route;
- repeated dosing and tissue redistribution;
- total exposure and duration of dependence;
- whether the drug has important **non-opioid** actions;
- and the person's physiology, co-occurring illness, and other substance use.

SAMHSA's general framework places early withdrawal from many short-acting opioids around **8–24 hours** after last use, with fully developed withdrawal commonly appearing over **1–3 days** and acute symptoms often resolving over roughly **7–10 days**. Long-acting opioids may not produce early withdrawal until much later and may require **14 days or more** for the acute syndrome to resolve [1]. These are population-level guideposts—not a countdown clock for a specific person.

Fentanyl is the most important modern exception to oversimplified "short acting = short withdrawal" rules. Its felt effects may be brief, but repeated exposure, high lipophilicity, variable illicit formulations, and prolonged clearance can complicate withdrawal and buprenorphine initiation. The evidence is still evolving, and studies of precipitated withdrawal in fentanyl-exposed patients do not all produce the same incidence estimate [9–12].

Finally, **withdrawal management is not the same thing as recovery treatment**. Detoxification can end physical exposure. It does not erase craving, loss of tolerance, sleep disruption, pain, mood symptoms, environmental triggers, or opioid use disorder. Large cohort meta-analyses consistently associate ongoing methadone or buprenorphine treatment with lower mortality while people remain in treatment [14–17].

## First, separate four concepts that are constantly blurred together

### Physical dependence

Physical dependence is neuroadaptation: after repeated exposure, the nervous system functions differently in the drug's presence, and stopping or sharply reducing exposure can produce withdrawal.

Dependence can occur during medically appropriate opioid treatment. It does **not**, by itself, prove addiction or opioid use disorder [29].

### Tolerance

Tolerance means a given exposure produces less of an effect over time, or a larger exposure is needed to produce a similar effect. Different opioid effects can develop tolerance at different rates.

Tolerance and dependence often travel together, but they are not the same biological process.

### Withdrawal

Withdrawal is the physiological and psychological syndrome that occurs when effective opioid signaling falls below the level to which the body has adapted. It can happen spontaneously after stopping or reducing an opioid, or it can be **precipitated** by an antagonist or by certain receptor transitions.

### Opioid use disorder

Opioid use disorder (OUD) is a behavioral and clinical syndrome involving features such as impaired control, craving, hazardous use, continued use despite harm, and functional consequences. A person can be physically dependent without meeting criteria for OUD, particularly during prescribed therapy [29].

This distinction matters because **the intensity of withdrawal is not an addiction severity score**.

## Why opioid withdrawal happens

Chronic mu-opioid-receptor activation suppresses several neural systems. With repeated exposure, those systems compensate.

One of the best-described examples involves **noradrenergic signaling in the locus coeruleus**. Opioids suppress neuronal firing and cyclic-AMP-related signaling; chronic exposure is followed by compensatory adaptation. When opioid signaling abruptly falls, the adapted system can rebound into noradrenergic hyperactivity. This helps explain sweating, racing heart, elevated blood pressure, gooseflesh, restlessness, anxiety, insomnia, and other autonomic symptoms.

Withdrawal is broader than one brain region. It also involves:

- pain-processing systems;
- stress and threat circuits;
- reward and motivational signaling;
- gastrointestinal motility;
- sleep/wake systems;
- endocrine and autonomic regulation;
- learned associations between withdrawal relief and renewed opioid use.

That is why opioid withdrawal can feel simultaneously like **flu, panic, insomnia, gastrointestinal illness, pain amplification, and overpowering craving**.

Mechanism also explains why alpha-2 adrenergic agonists such as clonidine and lofexidine can reduce some autonomic symptoms without replacing opioid receptor activation [6–8].

## The shared core: what most opioid withdrawal syndromes have in common

Common features include:

| Domain | Common findings |
|---|---|
| Autonomic | sweating, chills/hot flashes, gooseflesh, runny nose, tearing, yawning |
| Cardiovascular | increased pulse, elevated blood pressure in some people |
| Neuromuscular | restlessness, tremor/twitching, muscle and bone aches |
| Gastrointestinal | abdominal cramping, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, poor appetite |
| Sleep | insomnia, fragmented sleep, restless sleep |
| Psychological | anxiety, irritability, dysphoria, craving |
| Eyes/skin | dilated pupils, piloerection |
| Pain | rebound pain or hyperalgesia can become prominent |

COWS, the **Clinical Opiate Withdrawal Scale**, is an 11-item clinician-administered measure used to quantify observable and reported acute withdrawal features [3]. It has been validated against another withdrawal instrument in controlled opioid-dependent volunteers [4].

COWS is useful, but it is not a universal truth machine. It was not designed to perfectly characterize:

- every fentanyl exposure pattern;
- mixed alcohol/benzodiazepine/opioid withdrawal;
- tramadol's monoaminergic withdrawal features;
- novel synthetic opioids;
- concentrated 7-OH;
- or tianeptine's full symptom range.

A number can support clinical assessment; it does not replace pharmacology, history, examination, or judgment.

## The withdrawal clock: the most important comparison

| Exposure pattern | Typical broad pattern | What makes it different |
|---|---|---|
| **Short-acting full agonists**: heroin, many immediate-release morphine/oxycodone/hydromorphone exposures | Often starts within roughly 8–24 h, develops strongly over 1–3 days, and substantially improves over about a week | Fast fall in receptor activation produces earlier, more compressed withdrawal |
| **Fentanyl / illicit fentanyl mixtures** | Can begin relatively early, but severity and duration are not reliably predicted from its short felt effect | Repeated exposure, lipophilicity, tissue redistribution, adulterants, and uncertain exposure complicate the clock |
| **Methadone** | Often delayed; fully developed withdrawal may not occur for several days and can persist much longer than short-acting opioid withdrawal | Long and variable half-life, accumulation, sustained receptor activation |
| **Buprenorphine** | Often delayed and potentially prolonged after established dependence | Very high MOR affinity, partial agonism, long receptor residence/effective duration |
| **Extended-release prescription opioids** | Usually later/longer than the immediate-release form | Formulation prolongs drug delivery |
| **Tramadol** | Can include ordinary opioid withdrawal plus atypical sensory, anxiety, or neuropsychiatric features | MOR agonism plus serotonin/norepinephrine reuptake effects |
| **Kratom / mitragynine** | Opioid-like symptoms documented; course varies | Multi-alkaloid botanical exposure and non-MOR pharmacology |
| **Concentrated 7-OH** | Direct case reports now document clinically significant opioid-like withdrawal | Product chemistry differs sharply from ordinary leaf; clinical evidence is still small |
| **Tianeptine** | Opioid-like withdrawal can be severe | MOR agonism plus an atypical antidepressant history and poorly regulated U.S. products |
| **Novel synthetic opioids / nitazenes** | Dependence and withdrawal are biologically plausible and reported in real-world practice, but compound-specific timelines may be poorly characterized | Potency, metabolites, analog identity, and product composition can be uncertain |

**The rule to remember:** a short duration of intoxication does not guarantee a short withdrawal syndrome.

## Short-acting opioids: why withdrawal tends to hit earlier

For a classic short-acting full agonist, blood and brain opioid effect falls relatively quickly. The adapted nervous system is therefore exposed to a faster change.

SAMHSA TIP 63 places early withdrawal for short-acting opioids around **8–24 hours**, fully developed withdrawal over **1–3 days**, and total acute duration around **7–10 days** [1]. WHO guidance similarly emphasizes that shorter half-life generally means earlier onset, earlier peak, and earlier resolution [2].

Heroin is the traditional model, but even here the clock is not perfectly determined by heroin's own plasma half-life. Heroin is rapidly converted to active metabolites including 6-monoacetylmorphine and morphine. Route, dose, frequency, and contaminants also matter.

Prescription full agonists such as oxycodone, hydrocodone, morphine, hydromorphone, and codeine broadly share the same syndrome, but **formulation matters**. Extended-release delivery can move the timeline later and make it less compressed.

---

## Fentanyl: the modern exception to simple half-life rules

Fentanyl is highly lipophilic. After repeated exposure, redistribution from tissues can matter even after the most obvious intoxication has worn off. Illicit fentanyl exposure is also rarely a controlled pharmacokinetic experiment: product concentration, analogs, adulterants, frequency of dosing, and route can vary.

A 2023 study specifically examining fentanyl withdrawal found substantial withdrawal severity and investigated the relationship between body mass index, fentanyl clearance, and symptoms [9]. The study is important because it challenges the assumption that fentanyl should behave exactly like heroin simply because both can produce short-lived subjective effects.

### The buprenorphine question

Buprenorphine binds MOR with high affinity but activates it only partially. If it is introduced while a strongly activating full agonist still occupies enough receptors, it can displace that agonist and abruptly lower net receptor activation—**precipitated withdrawal**.

Fentanyl-era evidence is nuanced:

- a large multisite emergency-department study reported a **low incidence** of precipitated withdrawal during protocolized buprenorphine initiation [10];
- a 2024 retrospective hospitalized cohort found a **higher incidence** among fentanyl-using patients meeting its study criteria [11];
- ASAM therefore recommends individualized buprenorphine initiation strategies for people exposed to high-potency synthetic opioids rather than pretending one initiation path fits everyone [12].

These studies are not actually contradictory in a simple way. They involve different settings, inclusion criteria, definitions, protocols, and patient populations.

**What the evidence supports:** fentanyl exposure does not make buprenorphine unusable. It does mean initiation deserves attention to timing, symptoms, exposure history, and the possibility that a conventional approach may need to be individualized by a clinician.

## Methadone withdrawal: slower onset, longer tail

Methadone's long and variable half-life is one reason it works well as a maintenance medication: receptor activation can be sustained across a day without the rapid peaks and crashes of a short-acting opioid.

The same property changes withdrawal.

SAMHSA describes early withdrawal from long-acting opioids as potentially delayed to roughly **36 hours**, with fully developed withdrawal not necessarily appearing until **72–96 hours**, and total withdrawal lasting **14 days or more** [1]. Other clinical references describe methadone withdrawal lasting several weeks in some patients.

This later clock can fool people into thinking they are "fine" during the first day after stopping. It can also make comparisons based only on the first 48 hours misleading.

Methadone withdrawal is often described as less abruptly explosive than heroin withdrawal but more prolonged. That is a broad pattern, not a promise of lower suffering.

---

## Buprenorphine withdrawal: high affinity changes the geometry

Buprenorphine is a high-affinity partial MOR agonist with a long effective duration. These properties are therapeutic advantages: it suppresses withdrawal and craving and has a ceiling effect on respiratory depression relative to full agonists, although dangerous respiratory depression can still occur, especially with other sedatives.

After established physical dependence, stopping buprenorphine can produce withdrawal that is **delayed and prolonged** compared with many short-acting full agonists. The exact course depends on maintenance duration, formulation, taper pattern, dose history, and individual biology.

The important conceptual distinction is:

- **buprenorphine can cause physical dependence** because it is an opioid;
- that fact does **not** mean it is equivalent to uncontrolled fentanyl or heroin use;
- for OUD, remaining in effective buprenorphine treatment can substantially reduce mortality and illicit-opioid exposure [14–17].

Withdrawal burden should therefore not be used as an argument that treatment itself is a failure.

---

## Tramadol and tapentadol: "opioid withdrawal" may be only part of the story

Tramadol is unusual because its effects involve both opioid signaling and serotonin/norepinephrine reuptake inhibition. Published surveillance and reviews describe ordinary opioid-type withdrawal **plus atypical symptoms** such as unusual sensory experiences, severe anxiety/panic, confusion, paranoia, or hallucination-like phenomena in a minority of reports [20,21].

This is an important evidence-transfer lesson: a drug can belong to the opioid family and still have withdrawal features that do not fit a pure MOR model.

Tapentadol also combines MOR agonism with norepinephrine reuptake inhibition, although its pharmacology is not identical to tramadol. Its discontinuation literature is more limited. It should not automatically inherit every tramadol-specific withdrawal claim.

---

## Kratom and mitragynine: opioid-like, but not simply another prescription opioid

Kratom contains a complex alkaloid mixture. Mitragynine and 7-hydroxymitragynine interact with opioid receptors, but botanical kratom is pharmacologically more complicated than a single conventional full MOR agonist.

A systematic review with clinical cases concluded that chronic kratom use can produce tolerance, dependence, and withdrawal, with an opioid-like symptom pattern [22].

The most responsible interpretation is not "kratom withdrawal is identical to heroin withdrawal." It is:

- there is a real opioid-like dependence signal;
- the typical exposure is a multi-alkaloid plant product;
- preparations vary widely;
- concentrated extracts can create different exposures from leaf;
- and newer semi-synthetic products make the old category label **kratom** increasingly inadequate.

For a focused review, see [Kratom & 7-OH Withdrawal: 2026 Evidence & Kratom Differences](/guides/other/kratom-7oh-withdrawal-management/).

## Concentrated 7-OH: the evidence changed quickly

7-Hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) is a potent MOR-active kratom alkaloid now sold in concentrated and often chemically altered products.

Direct 2025–2026 clinical literature includes:

- medically managed acute withdrawal after high-dose chronic exposure [26];
- a case of 7-OH use disorder treated with buprenorphine [28];
- and a retrospective nine-patient series describing buprenorphine-based treatment for problematic purified 7-OH use [27].

This is enough to say concentrated 7-OH can produce clinically significant opioid-like dependence and withdrawal.

It is **not** enough to define a universal 7-OH withdrawal clock or copy a treatment protocol from one case report. Product-label accuracy and chemical composition remain major confounders.

See the full [7-Hydroxymitragynine evidence review](/articles/7-hydroxymitragynine/).

---

## Tianeptine: an atypical antidepressant with real MOR pharmacology

Tianeptine is prescribed as an antidepressant in some countries but is not FDA-approved for medical use in the United States. Its opioid relevance is mechanistically established: receptor work identified tianeptine as a **mu-opioid-receptor agonist** [23].

U.S. misuse has produced a recognizable clinical pattern of:

- escalating use;
- tolerance;
- opioid-like intoxication;
- physical dependence;
- and potentially severe withdrawal.

Published case reports describe treatment with medications used for OUD, but the evidence remains case-based rather than a mature randomized treatment literature [24,25].

That is why tianeptine belongs on an opioid-withdrawal map—but it deserves its own page rather than being reduced to "basically heroin."

See [Tianeptine: Opioid Activity, Dependence, Withdrawal, and U.S. Safety Evidence](/articles/tianeptine-opioid-dependence-withdrawal-evidence-review/).

## Novel synthetic opioids: do not invent timelines where data do not exist

Nitazenes and other emerging synthetic opioids can have very high MOR potency, but a receptor assay or forensic detection does not tell us a validated human withdrawal onset or duration.

For a poorly characterized novel opioid, the responsible evidence hierarchy is:

1. **chemical identity and verified exposure;**
2. **human clinical reports;**
3. **toxicology and pharmacokinetic data;**
4. **preclinical pharmacology;**
5. cautious analogy to better-characterized opioids.

The hierarchy should never be reversed so that a receptor-affinity number becomes a fake clinical timetable.

If compound-specific withdrawal data are absent, this site should say **unknown** and use the class page only for shared biology.

## Spontaneous vs precipitated withdrawal

### Spontaneous withdrawal

Spontaneous withdrawal happens when opioid exposure naturally falls after stopping, missing, or substantially reducing an opioid.

### Precipitated withdrawal

Precipitated withdrawal happens when receptor activation falls abruptly because another drug changes receptor occupancy.

Classic examples include:

- naloxone reversing an opioid overdose;
- naltrexone given while physiological opioid dependence is still present;
- buprenorphine displacing a full agonist before sufficient spontaneous withdrawal has developed.

Precipitated withdrawal can begin rapidly and feel much more abrupt than spontaneous withdrawal.

The phrase **precipitated withdrawal** describes a mechanism, not a moral failure and not proof that buprenorphine "doesn't work." Modern fentanyl-era research instead points toward individualized initiation strategies [10–12].

---

## How acute withdrawal is assessed

Clinicians combine:

- exposure history;
- time since last use;
- objective signs;
- subjective symptoms;
- vital signs;
- co-exposures;
- pregnancy status;
- medical and psychiatric history;
- and standardized tools such as COWS [3,4].

A key limitation is **mixed withdrawal**.

A person stopping opioids plus alcohol, benzodiazepines, GHB, or barbiturates may have a syndrome whose most medically dangerous component is *not* the opioid withdrawal. Seizure, hallucinations, delirium, or severe autonomic instability should not be casually attributed to "just opioids."

---

## What actually treats acute opioid withdrawal?

### Buprenorphine

A Cochrane review found buprenorphine more effective than clonidine or lofexidine for reducing withdrawal severity and improving retention/completion in the studied withdrawal settings [5].

This is not the same as saying every person should perform a self-directed buprenorphine induction. Timing matters, fentanyl complicates the transition for some patients, and treatment strategy should be individualized [10–12].

### Methadone

Methadone can suppress withdrawal and craving and is also an evidence-based long-term treatment for OUD. Its long half-life requires careful clinical induction because drug accumulation matters.

### Alpha-2 adrenergic agonists

Clonidine and lofexidine reduce the sympathetic/noradrenergic component of withdrawal.

Cochrane evidence supports alpha-2 agonists as more effective than placebo for acute withdrawal [6]. Lofexidine is FDA-approved in the United States for mitigation of opioid withdrawal symptoms associated with abrupt opioid discontinuation [8]. Comparative reviews suggest lofexidine and clonidine have broadly similar efficacy, with clonidine generally producing more hypotension [7].

These medications treat **symptoms of withdrawal**; they do not provide the same ongoing OUD treatment effect as methadone or buprenorphine.

### Supportive and symptom-targeted care

Clinical withdrawal care can also include management of:

- fluids and electrolytes;
- nausea/vomiting;
- diarrhea;
- pain;
- sleep disruption;
- anxiety/restlessness;
- co-occurring medical illness.

The specific medication choices depend on the individual and can have important contraindications and drug interactions. This page intentionally does not turn supportive care into a copyable prescription recipe.

---

## Detoxification is an event. Recovery is a process.

One of the most harmful conceptual errors in opioid care is treating "got through withdrawal" as the endpoint.

Acute withdrawal is the period when the body reacts to falling opioid signaling.

Recovery may additionally involve:

- persistent insomnia;
- low energy;
- dysphoria or depression;
- reduced ability to experience reward;
- anxiety and stress sensitivity;
- pain;
- craving;
- gastrointestinal instability;
- disrupted routines;
- damaged relationships or finances;
- infectious-disease care;
- housing or legal problems;
- and rebuilding daily functioning.

There is no single universally accepted biomarker-defined **post-acute withdrawal syndrome (PAWS)** with a fixed timeline. The term is often used clinically and by people in recovery to describe lingering symptoms, but individual symptoms should be evaluated on their own evidence.

Sleep is one of the better-supported persistent problems. Reviews describe sleep disturbance as common during OUD and recovery and link poor sleep with craving, stress, pain, negative affect, and worse treatment outcomes [18]. A 2026 systematic review further documented a substantial insomnia burden across OUD studies [19].

The right scientific stance is therefore:

**persistent symptoms are real; a rigid one-size-fits-all PAWS calendar is not.**

---

## Why relapse after detox can be especially dangerous

Tolerance can decline during abstinence.

If a person returns to a previously tolerated opioid amount after detoxification, incarceration, hospitalization, residential treatment, or another period of abstinence, the old exposure may now exceed their respiratory tolerance.

A classic follow-up study identified overdose deaths after inpatient detoxification and raised concern about loss of tolerance [13].

This is one reason the transition **after** withdrawal deserves as much attention as withdrawal itself.

Harm-reduction planning after detox can include:

- ready access to naloxone;
- avoiding solitary use if return to use occurs;
- awareness that tolerance has changed;
- treatment engagement;
- addressing fentanyl contamination and unknown product potency;
- and rapid re-entry to methadone or buprenorphine treatment when appropriate.

---

## The strongest recovery evidence: staying in effective OUD treatment

For people who meet criteria for OUD, the evidence base for methadone and buprenorphine is far stronger than the evidence for detoxification alone.

A 2017 meta-analysis found mortality substantially higher during periods **out of** methadone or buprenorphine treatment than during treatment [14].

A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis involving hundreds of thousands of participants found all-cause mortality during opioid agonist treatment was less than half the rate during time out of treatment [15].

A separate meta-analysis likewise found major mortality reductions during opioid agonist therapy [16].

A 2024 population-based study comparing buprenorphine/naloxone and methadone found methadone was associated with better treatment retention in that setting, while mortality during treatment was similar within wide confidence intervals [17].

These studies do **not** imply one medication is best for every person. They establish something more important:

> Remaining in effective OUD treatment is a major mortality intervention.

Recovery can be abstinence-based, medication-supported, harm-reduction-oriented, or evolve over time. The evidence does not justify treating medication as "not really recovery."

---

## Recovery domains worth tracking

Instead of one vague question—"Am I recovered yet?"—a more useful framework tracks multiple domains.

### 1. Sleep

Look at sleep onset, awakenings, total sleep time, nightmares, restless legs, circadian disruption, and daytime functioning. Sleep can remain abnormal well beyond the peak physical withdrawal period [18,19].

### 2. Mood and reward

Dysphoria, irritability, depression, and anhedonia can persist. These symptoms may reflect withdrawal/recovery, a pre-existing condition, stress, sleep loss, pain, or several factors at once.

### 3. Craving

Craving can outlast physical withdrawal by a wide margin. It is often cue-dependent and can re-emerge suddenly after a period of improvement.

### 4. Pain

Long-term opioid exposure can coexist with chronic pain and, in some people, opioid-induced hyperalgesia. Withdrawal can temporarily amplify pain. Recovery planning should not assume all pain is "just withdrawal."

### 5. Gastrointestinal function

Diarrhea and cramping are prominent acutely; bowel function can take time to normalize, especially after chronic opioid-induced constipation.

### 6. Stress regulation

The early recovery period can involve exaggerated stress responses. Environmental triggers, conflict, sleep deprivation, and acute stress can sharply raise craving.

### 7. Function

Return to work, parenting, exercise, eating, self-care, appointments, finances, and social connection may recover on a different timeline from physical symptoms.

## Medical opioid tapering is a different clinical context

A patient physically dependent on prescribed opioids for chronic pain is not automatically equivalent to a person with OUD.

Abruptly stopping long-term prescribed opioids can produce withdrawal, destabilize pain, and create psychological distress. Contemporary guidelines generally emphasize individualized tapering rather than forced rapid discontinuation.

The same neurobiology of dependence still matters, but treatment goals can differ:

- pain and function;
- medication safety;
- OUD assessment when indicated;
- slower dose reduction;
- management of co-prescribed sedatives;
- and shared decision-making.

This article focuses on the biology and evidence of withdrawal rather than giving a taper schedule.

---

## Pregnancy changes the risk-benefit calculation

Pregnancy is not the setting for a casual home-detox experiment.

For pregnant patients with OUD, major guidelines support treatment with methadone or buprenorphine rather than repeated cycles of uncontrolled intoxication and withdrawal [1,2].

Pregnancy introduces additional concerns:

- fetal exposure to maternal physiological stress;
- relapse and overdose;
- prenatal care access;
- nutrition;
- infectious disease;
- and neonatal opioid withdrawal after birth.

The safest plan is individualized obstetric and addiction care.

---

## When urgent medical evaluation matters

Opioid withdrawal itself is usually not directly lethal in an otherwise healthy adult, but **do not use that sentence to dismiss serious symptoms**.

Urgent evaluation is appropriate for:

- inability to keep fluids down with signs of significant dehydration;
- fainting or severe weakness;
- chest pain;
- severe shortness of breath;
- seizure;
- severe confusion, hallucinations, or delirium;
- uncontrolled agitation;
- very high fever;
- blood in vomit or stool;
- pregnancy with significant withdrawal;
- suicidal thoughts or inability to stay safe;
- unknown or adulterated drug exposure;
- simultaneous withdrawal from alcohol, benzodiazepines, barbiturates, GHB/GBL, or other sedative-hypnotics;
- or any concern that the person may actually be intoxicated/overdosing rather than withdrawing.

If a person is unresponsive or breathing slowly/irregularly, treat it as a possible opioid overdose: emergency care and naloxone are time-sensitive.

## Common myths, corrected

| Claim | Evidence-based correction |
|---|---|
| **"All opioid withdrawal is basically the same."** | The core syndrome overlaps, but timeline, severity, and special risks vary substantially by pharmacokinetics, receptor behavior, formulation, mixed pharmacology, and exposure pattern. |
| **"Fentanyl is short acting, so fentanyl withdrawal must be short."** | Repeated fentanyl exposure can involve prolonged clearance and an unpredictable withdrawal course; the simple rule is inadequate [9]. |
| **"If you made it through detox, the addiction is gone."** | Detox addresses physical withdrawal. OUD, craving, loss of tolerance, and relapse/overdose risk may persist. |
| **"Methadone or buprenorphine just replace one addiction with another."** | They can cause physical dependence, but high-quality evidence shows treatment is associated with major reductions in mortality and illicit-opioid harm [14–17]. |
| **"Bad withdrawal proves severe addiction."** | Withdrawal severity and OUD severity are related only imperfectly. Dependence, drug kinetics, and exposure pattern strongly affect withdrawal. |
| **"No withdrawal means no dependence."** | Timing matters. Long-acting opioids can produce delayed withdrawal. |
| **"Kratom, 7-OH, and tianeptine are not opioids because they are sold differently."** | Product category and retail setting do not override receptor pharmacology or documented clinical withdrawal [22–28]. |
| **"One fentanyl-free urine test means fentanyl is completely gone from the body."** | Detection windows depend on analyte, assay, exposure, metabolism, and tissue redistribution; clinical decisions should not reduce the pharmacology to one home-testing rule. |

## Evidence-strength ledger

| Question | Confidence | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Do classic MOR agonists produce a recognizable withdrawal syndrome? | **High** | Decades of human clinical evidence and guideline consensus |
| Do short- and long-acting opioids differ in withdrawal timing? | **High** | Consistent clinical pharmacology and guideline evidence |
| Is COWS useful for acute opioid withdrawal assessment? | **Moderate–High** | Widely used and supported by validation studies [3,4] |
| Is buprenorphine effective for withdrawal management? | **High** | Systematic-review evidence [5] |
| Do alpha-2 agonists reduce withdrawal symptoms? | **High** | Randomized-trial systematic reviews [6,7] |
| Does OUD treatment with methadone/buprenorphine reduce mortality? | **High** | Multiple large systematic reviews/meta-analyses [14–16] |
| Is fentanyl withdrawal always more severe or longer than heroin withdrawal? | **Not established as a universal rule** | Direct fentanyl-era evidence is growing but heterogeneous [9–12] |
| Can concentrated 7-OH produce opioid-like withdrawal? | **Emerging human evidence** | Case reports and a small case series [26–28] |
| Can tianeptine produce opioid-like dependence/withdrawal? | **Moderate safety signal; limited treatment evidence** | Mechanistic MOR evidence plus poison-center/case literature [23–25] |
| Is there one validated PAWS timeline after opioids? | **No** | Persistent sleep/mood/craving symptoms are supported, but a single fixed syndrome clock is not |
| Can novel synthetic-opioid withdrawal timelines be inferred from receptor potency alone? | **No** | Human PK and clinical withdrawal data are required |

## What this page intentionally does not do

This page does not provide:

- a universal home detox schedule;
- prescription medication doses;
- a fentanyl-to-buprenorphine timing formula;
- a "safe" amount to resume after abstinence;

- or a promise that one person's withdrawal will follow a published average.

Those are precisely the places where false precision becomes dangerous.

## Research gaps that actually matter

The next generation of opioid-withdrawal research should answer practical clinical questions:

- prospective comparison of fentanyl withdrawal with heroin and prescription-opioid withdrawal;
- better biomarkers of fentanyl clearance and receptor-relevant exposure;
- predictors of precipitated withdrawal during buprenorphine initiation;
- head-to-head evaluation of initiation strategies in high-potency-synthetic-opioid exposure;
- clearer longitudinal data on sleep, mood, cognition, pain, and craving after acute withdrawal;
- treatment outcomes after concentrated 7-OH and other kratom-derived semi-synthetic opioid dependence;
- standardized clinical characterization of tianeptine withdrawal;
- compound-specific withdrawal data for nitazenes and other emerging opioids;
- and better evidence on which recovery interventions improve long-term function rather than only short-term withdrawal scores.

The most important upgrade is conceptual: **research should stop treating “withdrawal completed” as the only meaningful endpoint.**
