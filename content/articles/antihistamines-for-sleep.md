> **Bottom line:** First-generation antihistamines such as diphenhydramine and doxylamine can cause sedation, and diphenhydramine has some older short-term insomnia trials showing modest benefit. But sedation is not the same thing as durable insomnia treatment. For chronic insomnia, the evidence base is weak enough that the American Academy of Sleep Medicine suggests **not** using diphenhydramine, and repeated use has additional problems: rapid tolerance, next-day impairment, and anticholinergic burden.

## Why antihistamines make you sleepy

Diphenhydramine and doxylamine are first-generation H1 antihistamines. Unlike newer, less-sedating allergy medications, they cross the blood-brain barrier readily. Blocking central histamine signaling produces drowsiness, which is why the same drug class appears in both allergy products and over-the-counter sleep aids.

That mechanism explains **sedation**. It does not automatically establish effective treatment of chronic insomnia, better sleep architecture, or improved next-day function.

## Diphenhydramine does have a short-term hypnotic signal

It would be inaccurate to say diphenhydramine never helps sleep.

A 1983 double-blind crossover study in 111 mildly to moderately insomniac primary-care patients tested 50 mg diphenhydramine against placebo for one week each. Diphenhydramine improved several reported sleep measures, including sleep latency, and patients generally preferred it despite experiencing more side effects.[2]

That is real evidence for a short-term sedative/hypnotic effect.

But later research has been less impressive. A randomized trial comparing a valerian-hops combination, diphenhydramine and placebo found only modest subjective improvement with diphenhydramine and no significant advantage on polysomnographic sleep-continuity variables.[4]

In older adults with insomnia, a 14-night randomized crossover trial found clearer sleep improvements with temazepam; diphenhydramine did not produce the same broad placebo-separated pattern.[3]

So the evidence is better summarized as **a modest short-term signal with inconsistent objective benefit**, not as proof that diphenhydramine is a strong insomnia therapy.

## Why AASM recommends against it for chronic insomnia

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine clinical practice guideline suggests that clinicians **not use diphenhydramine** for sleep-onset or sleep-maintenance insomnia in adults.[1]

That recommendation is explicitly about **chronic insomnia treatment**, not about whether a single dose can cause drowsiness.

This distinction resolves an apparent contradiction:

- yes, diphenhydramine is sedating;
- yes, some short trials show subjective benefit;
- no, the evidence is not strong enough to support it as a chronic-insomnia treatment.

Those statements can all be true at once.

## Tolerance can develop remarkably quickly

Repeated nightly use has another problem: the sedating effect may fade.

In a randomized double-blind crossover study, healthy adults received diphenhydramine 50 mg twice daily for four days. Marked sleepiness and psychomotor impairment were present initially, but by day four the sedation was indistinguishable from placebo. The investigators concluded that tolerance to the sedative effects developed rapidly.[5]

That study measured daytime sedation rather than bedtime insomnia treatment, so it should not be converted into an exact rule that "Benadryl stops working after three nights." But it provides strong biological evidence that tolerance to central H1 sedation can emerge within days.

For a nightly sleep aid, that is a major limitation: increasing dose in response to tolerance can increase adverse effects without solving the underlying sleep problem.

## Next-day impairment matters even if you fall asleep faster

A sleep aid is not successful if it simply moves impairment from nighttime to morning.

A PET crossover study found substantial brain H1-receptor occupancy the morning after a nighttime 50 mg dose of diphenhydramine.[6] Other controlled studies have also demonstrated residual sleepiness and psychomotor impairment after first-generation antihistamines.

The practical issue is larger for people who must drive early, operate equipment, make rapid decisions, or perform attention-heavy work the next morning.

"It knocked me out" is therefore not a complete efficacy endpoint. A useful sleep intervention should also be judged by next-day function.

## What about doxylamine?

Doxylamine is another strongly sedating first-generation antihistamine used in OTC sleep products.

Its direct insomnia evidence is thinner. A 2017 multicenter randomized study in adults with short-term insomnia reported substantial improvement, but the trial compared **two doxylamine products with each other**, not doxylamine against placebo.[7]

That means the study is useful for showing that both formulations behaved similarly, but it cannot establish how much of the improvement exceeded natural recovery, expectation, or regression toward the mean.

It is therefore not evidence that doxylamine is a proven long-term insomnia treatment, nor does it establish superiority over diphenhydramine.

## Diphenhydramine and doxylamine are not interchangeable with newer antihistamines

The sedating property is largely a feature of first-generation antihistamines that enter the brain easily.

Newer antihistamines such as fexofenadine and loratadine generally produce much less central impairment. That is why evidence about diphenhydramine's sedation should not be generalized to every drug carrying the word "antihistamine."

The opposite mistake is also common: assuming that because a drug is available without a prescription, it is automatically appropriate for nightly long-term use.

OTC status and chronic-treatment evidence are different questions.

## Older adults deserve extra caution

First-generation antihistamines also have anticholinergic effects. The 2023 American Geriatrics Society Beers Criteria list strongly anticholinergic first-generation antihistamines among medications that are potentially inappropriate for many adults age 65 and older.[8]

The concern is not merely sleepiness. Anticholinergic burden can contribute to confusion, dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention and other adverse effects, with older adults generally more vulnerable.

That does not mean every exposure is automatically dangerous. It means the risk-benefit equation changes substantially with age, comorbidities and other medications.

## Acute sleep loss is different from chronic insomnia

A person who has one or two disrupted nights is answering a different clinical question from someone with persistent insomnia for months.

Short-term antihistamine sedation may feel useful in an acute situation. Chronic insomnia, however, often involves conditioned arousal, circadian misalignment, sleep apnea, restless legs, pain, mood symptoms, medication effects, substance use, or insufficient sleep opportunity.

Repeatedly adding a sedating antihistamine can mask that bottleneck without fixing it.

## Bottom line

Diphenhydramine and doxylamine are **real sedatives**, but that fact is easy to overinterpret.

Diphenhydramine has some older short-term placebo-controlled evidence, yet later trials are mixed and the AASM guideline recommends against it for chronic insomnia. Tolerance to sedation can develop within days, and next-day impairment is measurable. Doxylamine has even thinner placebo-controlled insomnia evidence.

The most defensible verdict is: **possible short-term sedative benefit, weak chronic-insomnia evidence, and meaningful tradeoffs with repeated use**.
