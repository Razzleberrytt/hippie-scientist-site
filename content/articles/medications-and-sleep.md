> **Bottom line:** Before adding something to improve sleep, it is worth checking whether something you already take is part of the problem. A 2025 review of commonly prescribed medications found sleep effects running in **both** directions and varying sharply **within** drug classes. Beta blockers have a mechanism-linked story — they suppress nighttime melatonin. This page is for having a better conversation with a prescriber, not for changing a prescription on your own.

Sleep content has a blind spot.

It will discuss magnesium forms, melatonin timing, screen light, room temperature, caffeine half-life and CBT-I — and never ask what is in the medicine cabinet.

That is a strange omission, because more than half of American adults use prescription medications, and many primary care patients report sleep issues.[1] The overlap between those two groups is large, and it is not a supplement problem.

## What a recent review actually found, class by class

A 2025 review in *Mayo Clinic Proceedings* synthesized literature on how commonly prescribed medications affect sleep, based on a PubMed search of the past decade plus additional references.[1]

The findings are notable mostly for how uneven they are:

- **Beta blockers** are often linked to increased fatigue and somnolence.[1]
- **Diuretics** may benefit patients with obstructive sleep apnea, alongside an increased risk of nocturia.[1]
- **Statins** appear to have minimal impact on sleep, although rare cases of insomnia or nightmares are reported.[1]
- **Antidepressants** have mixed effects — some increase somnolence (trazodone, amitriptyline), others are associated with insomnia (bupropion), and mirtazapine may worsen restless legs syndrome.[1]
- **Levothyroxine** does not significantly disrupt sleep, and evening administration may actually enhance hypothyroidism management.[1]
- **Proton pump inhibitors** improve sleep quality in patients with gastroesophageal reflux disease.[1]
- **Phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors** such as sildenafil may exacerbate severe obstructive sleep apnea, warranting caution.[1]
- **Metformin** does not appear to interfere with sleep, with some studies suggesting limited benefit.[1]

Two things stand out.

First, the direction is not uniform. Several of these **improve** sleep, either directly or by treating the thing that was disrupting it — the PPI finding in reflux patients is the clearest example.[1]

Second, the class label is close to useless. "Antidepressants" contains drugs that reliably sedate and drugs associated with insomnia.[1] Knowing someone is on an antidepressant tells you almost nothing about their sleep.

## Beta blockers have the clearest mechanism

Most medication-sleep associations in that review are reported as effects. Beta blockers come with a proposed mechanism.

Approximately 22 million people in the United States take beta blockers chronically, and **these medications suppress endogenous nighttime melatonin secretion** — which has been proposed to explain the reported side effect of insomnia.[2]

That is a specific, testable claim rather than a general association, and it was tested.

A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel-group trial enrolled **16 hypertensive patients aged 45–64 treated with atenolol or metoprolol**, using two 4-day in-laboratory admissions with polysomnographically recorded sleep.[2]

Compared with placebo, 3 weeks of 2.5 mg nightly melatonin:[2]

- increased **total sleep time by 36 minutes** (P = 0.046);
- increased **sleep efficiency by 7.6%** (P = 0.046);
- decreased **sleep onset latency to Stage 2 by 14 minutes** (P = 0.001); and
- increased **Stage 2 sleep by 41 minutes** (P = 0.037).

Sleep onset latency remained shortened the night after melatonin was discontinued, suggesting a carryover effect, and the authors reported no apparent tolerance and no rebound sleep disturbance on withdrawal.[2]

That is a genuinely well-designed study — objective endpoints, placebo control, in-laboratory polysomnography.

It is also **16 patients**, in one narrow population: hypertensive adults already on two specific beta blockers. The authors framed it as findings that "may assist in developing countermeasures."[2] It should not be read as evidence that melatonin fixes medication-related insomnia generally.

## The sleep apnea interaction deserves separate attention

One item in the review list is different in kind from the others.

Phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors may **exacerbate severe obstructive sleep apnea**, warranting caution.[1]

That is not a comfort issue. Obstructive sleep apnea is characterized by repetitive partial or complete upper-airway obstruction during sleep, and it is linked to a wide range of cardiovascular outcomes including hypertension, heart failure, arrhythmias, coronary artery disease and stroke.[3]

A medication that worsens untreated severe OSA is operating on a condition with real cardiovascular stakes, not just on sleep quality.

The same logic runs the other way for diuretics, which the review notes may **benefit** patients with obstructive sleep apnea.[1]

If you have diagnosed OSA, the medication list is worth reviewing specifically against that diagnosis.

## How to use this without doing something unwise

The failure mode for an article like this is obvious: someone reads it, decides their beta blocker is the problem, and stops taking it.

Do not do that. Beta blockers, antidepressants and PPIs are prescribed for conditions whose untreated consequences are considerably worse than disrupted sleep.

What the evidence supports is a **better conversation**, not a unilateral change:

**Bring the actual list.** Including doses and timing, not just drug names. Timing is sometimes the adjustable variable — the review notes that evening levothyroxine administration may enhance management.[1]

**Ask about the specific drug, not the class.** Bupropion and trazodone are both antidepressants and sit at opposite ends of this literature.[1]

**Mention it if you have OSA or suspect it.** Some medications interact with that diagnosis in ways that matter cardiovascularly.[1][3]

**Do not add melatonin as a reflex.** The trial supporting it was in a specific population on specific drugs.[2] Whether it applies to you is a question for the person managing your prescriptions.

**Sequence it before supplements.** If a medication is contributing, adding a supplement on top is optimizing around the problem rather than addressing it.

## What this page is not claiming

It is not claiming these medications are harmful. Most of the sleep effects described are modest, several are beneficial, and all of these drugs are prescribed for reasons.

It is not claiming melatonin counteracts medication-induced insomnia in general. One 16-patient trial in beta-blocker-treated hypertensive adults does not generalize to every drug and every patient.[2]

It is not offering dosing guidance, and it deliberately does not name a "best" alternative within any class — that is a prescribing decision requiring your history, your indication and your other medications.

And it is not a substitute for review by the clinician or pharmacist who can see your full list.
