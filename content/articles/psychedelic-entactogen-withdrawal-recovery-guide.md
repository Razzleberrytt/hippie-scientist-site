# Psychedelic & Entactogen Withdrawal and Recovery

> **Emergency safety:** Persistent psychosis or mania, suicidal intent, dangerous agitation, seizure, severe overheating, chest pain, collapse, severe confusion, or a visual disturbance that makes driving or other essential activities unsafe requires medical assessment. Hyperthermia, seizure, cardiovascular symptoms, or severe agitation after an entactogen or NBOMe/NBOH exposure are signs of possible **acute toxicity or co-exposure**, not a routine “comedown.”

## Executive answer

“Psychedelic withdrawal” is often used too loosely.

For **classic serotonergic psychedelics** such as LSD, psilocybin and related tryptamines or lysergamides, current human evidence does **not** establish a stereotyped physical withdrawal syndrome comparable with alcohol, benzodiazepines, opioids or nicotine. Rapid tolerance can develop with repeated exposure, but tolerance is not the same thing as physical dependence or withdrawal [1,5].

That does not mean every person feels completely normal when the acute psychedelic effect ends. Sleep loss, emotional exhaustion, anxiety, headache, residual stimulation, difficult psychological material, or a “flat” day afterward can occur. Those are better described as **post-use after-effects** unless a reproducible withdrawal syndrome is actually demonstrated.

**Entactogens and stimulant-like serotonergic drugs** are different. MDMA and benzofuran analogues such as 5-APB, 6-APB, 5-MAPB and 6-MAPB strongly affect monoamine systems. Human MDMA studies support short-lived post-use changes in mood, sleep and cognition, while direct withdrawal/recovery data for benzofurans remain extremely limited [11-21].

A third category is even more important to separate from withdrawal: **persistent complications** such as hallucinogen-persisting perception disorder (HPPD), prolonged anxiety, mania, or psychosis. These can continue after the drug has left the body, but calling them “withdrawal” can obscure the diagnosis and the treatment needed [2-9].

---

## Four different things people call “withdrawal”

### 1. Acute after-effects

These occur as intoxication resolves and may include:

- fatigue;
- headache;
- sleep disruption;
- emotional sensitivity;
- reduced appetite;
- residual stimulation;
- difficulty concentrating.

They can follow an intense experience without implying physical dependence.

### 2. An entactogen or stimulant-like comedown

After MDMA-like exposure, some people report:

- low mood;
- fatigue;
- irritability;
- sleep disturbance;
- reduced concentration;
- diminished motivation.

Human studies suggest these effects are real but variable and are influenced by sleep, repeated dosing, environment, co-use and baseline mental health [11-14].

### 3. Persistent psychiatric or perceptual complications

These include:

- HPPD;
- persistent anxiety or panic;
- mania/hypomania;
- psychosis;
- derealization/depersonalization.

These are **not equivalent to a classic physical withdrawal syndrome** [2-9].

### 4. A substance use disorder

A person can develop compulsive or hazardous use even when physical withdrawal is weak or absent. Use disorder is defined by impaired control and harm, not by whether someone shakes or vomits when stopping.

---

## Tolerance is not dependence

Classic serotonergic psychedelics can produce **rapid tolerance**, often accompanied by cross-tolerance among drugs acting strongly through 5-HT2A signaling.

Tolerance means the same exposure produces less effect after repeated use.

Physical dependence means neuroadaptation produces a characteristic withdrawal syndrome when exposure stops.

Those are not interchangeable. The existence of rapid psychedelic tolerance does not prove that stopping after repeated psychedelic exposure produces a medically significant physical withdrawal syndrome.

For many classic psychedelics, direct evidence for such a syndrome is weak or absent.

---

## Why classic psychedelics usually do not look like opioid or sedative withdrawal

Classic psychedelic effects are strongly associated with **5-HT2A receptor agonism** and downstream cortical network changes [5].

Repeated exposure can rapidly reduce subjective responsiveness. But the pattern of neuroadaptation does not appear to produce the same rebound physiology seen after chronic GABAergic sedatives or opioid agonists.

That is why standard descriptions of LSD or psilocybin discontinuation do not center on seizures, delirium, autonomic instability, vomiting/diarrhea, or a predictable physical withdrawal timetable.

The safer scientific statement is not “psychedelics can never cause withdrawal.” It is:

> **A consistent, clinically important physical withdrawal syndrome has not been established for classic serotonergic psychedelics.**

That preserves room for unusual cases, novel analogues and future data without inventing a syndrome the current evidence does not support.

---

## What happens after a psychedelic experience?

The period after intoxication varies widely.

Possible short-term after-effects include:

- tiredness after prolonged wakefulness;
- emotional sensitivity;
- anxiety after a difficult experience;
- sleep disturbance;
- headache;
- residual perceptual sensitivity;
- difficulty focusing.

Positive “afterglow” effects are also reported in therapeutic and observational settings.

The important diagnostic question is whether symptoms are:

- normal recovery from an intense experience;
- sleep deprivation;
- a medication or substance interaction;
- persistent anxiety;
- emerging mania or psychosis;
- HPPD;
- withdrawal from another co-used drug.

---

## There is no validated classic-psychedelic withdrawal timeline

A table that claims LSD or psilocybin withdrawal “starts at X hours and ends on day Y” would create false precision.

| Situation | Best current interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| One classic psychedelic exposure | Acute effects resolve according to drug PK; a classic withdrawal syndrome is not expected |
| Repeated closely spaced psychedelic use | Rapid tolerance can reduce effect; physical withdrawal remains poorly supported |
| Difficult experience with anxiety afterward | Psychological after-effects may persist; assess severity and function |
| Persistent visual symptoms | Consider HPPD or another neurologic/ophthalmic cause, not “detox” |
| Persistent mania or psychosis | Psychiatric complication requiring assessment |
| MDMA/benzofuran exposure | Post-use mood, sleep and cognitive changes are more plausible because of monoamine-releasing/stimulant pharmacology |
| Polysubstance exposure | Symptoms may reflect another drug's intoxication or withdrawal |

---

## Entactogens are not simply “psychedelics with empathy”

MDMA-like entactogens affect serotonin, dopamine and norepinephrine transporters. Benzofurans such as 5-APB and 6-APB can produce MDA-like monoamine release and have additional receptor activity [16-18].

That makes their post-use pattern meaningfully different from LSD or psilocybin.

### Human MDMA evidence

Controlled and observational studies have found transient post-use changes in:

- mood;
- memory;
- sleep;
- attention and concentration [11-14].

The magnitude is variable.

A 2026 study of “Monday mood” after weekend ecstasy use found that accounting for **time in bed** attenuated the mood difference, illustrating why a post-use crash cannot automatically be assigned to one neurotransmitter or called withdrawal [12].

### Benzofurans

Direct human longitudinal recovery data for 5-APB, 6-APB, 5-MAPB and 6-MAPB are sparse.

Their monoamine pharmacology and acute toxicology make stimulant/entactogen-like after-effects plausible, but there is **no validated benzofuran withdrawal timeline** [16-21].

---

## Dependence and compulsive use

### Classic psychedelics

Classic serotonergic psychedelics are generally associated with lower compulsive-use liability than many sedatives, opioids or stimulants.

That does not make problematic use impossible. Hazardous repeated use can still occur, particularly when someone repeatedly uses psychedelics despite psychiatric, occupational, relationship or safety consequences.

### Entactogens

Repeated MDMA-like use can become compulsive. The stimulant-use-disorder evidence base is more relevant to heavy entactogen use than a classic sedative-withdrawal model [15].

### RC/NPS products

For most novel psychedelic and benzofuran compounds, population-level incidence of dependence or use disorder is unknown.

Absence of data should not be rewritten as proof of zero risk.

---

## HPPD: persistent perception is not withdrawal

Hallucinogen-persisting perception disorder involves persistent or recurrent perceptual disturbances after hallucinogen exposure.

Reported phenomena include:

- visual snow or static;
- trails or afterimages;
- halos;
- intensified colors;
- geometric imagery;
- altered size or motion perception;
- photophobia;
- persistent visual distortions [6-9].

HPPD is uncommon and difficult to study. Published evidence consists heavily of case reports and observational descriptions.

### Why the distinction matters

If a person interprets persistent visual symptoms as “withdrawal,” they may expect them to resolve on an opioid- or benzodiazepine-style detox timeline.

That is not an evidence-based model.

Persistent visual symptoms should be assessed for:

- HPPD;
- migraine;
- seizure phenomena;
- ophthalmic disease;
- medication effects;
- anxiety-related perceptual amplification;
- other neurologic causes.

A 2025 systematic treatment review found that the HPPD treatment literature remains low quality and case-heavy [9]. This page therefore does not present a medication recipe.

---

## Psychosis and mania are complications, not routine withdrawal

Psychedelic-associated psychosis is uncommon relative to total use, but it is documented [2,3].

Published cases are heterogeneous. Some involve:

- predisposition to psychotic or bipolar disorders;
- high or repeated exposure;
- polysubstance use;
- sleep deprivation;
- unusually potent or mislabeled products.

A 2025 treatment systematic review of published psychedelic-induced psychosis cases found a case-based literature with highly variable courses [3].

A 2026 systematic review/meta-analysis also documents psychedelic-associated mania/hypomania reports [4].

Persistent inability to sleep, grandiosity, racing thoughts, dangerous impulsivity, paranoia, hallucinations outside expected intoxication, or severe disorganization warrants psychiatric assessment.

---

## NBOMe and NBOH compounds: toxicity can masquerade as a “bad comedown”

NBOMe and NBOH phenethylamines can produce severe toxicity involving:

- agitation;
- seizures;
- hyperthermia;
- hypertension;
- tachycardia;
- rhabdomyolysis;
- organ injury [25-27].

Those complications are **not evidence of a classic withdrawal syndrome**.

If severe symptoms occur after use, the priority is to recognize acute toxicity, delayed complications, co-exposure or mislabeling rather than assume the person simply needs to “ride out withdrawal.”

---

## RC tryptamines and lysergamides

Compounds such as:

- 1P-LSD;
- 1cP-LSD;
- AL-LAD;
- 4-AcO-DMT;
- 4-HO-MET;
- 5-MeO-MiPT;

have much thinner human recovery literature than LSD or psilocybin [22-24].

1P-LSD has direct human pharmacokinetic data, but that does not create a 1P-LSD withdrawal syndrome [22].

For newer tryptamines, much of the literature concerns pharmacology, intoxication or toxicology rather than dependence.

A responsible monograph should therefore say **“not established”** rather than invent a withdrawal timeline from chemical similarity.

---

## Recovery after a difficult or destabilizing psychedelic experience

Recovery can involve more than waiting for the molecule to clear.

### Sleep

Sleep deprivation can intensify anxiety, visual sensitivity, derealization and mood instability.

Restoring a regular sleep-wake schedule is useful, but severe prolonged insomnia can also signal mania or another psychiatric problem.

### Anxiety and panic

A frightening psychedelic experience can leave persistent anticipatory anxiety or panic.

If symptoms are severe, recurrent or functionally impairing, they deserve standard mental-health assessment rather than being dismissed as a temporary “comedown.”

### Integration and meaning-making

Some people benefit from structured reflection after intense experiences.

“Integration” is not a substitute for treatment of psychosis, mania, PTSD, severe depression or suicidality.

### Return to ordinary function

A useful recovery marker is not whether a person can explain the experience—it is whether sleep, work, relationships, attention, driving and daily safety have returned to baseline.

---

## Recovery after entactogen use

Entactogen recovery may include:

- sleep restoration;
- managing fatigue;
- mood monitoring;
- hydration/nutrition after acute exertion where medically appropriate;
- avoiding repeated use while still sleep-deprived or emotionally depleted;
- assessing depression or suicidality when symptoms are severe;
- addressing compulsive use patterns.

There is no scientifically defensible “serotonin replenishment stack” proven to prevent or cure an MDMA or benzofuran comedown.

Supplement regimens can introduce interactions and should not be presented as a substitute for rest, medical assessment or treatment of a use disorder.

---

## Treatment of problematic psychedelic or entactogen use

There is no medication specifically approved for “psychedelic withdrawal.”

For classic psychedelics, the treatment target is usually not physical detoxification but one of the following:

- hazardous or compulsive use;
- anxiety;
- HPPD;
- psychosis or mania;
- trauma related to a difficult experience;
- another co-occurring substance use disorder.

For heavy MDMA-like or benzofuran use, stimulant-use-disorder treatment principles may be relevant. The 2024 ASAM/AAAP stimulant guideline emphasizes behavioral interventions and management of psychiatric and medical complications [15].

The evidence does not justify a benzofuran-specific detox medication algorithm.

---

## Polysubstance reality

Many psychedelic and entactogen exposures occur alongside:

- cannabis;
- alcohol;
- stimulants;
- benzodiazepines;
- opioids;
- dissociatives;
- multiple serotonergic drugs.

A person who stops several drugs at once may experience a true withdrawal syndrome from **another substance** while assuming it is “psychedelic withdrawal.”

Examples:

- alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal can cause seizures/delirium;
- stimulant withdrawal can cause fatigue, depression and hypersomnia;
- opioid withdrawal causes autonomic and gastrointestinal symptoms;
- cannabis withdrawal can cause irritability, insomnia and appetite changes.

The exposure history matters.

---

## Product mislabeling changes the recovery question

An unknown blotter, pressed tablet, capsule or powder may not contain the expected drug.

Examples include:

- NBOMe/NBOH compounds sold as LSD-like products;
- benzofurans or cathinones represented as MDMA;
- positional isomers sold under one market name;
- mixtures containing multiple psychoactive drugs.

If the chemical identity is wrong, the expected recovery pattern may be wrong too.

For RC/NPS exposures, analytical uncertainty should be treated as a core part of the clinical history.

---

## Special populations

### Bipolar-spectrum or psychotic disorders

People with personal or strong family vulnerability to mania or psychosis may require greater caution. Persistent activation, paranoia or disorganization after use warrants assessment [2-4].

### Adolescents and young adults

Direct long-term evidence is limited, while developmental and psychiatric vulnerability may be especially important.

### People taking serotonergic or stimulant medications

Post-use symptoms can reflect interactions or acute toxicity rather than withdrawal.

### People with persistent visual symptoms

Visual problems that impair driving, reading, sleep or daily function deserve evaluation rather than a fixed “wait it out” timeline.

---

## Myths vs evidence

### “LSD and psilocybin have the same kind of withdrawal as opioids.”

**False.** A classic physical withdrawal syndrome is not established for serotonergic psychedelics.

### “Tolerance proves physical dependence.”

**False.** Tolerance and withdrawal are distinct adaptations.

### “Feeling depressed after MDMA proves serotonin is permanently depleted.”

**Unsupported.** Post-use mood changes are multifactorial and can be influenced by sleep, environment, repeated dosing and baseline mental health [11-14].

### “HPPD is just a long psychedelic withdrawal.”

**False.** HPPD is a persistent perceptual disorder with a different clinical framework [6-9].

### “Psychosis after a psychedelic is a normal comedown.”

**False.** Persistent psychosis or mania is a complication requiring assessment [2-4].

### “All benzofurans recover like MDMA.”

**Unsupported.** Direct longitudinal recovery data for 5/6-APB and 5/6-MAPB are sparse.

### “A product sold as LSD or MDMA tells you what recovery should look like.”

**False.** NPS mislabeling can completely change the toxicology and recovery problem.

---

## Evidence ledger

| Claim | Evidence strength | Best evidence | Important limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Classic serotonergic psychedelics produce rapid tolerance | **High** | Human/class pharmacology [1,5] | Tolerance is not withdrawal |
| A stereotyped physical withdrawal syndrome after LSD/psilocybin is established | **No** | Evidence review | Sparse data for very high-frequency use |
| MDMA-like use can be followed by transient mood/cognitive/sleep changes | **Moderate** | Controlled and observational human studies [11-14] | Polysubstance use, sleep and setting confound effects |
| Benzofurans can be assumed to have the same recovery timeline as MDMA | **No** | Pharmacology + sparse case literature [16-21] | No prospective benzofuran recovery cohorts |
| HPPD can persist after hallucinogen exposure | **Moderate-high for existence** | Reviews/phenomenology studies [6-9] | Incidence and treatment efficacy remain uncertain |
| Psychedelic-associated psychosis/mania can occur | **Moderate for existence** | Systematic reviews [2-4] | Absolute risk is difficult to estimate from case-heavy literature |
| A generic “psychedelic detox medication” is evidence-based | **No** | Evidence review | Treatment should target the actual complication/use disorder |
| RC tryptamines/lysergamides have validated withdrawal timelines | **No** | Human PK/toxicology literature [22-24] | Dependence studies largely absent |
| NBOMe/NBOH severe toxicity represents withdrawal | **No** | Toxicology review/cases [25-27] | Severe acute toxicity may persist after ingestion |

---

## Evidence gaps

High-value unanswered questions include:

- prospective studies of repeated high-frequency classic psychedelic use;
- validated measures separating after-effects from withdrawal;
- incidence of problematic use across newer lysergamides and tryptamines;
- longitudinal mood and sleep recovery after 5-APB/6-APB/5-MAPB/6-MAPB;
- whether benzofuran-specific neurotoxicity meaningfully changes recovery;
- predictors and natural history of HPPD;
- comparative treatments for HPPD;
- predictors of psychedelic-associated mania or psychosis;
- long-term outcomes after NBOMe/NBOH intoxication;
- how mislabeling changes apparent “withdrawal” reports in RC/NPS populations.

---

## Bottom line

The phrase **“psychedelic withdrawal” hides several different clinical problems**.

For classic serotonergic psychedelics, rapid tolerance is real, but a stereotyped physical withdrawal syndrome is not well established. Recovery after use is usually better framed around sleep, emotional state, anxiety, function and recognition of persistent complications.

MDMA-like entactogens and benzofurans sit closer to stimulant recovery: transient low mood, fatigue, sleep disturbance and cognitive changes are plausible, but the evidence does not support one universal “serotonin crash” timeline.

Persistent visual symptoms, mania or psychosis are **not routine detox**. They are complications that need their own diagnostic and treatment framework.

For research chemicals, the most important scientific rule is simple: **do not turn structural similarity into a withdrawal schedule.** When direct human evidence does not exist, say so.
