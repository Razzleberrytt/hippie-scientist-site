> **Bottom line:** Gastrodia elata, also called **Tianma**, now has a meaningful modern human cognition trial. The 2025 randomized study is encouraging, but it is still one 12-week extract-specific trial. That supports a **preliminary human evidence** label—not a dementia-treatment claim.

## Why Gastrodia is interesting now

Gastrodia elata has a long history in East Asian herbal medicine and a large preclinical literature involving neurological pathways.

For years, that made it easy to find mechanistic claims but much harder to answer the question that matters most: does a controlled human trial show a cognitive benefit?

A 2025 randomized trial gives us a much better starting point.[1]

## What the 2025 trial did

The study enrolled **100 adults aged 40 to 65** with normal cognition or mild impairment and randomly assigned them to Gastrodia elata extract or placebo for 12 weeks.[1]

The trial was double-blind and placebo-controlled.

Its primary outcome used the Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale-cognitive subscale, or ADAS-cog.

Secondary outcomes included the Korean Mini-Mental State Examination, BDNF, amyloid-beta, and total antioxidant status.[1]

## What the trial found

After 12 weeks, the Gastrodia group improved more on the **ADAS-cog total score** than the placebo group.[1]

The reported mean change was about -2.15 points with Gastrodia versus -0.92 with placebo.

K-MMSE scores, BDNF, and total antioxidant status also favored the Gastrodia group.

Amyloid-beta did **not** significantly differ between groups.[1]

No serious adverse events were reported.

Those findings are genuinely interesting because the primary cognitive outcome—not merely a post-hoc biomarker—favored the intervention.

## Why the evidence is still preliminary

One positive randomized trial is the beginning of a clinical evidence base, not the end.

The study lasted 12 weeks, enrolled one age range in one research context, and tested a particular preparation.

That leaves obvious unanswered questions:

- Would an independent research group reproduce the result?
- Would the effect persist beyond three months?
- Does it apply to diagnosed mild cognitive impairment or dementia?
- Are other extracts chemically comparable?
- What is the smallest clinically meaningful change on the outcomes used?
- What adverse effects appear in much larger samples?

Until those questions are answered, the result should not be generalized to every Tianma product.

## Does the BDNF result prove a mechanism?

No.

The BDNF finding is useful as a mechanistic clue, but a biomarker change does not prove why a cognitive score changed.

The same caution applies to antioxidant-status measures.

Clinical outcomes and biomarkers should be reported separately rather than turned into a tidy cause-and-effect story.

## Is Gastrodia a treatment for dementia?

The trial does not establish that.

Participants were middle-aged adults with normal cognition or mild impairment, not a confirmed Alzheimer's-disease population.

The study also lasted only 12 weeks.

A cognitive score improving over that period is not evidence that the herb prevents dementia or slows a neurodegenerative disease.

## Safety context

Ten participants in the Gastrodia group and seven in the placebo group experienced mild or moderate adverse events, with no significant difference in overall incidence.[1]

Two gastrointestinal events in the active group were considered possibly related to the study product, and one participant discontinued because of abdominal discomfort.

No serious adverse events occurred.

That gives useful short-term information but not a mature long-term safety database.

## Bottom line

Gastrodia elata now has a **real human signal worth watching**.

A randomized trial of 100 adults reported improvement on its primary cognitive measure, which is much stronger than mechanism-only supplement hype.

But one trial is still one trial. The evidence grade should remain preliminary until the result is independently replicated and shown to generalize beyond the specific extract and study population.
