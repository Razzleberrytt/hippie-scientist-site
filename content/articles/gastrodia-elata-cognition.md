> **Bottom line:** Gastrodia elata, also called **Tianma**, now has a meaningful modern human cognition trial. The 2025 randomized study is encouraging, but it is still one 12-week extract-specific trial. That supports a **preliminary human evidence** label—not a dementia-treatment claim.

## Why Gastrodia is interesting now

Gastrodia elata has a long history in East Asian herbal medicine and a large preclinical literature involving neurological pathways.

For years, that made it easy to find mechanistic claims but much harder to answer the question that matters most for a supplement evidence page: **does a controlled human trial show a cognitive benefit?**

A 2025 randomized trial gives us a much better starting point.[1]

## What the 2025 trial did

The study enrolled **100 adults aged 40 to 65** with normal cognition or mild impairment and randomly assigned them to Gastrodia elata extract or placebo for 12 weeks.[1]

The trial was double-blind and placebo-controlled. Its primary cognitive outcome used the Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale-cognitive subscale, or ADAS-cog.

Secondary outcomes included the Korean Mini-Mental State Examination, brain-derived neurotrophic factor, amyloid-beta, and total antioxidant status.[1]

That design is considerably more informative than an uncontrolled supplement survey or a rodent-memory experiment.

## What the trial found

After 12 weeks, the Gastrodia group improved more on the **ADAS-cog total score** than the placebo group.[1]

The reported mean change was about -2.15 points with Gastrodia versus -0.92 with placebo, with lower scores representing improvement on that scale.

K-MMSE scores, BDNF, and total antioxidant status also favored the Gastrodia group. Amyloid-beta did **not** significantly improve.[1]

No serious adverse events were reported during the trial.

Those findings are legitimately interesting because the primary cognitive outcome—not merely a post-hoc biomarker—favored the intervention.

## Why the evidence is still preliminary

One positive randomized trial is the beginning of a clinical evidence base, not the end of one.

The study lasted 12 weeks, enrolled one age range in one research context, and tested a particular Gastrodia preparation. That leaves several unanswered questions:

- Would an independent research group reproduce the result?
- Would the effect persist beyond three months?
- Does it apply to people with diagnosed mild cognitive impairment or dementia?
- Are other extracts chemically comparable?
- What is the smallest clinically meaningful change on the outcomes used?
- What adverse effects appear in much larger samples?

Until those questions are answered, the result should not be generalized to every Tianma product.

## Does the BDNF change prove a mechanism?

No.

The BDNF finding is useful as a mechanistic clue, but a change in a biomarker does not prove why a cognitive score changed. The same caution applies to antioxidant-status measures.

The clinical outcome and the biomarker outcome should be reported separately rather than turned into a tidy cause-and-effect story.

## Is Gastrodia a treatment for dementia?

The trial does not establish that.

Participants were middle-aged adults with normal or mildly impaired cognition, not a large cohort with diagnosed Alzheimer's disease. A supplement trial in that population cannot be converted into a disease-treatment claim.

That boundary is especially important for search and AI summaries, where "studied for cognition" can easily mutate into "treats cognitive decline."

## Safety context

The 12-week study did not identify serious adverse events.[1] That is helpful but limited.

It does not define long-term safety, rare adverse effects, pregnancy or breastfeeding safety, or all medication interactions. Botanical authentication and extract standardization are also essential because a named herb is not one chemically identical product.

## Verdict

Gastrodia elata has crossed an important threshold: there is now a modern randomized human cognition signal worth following.

The page should therefore sit above purely preclinical herbs in the research hierarchy, while remaining below botanicals with replicated trials and systematic-review support.

For now, **preliminary human evidence** is the most accurate description.
