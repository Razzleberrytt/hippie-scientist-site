> **Bottom line:** Chamomile has a genuine human sleep literature, but the evidence is much more nuanced than the familiar “chamomile tea helps you sleep” story. A 2024 meta-analysis found improvement in some sleep outcomes—particularly nighttime awakenings—while total sleep duration, sleep efficiency, and daytime functioning did not consistently improve. A direct chronic-insomnia trial was also largely negative.

## The 2024 meta-analysis gives the best current overview

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated clinical trials of *Matricaria chamomilla* and sleep. The authors concluded that chamomile improved sleep in some respects, especially the number of awakenings or the ability to stay asleep, but did not produce consistent improvements in sleep duration, sleep efficiency, or daytime functioning.[1]

That is a useful result because it resists an all-or-nothing interpretation. Chamomile does not need to “work” or “not work” across every sleep outcome. A botanical can show a signal for one dimension of sleep and fail on another.

The same review also highlighted quality problems in the underlying literature. Only one included study tested blinding success and product purity or potency. Objective sleep measurements were uncommon. Those weaknesses reduce confidence in a strong clinical conclusion.

## The chronic-insomnia trial was not convincing

A randomized double-blind placebo-controlled pilot study enrolled 34 adults with chronic primary insomnia and tested a standardized chamomile extract for 28 days.[3]

The study did not find significant between-group improvements in total sleep time, sleep efficiency, sleep latency, wake after sleep onset, number of awakenings, or subjective sleep quality.

That null result matters because it directly tested the question most readers care about: whether chamomile materially improves insomnia rather than simply promoting relaxation.

The trial was small, so it cannot prove that chamomile has no effect. But it does mean that a claim such as “chamomile is clinically proven for insomnia” is not supported by the available evidence.

## Why older summaries can sound more positive

A 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis found a significant pooled improvement in sleep quality across the available trials, while also noting that only one randomized trial directly evaluated insomnia severity and that study did not show a significant improvement.[2]

This apparent tension is mostly about outcome definitions. “Sleep quality” can include subjective ratings collected in many different populations. “Insomnia” is a more specific clinical problem, and the direct evidence there is much thinner.

The lesson is not that one review is right and another is wrong. It is that the answer changes depending on the question:

- **General subjective sleep quality:** possible benefit.
- **Nighttime awakenings or staying asleep:** a signal appears in the newer synthesis.
- **Total sleep time:** no reliable improvement established.
- **Sleep efficiency:** no consistent improvement established.
- **Chronic insomnia severity:** evidence remains weak.

## Tea is not the same thing as extract

Many people consume chamomile as tea, but clinical trials often use standardized extracts, capsules, or specific preparations. The concentration of active constituents can differ dramatically.

A positive study using a standardized extract cannot automatically validate every tea bag, tincture, gummy, or multi-ingredient bedtime blend. Conversely, a null extract trial does not prove that the ritual of drinking a warm caffeine-free tea has no value for a person's bedtime routine.

That distinction is important for this site: we should separate the pharmacologic evidence for chamomile from the behavioral value of a relaxing pre-sleep ritual.

## What about apigenin?

Chamomile contains apigenin, a flavonoid often promoted as the ingredient responsible for its calming effects. Mechanistic and preclinical findings make apigenin biologically interesting, but they do not establish that chamomile's human sleep effects are explained by apigenin alone.

Likewise, isolated apigenin supplements should not inherit the entire chamomile evidence base. Ingredient decomposition is not the same thing as a clinical trial.

## Safety context

Chamomile is widely consumed as a food or tea and is generally well tolerated, but concentrated products still deserve normal supplement caution. Allergic reactions are possible, particularly in people sensitive to plants in the Asteraceae family. Product quality and extract strength also vary.

Medication interactions are not mapped comprehensively enough to justify a blanket “safe with everything” claim. People who are pregnant, breastfeeding, taking medications with narrow safety margins, or managing significant medical conditions should evaluate concentrated products more carefully than an ordinary cup of tea.

## Practical interpretation

Chamomile makes the most sense as a **low-certainty, low-intensity sleep-support option**, especially for someone who values the relaxing ritual and is not expecting a strong sedative effect.

The evidence does not justify describing it as a reliable insomnia treatment. It also does not justify dismissing it as pure folklore: modern clinical syntheses do find some favorable sleep-quality signals.

This is exactly the kind of ingredient where outcome-specific wording improves trust. “May help some aspects of sleep quality” is more accurate than “helps you sleep.”

## Bottom line

Chamomile has limited but real human evidence for sleep. The strongest current synthesis suggests possible improvement in nighttime awakenings and some subjective sleep measures, while total sleep time, sleep efficiency, and daytime functioning remain unimpressive. A direct chronic-insomnia trial was largely negative.

The best evidence grade is **limited**, with a narrower signal than popular sleep marketing implies.
