> **Emergency opioid safety:** If someone cannot be awakened, has slow/irregular/stopped breathing, blue or gray lips/skin, gurgling/choking, or severe collapse after a pseudoindoxyl/7-OH/kratom-derived product, call emergency services. **Naloxone is appropriate when opioid exposure is possible**; it does not identify the product and does not replace emergency care.  
>
> Mitragynine pseudoindoxyl (MP) is a potent opioid-active compound with no FDA-approved medical use and no established safe consumer dose. This page is an evidence and public-health review, not a dosing, sourcing, preparation, withdrawal-treatment, or legal-evasion guide.

## Bottom line

Mitragynine pseudoindoxyl is **not ordinary kratom leaf in tablet form**. It is a potent mu-opioid-receptor-active derivative related to 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH), and it has moved from laboratory pharmacology into a real commercial market.

As of September 30, 2026:

- strong preclinical and in-vitro evidence establishes opioid-receptor activity;
- 2025 market studies documented **hundreds of 7-OH/MP products** and dozens of MP-containing products sold in consumer-friendly formats;
- published 2026 case reports now describe severe opioid-like withdrawal after repeated MP-tablet use;
- the federal temporary Schedule I order for mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16 took effect **August 26, 2026**; [DEA order](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-08-26/pdf/2026-17429.pdf)
- no controlled human efficacy trial establishes a therapeutic use, safe dose, long-term safety profile, or consumer dosing regimen.

The correct evidence grade is therefore not simply “very low.” **Human efficacy evidence is absent, while opioid pharmacology and emerging human-harm evidence are substantially stronger.**

### Same formula does not mean same substance

7-OH and MP share the same molecular formula but differ structurally. That creates a practical toxicology problem: methods that do not chromatographically resolve them can misidentify or misquantify the analyte. Chemical lineage also does not justify converting one compound's receptor potency, dose, half-life, or withdrawal timeline into the other's.

## What mitragynine pseudoindoxyl is

Mitragynine pseudoindoxyl is a rearranged opioid-active derivative associated with mitragynine/7-OH chemistry. Experimental work has also shown that 7-OH can rearrange to pseudoindoxyl under human-plasma conditions. [PMID 33344889](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33344889/)

That chemistry should not be confused with saying ordinary botanical kratom naturally contains consumer-tablet quantities of MP.

### Evidence-transfer boundary

A study of:

- dried kratom leaf;
- mitragynine;
- 7-OH;
- mitragynine pseudoindoxyl;
- MGM-15;
- or a mixed commercial tablet

is not automatically evidence for the others.

These compounds can share structural lineage and opioid pharmacology while producing different exposures and product risks.

## Pharmacology: opioid activity is established; human safety is not

Preclinical work describes mitragynine pseudoindoxyl as a potent **mu-opioid receptor agonist** with delta-opioid-receptor antagonism. Some assays also describe signaling bias. [Varadi et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5344672/)

The most important interpretation rule is:

> **Receptor signaling bias is not proof of lower overdose, respiratory, dependence, or withdrawal risk in humans.**

A compound can look mechanistically interesting in receptor assays and still lack the human clinical data needed to establish therapeutic safety.

| Evidence domain | What is known | What remains unknown |
|---|---|---|
| MOR activity | Potent agonist activity in preclinical systems | Human exposure-response curve |
| Delta activity | Antagonism described in preclinical work | Clinical importance |
| Signaling bias | Assay-dependent mechanistic finding | Whether it reduces human respiratory or dependence risk |
| Human PK | No controlled consumer-style MP pharmacokinetic program identified | Onset, accumulation, interindividual variability |
| Long-term use | No controlled safety program | Cardiopulmonary, endocrine, cognitive, dependence and organ effects |

## “Pseudoindoxyl tablets” are now a real market category

The commercial market is no longer hypothetical.

### 304-product marketing study

A 2025 study identified **304 online products containing 7-OH and/or mitragynine pseudoindoxyl**. Most were 7-OH-only, but combination 7-OH/MP products and MP-only products were also identified. Products were commonly sold as chewable or sublingual tablets, shots, gummies, and related formats, often with relaxation, focus, mood, or opioid-alternative marketing. [PMID 40373645](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40373645/)

### 51 MP-containing products

A separate 2025 descriptive assessment identified **51 unique products containing mitragynine pseudoindoxyl**. Most contained MP plus 7-OH; some contained MP alone; others included additional hydroxymitragynine derivatives. The study also documented child-appealing flavors, bright packaging, food imagery, and other consumer-product features. [PMID 40568898](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40568898/)

### Why this matters

A tablet described as “kratom,” “7-OH,” “botanical,” or “pseudo” may not represent one chemically simple exposure.

Product-level questions include:

- Is MP actually present?
- Is 7-OH present too?
- Are MGM-15 or other derivatives present?
- Does the label accurately describe the composition?
- Was the compound created or enriched during processing?
- Is there current independent analytical testing?

A brand name or front-label milligram number is not a substitute for validated chemical analysis.

## Metabolism and pharmacokinetics

The human-plasma conversion experiment supports formation of pseudoindoxyl from 7-OH **in vitro**. It does not quantify absorption, clearance, or accumulation of isolated MP tablets in living humans. Its species differences also limit extrapolation from rodent plasma to human exposure. Controlled human PK remains a gap. [Primary plasma study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33344889/)

## Interactions

Opioid pharmacology creates concern about co-exposure to other opioids or sedatives; the cited MP studies do not establish safe clinical combinations or quantify interaction magnitude. Mixed tablets add uncertainty about which substance drove an event. Receptor signaling bias is not evidence that combining a product with a depressant is safe. [Preclinical pharmacology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5344672/)

## Commercial assay uncertainty extends beyond MP

A 2026 analytical study of commercial kratom-related products found substantial inconsistencies between some labeled and measured **7-OH** quantities and discussed 7-OH as a transient precursor related to pseudoindoxyl formation. [PMID 41825819](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41825819/)

That paper does not prove that every pseudoindoxyl label is inaccurate.

It does strengthen a broader conclusion: **this emerging semi-synthetic market should not be treated as analytically equivalent to a standardized pharmaceutical supply chain.**

## Human evidence: dependence and withdrawal

**Deep recovery guide:** [Opioid Withdrawal & Recovery](/articles/opioid-withdrawal-recovery-guide/) covers withdrawal biology, evidence-based opioid-use-disorder treatment, precipitated withdrawal, relapse prevention, and the overdose risk that follows loss of tolerance.

The old statement “there are no human data” is no longer adequate.

There are still no controlled human efficacy trials, but 2026 case reports provide direct clinical evidence of dependence/withdrawal concerns.

### Severe early-onset withdrawal case

A 2026 case report described a man who progressed from kratom to 7-OH tablets and then MP tablets and presented with severe opioid-like withdrawal, including autonomic and gastrointestinal symptoms and a high Clinical Opiate Withdrawal Scale score. [PMID 41982589](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41982589/)

This single case cannot estimate how frequently MP dependence occurs, define a threshold exposure, or establish a universal treatment approach.

It **does** show that severe clinically recognizable withdrawal can occur in the real-world MP tablet market.

A second 2026 case report also describes medically managed withdrawal associated with regular pseudoindoxyl use.

### What case reports can and cannot prove

**They support:**

- clinical plausibility of opioid-like physical dependence;
- real-world exposure to MP-containing products;
- need for clinicians and poison/addiction specialists to recognize the compound.

**They do not support:**

- a safe dose;
- a typical withdrawal timeline;
- a self-treatment protocol;
- population-level incidence;
- comparative safety versus 7-OH or prescription opioids.

## Safety and risk

Mitragynine pseudoindoxyl should be treated as a potent, poorly characterized opioid-active substance.

Important concerns include:

- opioid-like physical dependence and withdrawal;
- profound sedation;
- respiratory depression risk expected from potent MOR agonism and highlighted by DEA;
- unpredictable risk when products contain multiple active opioid derivatives;
- additive harm with alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, or other CNS depressants;
- uncertain long-term toxicity;
- labeling and quality-control uncertainty.

The earlier version of this page included advice such as “do not use alone.” That kind of language can be read as a use-optimization instruction and has been removed. The evidence purpose here is to explain risk—not to make an unapproved opioid product easier to use.

## Human harm and forensic surveillance: what the 2026 federal record adds

The August 2026 DEA temporary scheduling order adds a much larger surveillance signal than the published case-report literature alone.

According to the federal record:

- **DEA TOX identified mitragynine pseudoindoxyl in at least 56 overdose cases from February 2025 through May 2026; 48 were fatal events.**
- CFSRE trend reporting cited by DEA identified MP in **103 toxicology specimens and 12 drug materials**.
- State/local forensic laboratory data cited in the order included **19 reports** across Arkansas, New York, Ohio, and Wyoming as of June 23, 2026.

Those numbers establish **rapid real-world detection**, not a clean MP-only fatality rate.

A toxicology detection in an overdose or death can coexist with fentanyl, other opioids, sedatives, medical disease, or multiple kratom-derived compounds. Therefore:

> **Detection is evidence of exposure. It is not automatically proof that MP alone caused the event.**

That distinction is central to interpreting the "48 fatal events" figure responsibly.

## Acute toxicity and overdose

Controlled human overdose thresholds do not exist. The risk model rests on:

1. potent MOR agonism in preclinical systems;
2. real-world overdose detections in federal surveillance;
3. severe withdrawal/dependence cases proving clinically meaningful exposure;
4. uncertain composition of commercial products;
5. class-level opioid knowledge about respiratory depression and additive CNS-depressant risk.

Red flags requiring emergency care include inability to awaken, slow/abnormal breathing, blue/gray color, collapse, severe confusion, or suspected mixed-opioid exposure.

**Naloxone context:** MP is opioid-receptor active, so naloxone is appropriate when opioid overdose is suspected. There are not controlled trials that define an MP-specific naloxone dose or observation period. Emergency care remains necessary because product identity and co-exposure may be uncertain.

## Tolerance, dependence, withdrawal, and addiction

### Physical dependence vs substance use disorder

Repeated opioid-receptor agonism can produce physical dependence. Physical dependence means withdrawal occurs when exposure falls; it is **not by itself identical to addiction**.

Substance use disorder is a broader behavioral syndrome that can include craving, inability to cut down, compulsive use, continued use despite harm, escalating use, time spent obtaining/recovering, and interference with work or relationships.

MP case reports contain both kinds of evidence: objectively scored opioid withdrawal and patterns of unsuccessful attempts to reduce use.

### Withdrawal evidence

The strongest direct evidence is still case-level:

- one 2026 case described **severe** opioid-like withdrawal with a COWS score of 31 and marked autonomic/GI symptoms;
- a separate 2026 hospital case described suspected MP withdrawal managed with buprenorphine-based addiction care.

These reports prove that clinically significant withdrawal can occur. They do not define a universal time course, incidence, or self-treatment protocol.

### Treatment and support

Published MP treatment reports should be read as evidence that **standard opioid-use-disorder treatment pathways can be clinically relevant**, not as instructions for self-induction or a one-size-fits-all regimen.

People with repeated withdrawal, craving, inability to stop, escalating use, or use despite harm can seek addiction-medicine care. U.S. resources include **FindTreatment.gov** and SAMHSA's National Helpline (1-800-662-HELP / 4357).

Emergency overdose, severe dehydration, confusion, chest pain, suicidal thoughts, pregnancy, or a medically unstable withdrawal presentation requires urgent medical assessment rather than online taper advice.

## Drug testing and analytical toxicology

MP creates an unusually important **identity problem**.

CFSRE/Cayman analytical work notes that **traditional GC-MS methods can allow 7-OH and MP to co-elute**, compromising identification or quantification unless the method is designed to separate them. Appropriate chromatography, mass-spectrometric confirmation, and reference material matter.

Practical interpretation rules:

- a generic opioid immunoassay should not be expected to identify MP specifically;
- a "kratom positive" result does not prove MP was tested;
- a negative routine screen does not exclude MP;
- a result reported as 7-OH can be unreliable if the method cannot separate MP;
- comprehensive LC-MS/MS or LC-QTOF/HRMS methods are more informative when they include the relevant targets and standards;
- a COA is limited to the tested analytes, sample, method, and batch.

These are reasons to improve testing—not instructions for avoiding detection.

## Forensic interpretation

The current MP evidence base includes toxicology cases, overdose detections, drug-material analyses, and deaths, but there is no validated "fatal blood level" that can be converted into a personal lethal dose.

Interpretation should consider:

- whether MP was analytically distinguished from 7-OH;
- specimen type and postmortem effects;
- co-detected opioids/sedatives;
- tolerance;
- product composition;
- time from exposure to sampling;
- whether the medical examiner attributed causation or merely documented detection.

A federal count of fatal events with MP present is a serious public-health signal, but it should not be rewritten as "MP alone killed 48 people."

## Special populations

No adequate MP-specific pregnancy, breastfeeding, adolescent, geriatric, hepatic, renal, respiratory-disease, or seizure-disorder safety studies were identified.

For an opioid-active compound with uncertain human PK, those gaps argue for additional caution rather than reassurance. Risk is especially concerning with other opioids, benzodiazepines, alcohol, gabapentinoids, sedating medications, or serious respiratory disease.

## Myths and common misconceptions

**"It's just a stronger kind of kratom."**  
Misleading. MP is a distinct rearranged/semi-synthetic opioid-active compound with a different evidence and legal profile from traditional leaf.

**"Biased agonism means it cannot suppress breathing."**  
Not established. Signaling bias in preclinical assays is not proof of human respiratory safety.

**"If the lab says 7-OH, pseudoindoxyl is ruled out."**  
Not necessarily. Some analytical methods can have separation problems; method capability matters.

**"Schedule I means every botanical kratom trace containing related chemistry is prosecuted as MP."**  
No. DEA/DOJ have described an enforcement policy for incidental trace MGPI in otherwise botanical material. That policy is not a legal exemption for deliberately manufactured or added MP.

**"A fatal-case concentration is a lethal dose."**  
False. Blood concentration, ingested amount, tolerance, co-drugs, specimen type, and postmortem change are different variables.

## Regulatory status — verified October 2, 2026

DEA's temporary order places mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16 in federal Schedule I, **effective August 26, 2026 through August 26, 2028**, subject to extension or permanent scheduling. It supersedes the July notice-only snapshot. [Federal Register order](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-08-26/pdf/2026-17429.pdf)

DOJ describes enforcement discretion for incidental trace MGPI in otherwise botanical kratom. That policy is not a legal exemption and does not change MGPI's Schedule I status; it excludes deliberately manufactured, concentrated, fortified, or added MGPI. [DOJ policy](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-announces-emergency-scheduling-three-potent-opioid-compounds)

This order is distinct from the separate 7-OH threshold proceeding. State law and FDA product-law requirements also require separate assessment; this dated research snapshot is not individualized legal advice.

## Why botanical-kratom evidence does not validate pseudoindoxyl tablets

Traditional kratom leaf contains a complex botanical alkaloid mixture dominated by mitragynine.

Commercial pseudoindoxyl tablets can instead deliver deliberately altered or enriched semi-synthetic opioid-active compounds.

That creates a major evidence mismatch:

| Botanical evidence | Cannot automatically answer |
|---|---|
| Kratom leaf pharmacokinetics | MP-tablet pharmacokinetics |
| Traditional-use surveys | Safety of concentrated MP |
| Leaf alkaloid composition | Composition of a branded tablet |
| Mitragynine effects | Pseudoindoxyl dose-response |
| Kratom dependence literature | Incidence/severity of MP-specific dependence |

“Derived from kratom chemistry” is not the same as “equivalent to kratom leaf.”

## Evidence gaps: what the evidence does not justify

Current evidence does not justify:

- a recreational or therapeutic pseudoindoxyl dose;
- a tablet-strength ranking;
- a product recommendation;
- a conversion from 7-OH to MP exposure;
- instructions for maximizing effect;
- a withdrawal self-treatment plan;
- a claim that signaling bias makes MP safer than conventional opioids;
- a claim that a product is lawful merely because it is sold openly.

## FAQ

### What are pseudoindoxyl tablets?

They are retail products marketed as containing mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, sometimes alone and often alongside 7-OH or other related derivatives. Market studies confirm that tablets and other consumer formats are widely sold, but product composition is not standardized like an approved medicine.

### Is mitragynine pseudoindoxyl the same as 7-OH?

No. They are related but distinct compounds. 7-OH can rearrange to pseudoindoxyl under some experimental conditions, and commercial products may contain both.

### Is pseudoindoxyl stronger than kratom?

That comparison is too vague to be scientifically useful. MP has potent MOR activity in preclinical studies, while botanical kratom leaf contains a different alkaloid matrix and exposure profile. There is no controlled human head-to-head trial establishing a simple potency conversion.

### Can pseudoindoxyl cause withdrawal?

Yes, published 2026 case reports now describe severe opioid-like withdrawal associated with repeated MP-tablet use. Case reports cannot define incidence or a universal timeline.

### Are pseudoindoxyl tablets federally Schedule I?

Yes. The federal temporary Schedule I order took effect August 26, 2026. DOJ's incidental-trace enforcement discretion is not a legal exemption. State and FDA rules are separate. [DEA order](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-08-26/pdf/2026-17429.pdf)

### Why is there no dosing section?

Because no controlled human trial establishes a safe consumer dose, products can contain multiple potent derivatives, severe withdrawal has been reported, and the purpose of the article is evidence and safety—not use optimization.

---

## Final verdict

Mitragynine pseudoindoxyl has crossed an important threshold in the evidence base.

It is no longer merely a potent laboratory derivative with theoretical human risk. **Commercial tablets and other products are documented, and severe opioid-like withdrawal has been reported in human cases.** The federal temporary Schedule I order is now effective.

At the same time, controlled human pharmacokinetics, therapeutic efficacy, long-term safety, product standardization, and dose-response remain absent or inadequate.

The most defensible description at this September 2026 update is:

> **A potent semi-synthetic opioid-active kratom derivative with emerging direct human harm evidence, an established commercial product market, and no validated consumer or medical use.**
