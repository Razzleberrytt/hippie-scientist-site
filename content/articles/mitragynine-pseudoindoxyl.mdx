> **Safety notice:** This is an evidence review, not a dosing or withdrawal-treatment guide. Mitragynine pseudoindoxyl (MP) is a potent opioid-active compound with no FDA-approved use, no established therapeutic dose, and no controlled human safety trial. Published human withdrawal reports now exist, so describing the human evidence as “none” is no longer accurate.

## Bottom line

Mitragynine pseudoindoxyl is a potent opioid-active rearrangement product related to [7-hydroxymitragynine](/articles/7-hydroxymitragynine/). Laboratory and animal work supports mu-opioid receptor agonism and delta-opioid receptor antagonism. A 2020 **in-vitro human-plasma experiment** showed that 7-OH can rearrange to MP in plasma, but that experiment does not establish how much MP forms in living people after any retail product. [PMID 33344889](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33344889/)

The human evidence base is still very small, but it is no longer empty: a 2026 case report directly documents severe opioid-like withdrawal after intentional MP use. [PMID 41982589](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41982589/) There are still **no controlled human trials establishing efficacy, pharmacokinetics, a safe exposure, comparative respiratory safety, or long-term risk**.

As of **August 22, 2026**, DEA has published a Notice of Intent to temporarily place MP, MGM-15, and MGM-16 in Schedule I. A Notice of Intent is not itself the temporary scheduling order; this review did not locate a later Federal Register order taking effect by the review date. [Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/07/06/2026-13581/schedules-of-controlled-substances-temporary-placement-of-mitragynine-pseudoindoxyl-mgm-15-and)

## Chemistry and metabolism: do not collapse three different claims

Three statements are often blurred together:

1. **MP can be synthesized or generated by rearrangement chemistry.**
2. **7-OH converted to MP in an in-vitro human-plasma experiment.** [PMID 33344889](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33344889/)
3. **Commercial products can contain MP directly.** Market and forensic surveillance have documented such products. [PMID 40568898](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40568898/) [UNODC](https://www.unodc.org/LSS/Announcement/Details/d0c66623-b200-4537-9f25-7df89fbdbf8b)

They are not interchangeable. An in-vitro plasma conversion experiment does not prove that an oral 7-OH product produces a predictable amount of MP in vivo, and direct MP in a product is a different exposure again.

---

## Pharmacology: potent does not mean clinically characterized

Váradi and colleagues described MP as a potent mu-opioid receptor agonist with delta-opioid receptor antagonism in preclinical systems. The compound showed limited beta-arrestin-2 recruitment in the assays used. [PMID 27556704](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27556704/)

The same 2016 paper reported a more favorable respiratory/dependence profile than morphine in selected animal experiments. That is scientifically interesting, but **animal signaling bias and animal respiratory findings do not establish lower overdose risk in humans**. Later work on biased opioid signaling more broadly has also made clear that beta-arrestin findings should not be treated as a simple safety switch.

The clinically defensible statement is therefore:

> MP has potent opioid pharmacology and unusual signaling features, but its comparative human therapeutic window is unknown.

## Human evidence: no longer “none,” still extremely limited

### 2026 severe-withdrawal case report

A 2026 report described a 34-year-old man with intentional MP use who presented with severe, full-spectrum opioid-like withdrawal and a high Clinical Opiate Withdrawal Scale score. [PMID 41982589](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41982589/)

A single case cannot establish:

- how frequently dependence occurs;
- a dose-response relationship;
- a typical withdrawal timeline;
- comparative severity versus 7-OH or conventional opioids;
- or one standard treatment protocol.

It **does** falsify the older blanket claim that there are no human safety observations at all.

### What remains absent

No controlled human study identified here establishes:

- therapeutic efficacy;
- oral or other-route pharmacokinetics of verified MP;
- receptor occupancy;
- respiratory dose-response;
- long-term organ toxicity;
- dependence incidence;
- or a safe exposure threshold.

## Product-market evidence

A 2025 descriptive assessment found MP-containing products being marketed online, often as “dietary supplements”; only a minority of websites disclosed opioid-receptor activity, while many products used marketing features that could obscure pharmacological risk. [PMID 40568898](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40568898/)

A separate 2025 marketing analysis described 7-OH and MP products as “de facto opioids,” highlighting product positioning and labeling concerns. [PMID 40373645](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40373645/)

Avula and colleagues' 2026 analytical work on commercial kratom-related products also identified MP alongside semi-synthetic 7-OH signatures and oxidation-related chemistry. [PMID 41825819](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41825819/)

**Directness limit:** market surveys establish what was advertised or detected in sampled products. They do not tell us the prevalence, purity, or clinical risk of every product sold under “kratom,” “7-OH,” or MP branding.

## Safety: what can and cannot be said

### Dependence and withdrawal

Direct human case evidence now supports opioid-like dependence/withdrawal as a real clinical concern. The incidence and exposure-response relationship remain unknown. [PMID 41982589](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41982589/)

### Respiratory risk

Preclinical studies show opioid pharmacology and respiratory effects relevant to risk assessment. Claims that MP is “safer than morphine” in people are **not established** because controlled comparative human respiratory studies do not exist.

### Product uncertainty

Product identity, amount, co-occurring alkaloids, and labeling can be uncertain. A brand name or “kratom” label should not be treated as proof that the exposure is ordinary botanical leaf.

### Interactions and long-term toxicity

Robust human interaction studies and long-term toxicology are absent. Mechanistic plausibility is not enough to quantify the risk of a specific combination, so this page does not provide combination guidance.

## Federal regulatory status — verified August 22, 2026

On **July 1, 2026**, DEA announced a federal temporary-scheduling process covering MP, MGM-15, and MGM-16. The associated Federal Register Notice of Intent was published **July 6, 2026** and states that DEA intends to issue a temporary order placing those substances in Schedule I after the statutory notice period. [DEA](https://www.dea.gov/press-releases/2026/07/01/dea-temporarily-schedule-7-oh-and-related-substances-protect-public) [Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/07/06/2026-13581/schedules-of-controlled-substances-temporary-placement-of-mitragynine-pseudoindoxyl-mgm-15-and)

**Precision matters:** the July publication is a **Notice of Intent**, not the operative temporary order. The notice says Schedule I controls take effect when the temporary order is published. A fresh search for this review did not locate a later effective Federal Register order through **August 22, 2026**.

State law can be stricter. For example, Ohio's current Schedule I rule expressly lists mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, while state kratom laws elsewhere differ. See the [7-OH owner page](/articles/7-hydroxymitragynine/) for the broader August 2026 state-law snapshot.

## FAQ

### Is mitragynine pseudoindoxyl the same as 7-OH?

No. They are chemically related but distinct compounds. 7-OH can rearrange to MP under experimental conditions, and some commercial products may contain one or both.

### Does the human body convert 7-OH to MP?

Conversion was demonstrated in **human plasma in vitro**. That supports biological plausibility but does not establish the amount, timing, or clinical importance of MP formation in living people after a retail product. [PMID 33344889](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33344889/)

### Is there human evidence for MP withdrawal?

Yes. A published 2026 case report documents severe opioid-like withdrawal after intentional MP use. That is direct evidence of possibility, not an incidence estimate. [PMID 41982589](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41982589/)

### Is MP proven safer than morphine because it is G-protein biased?

No. Selected preclinical studies found unusual signaling and a more favorable profile in some animal assays, but no controlled human study establishes a respiratory-safety advantage.

### Is MP federally Schedule I as of August 22, 2026?

DEA has published a Notice of Intent to temporarily schedule MP, MGM-15, and MGM-16. This review did not locate a later Federal Register temporary order taking effect by August 22. State law can already be stricter.

## Final verdict

Mitragynine pseudoindoxyl is **not clinically characterized enough to support therapeutic or consumer-use claims**. Its opioid pharmacology is well established preclinically, in-vitro human-plasma work provides a plausible pathway from 7-OH to MP, product surveillance confirms real-world commercial exposure, and a 2026 case report supplies direct human evidence of severe opioid-like withdrawal.

The key uncertainty is no longer whether MP can engage opioid biology. It is how its pharmacokinetics, respiratory effects, dependence liability, product variability, and long-term toxicity behave in humans. Until controlled human data exist, animal signaling advantages should not be promoted as human safety advantages.
