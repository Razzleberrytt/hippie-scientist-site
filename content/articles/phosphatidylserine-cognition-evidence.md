> **Bottom line:** Phosphatidylserine has a long human cognition literature, but it is messier than the familiar "clinically proven memory supplement" claim suggests. Older trials were positive, a later soy-derived PS trial was null, and newer positive studies often use modified or combined formulations.

## The older evidence looked promising

Randomized trials in the early 1990s reported improvements in memory or cognitive/behavioral measures among older adults with age-associated impairment.[1][2]

These studies helped establish phosphatidylserine's reputation.

The formulations often used bovine-cortex-derived phosphatidylserine.

That matters because modern supplements are generally sourced differently.

## The soy-derived trial was negative

A 2001 randomized controlled trial assigned 120 older adults with memory complaints to placebo, 300 mg/day soy-derived phosphatidylserine, or 600 mg/day.[3]

The primary outcomes involved delayed recall and recognition.

No significant treatment differences were found on the primary or other cognitive outcomes.[3]

That negative result is important because it tested a form closer to many later commercial products.

## Later trials complicate attribution

A 2010 randomized trial tested a phosphatidylserine preparation containing omega-3 fatty acids in older adults with memory complaints.[4]

Some memory outcomes improved.

But PS-DHA is not identical to isolated phosphatidylserine.

When a formulation chemically incorporates or combines other bioactive components, the result belongs to that formulation unless evidence shows equivalence.

## Why source history matters

Earlier bovine-derived products and modern soy- or marine-derived phosphatidylserine differ in origin and sometimes fatty-acid composition.

It is not safe to assume that every historical positive trial maps directly onto a modern capsule.

That is a classic example of why an ingredient database needs formulation-level evidence rather than a single pooled "works/doesn't work" verdict.

## What about stress claims?

Phosphatidylserine has also been studied in stress-response experiments, sometimes alone and sometimes with omega-3 fatty acids.

Those results are mixed and often involve small samples.

They should remain separate from age-related cognition evidence.

## What evidence would resolve the formulation question?

The cleanest answer would come from modern head-to-head or independently replicated trials that identify the phosphatidylserine source and fatty-acid composition rather than treating every PS preparation as interchangeable. That would show whether the older positive findings reflect phosphatidylserine itself, a particular source, or a formulation-specific effect.

Primary outcomes matter as well. Memory complaints can be measured with many tests, and a positive secondary task does not cancel a neutral prespecified primary endpoint. Future trials should make that hierarchy easy to see and should report effect sizes rather than only whether a threshold for statistical significance was crossed.

The population boundary also needs to remain intact. Age-associated memory complaints, diagnosed dementia, stress-response studies, and healthy-young-adult nootropic use are different questions. Evidence from one group should not automatically raise confidence for the others. The same rule applies to duration: a short cognitive-test change cannot establish protection against long-term cognitive decline or dementia.

## Verdict

Phosphatidylserine has **limited, mixed human evidence for memory**, with meaningful positive historical trials and meaningful negative evidence.

The biggest interpretive issue is formulation identity: old bovine PS, soy PS, and PS-DHA should not be treated as one interchangeable intervention.
