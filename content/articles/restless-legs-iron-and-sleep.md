A person who says “I cannot fall asleep” may not have a generic insomnia problem at all. If the barrier is an uncomfortable urge to move the legs that appears or worsens at rest, becomes more noticeable in the evening, and improves temporarily with movement, the sleep complaint may point toward **restless legs syndrome (RLS)**.

That distinction matters because the evidence path changes quickly once RLS enters the picture. Instead of adding another sedating supplement, current sleep-medicine guidance puts unusual emphasis on **iron status**.

## Why RLS can look like insomnia

RLS often creates sleep-onset difficulty because lying still is exactly when symptoms can become most intrusive. A person may get into bed tired, then experience creeping, pulling, tingling, aching, or simply an irresistible need to move.

Walking, stretching, or moving the legs may provide temporary relief. The cycle can repeat when the person lies down again.

From the outside, that may sound like “insomnia.” Mechanistically, though, the treatment question is different from racing thoughts, circadian delay, or insufficient sleep opportunity.

## Iron is not just another sleep ingredient here

The 2025 American Academy of Sleep Medicine clinical practice guideline for RLS and periodic limb movement disorder includes a good-practice statement that clinicians should regularly test iron studies in patients with clinically significant RLS.

The recommended evaluation includes **ferritin** and **transferrin saturation**, generally using morning blood testing under specified conditions. The guideline also notes that RLS-specific iron thresholds differ from the thresholds commonly used for the general population.

That is a major evidence distinction.

On a supplement site, “iron helps sleep” would be sloppy. A much more accurate statement is:

> Iron status is clinically important in RLS, and treatment decisions should be guided by RLS-specific evaluation rather than by treating iron as a universal sedative or sleep enhancer.

## Why blind iron supplementation is a bad shortcut

Iron is essential, but more is not automatically better. Unlike many trendy sleep ingredients, iron has a clear physiologic requirement and a real downside when taken unnecessarily or excessively.

That is why the RLS guideline centers **testing first**. A person with restless legs symptoms may need evaluation of iron status, but symptoms alone do not establish iron deficiency or identify the right form, dose, route, or duration.

This is also where generic “best supplements for sleep” lists can fail. They tend to rank ingredients by popularity while ignoring disorders in which the relevant nutrient question is conditional on laboratory data.

## RLS and periodic limb movements are related but not identical

Restless legs syndrome is defined primarily by the **awake symptom experience**: the urge to move, worsening at rest, evening predominance, and relief with movement.

Periodic limb movements of sleep are repetitive movements recorded during sleep. They can occur with RLS but are not synonymous with it.

That difference matters when reading studies. A trial can change limb-movement frequency without proving that every insomnia symptom improves, just as a sleep supplement can change one questionnaire score without fixing an underlying movement disorder.

## What can worsen RLS symptoms

The AASM guideline also emphasizes reviewing exacerbating factors and coexisting conditions. The exact clinical workup varies, but the broader principle is useful: **symptoms should be interpreted in context rather than treated with a random calming stack.**

Some medications, sleep deprivation, pregnancy, kidney disease, and other medical factors can be relevant to RLS. The presence of these possibilities is another reason self-directed iron treatment is not the same thing as evidence-based care.

## Why this page belongs in a supplement research library

RLS is a perfect example of where a responsible supplement site can create more value by **redirecting the question** rather than recommending another product.

If someone has persistent difficulty falling asleep because of leg discomfort or an urge to move, the highest-value next step may not be melatonin, magnesium, valerian, or L-theanine. It may be determining whether the sleep complaint fits an RLS pattern and whether iron studies are appropriate.

## Bottom line

Restless legs syndrome can present as “I cannot sleep,” but the mechanism and evidence pathway differ from primary insomnia.

The strongest current guidance does **not** say “take iron for sleep.” It says iron status is important enough in clinically significant RLS that ferritin and transferrin saturation should be evaluated and used to inform treatment.

That is a far more useful—and safer—answer than adding iron to a generic nighttime stack.
