> **Bottom line:** Shilajit has a few randomized human studies that are genuinely interesting, including testosterone and muscular-fatigue outcomes. The bigger problem is generalization: Shilajit is a chemically variable natural material, so results from one purified product cannot be assigned to every resin or capsule sold under the name.

## The testosterone trial

A randomized double-blind placebo-controlled study enrolled healthy men aged 45 to 55 and tested purified Shilajit for 90 days.[1]

The active group showed higher total testosterone, free testosterone, and DHEAS compared with placebo.

That is a direct human hormonal result.

It does not establish that Shilajit raises testosterone in younger men, men with diagnosed hypogonadism, women, or every commercial product.

## The strength study

A separate randomized study enrolled 63 recreationally active young men and tested two doses of Shilajit against placebo for eight weeks.[2]

The higher-dose group showed a smaller fatigue-related decline in maximal isometric strength under the study protocol.

That is a useful performance signal, but it is not the same as demonstrating larger muscle gain, higher one-repetition maximum strength, or better athletic performance across sports.

## Product identity is unusually important

Shilajit is a complex natural material containing humic substances, minerals, and many other constituents.

Geography and processing can materially change composition.

That means a trial on a purified standardized product is not a trial on random raw resin purchased online.

## Heavy-metal findings are mixed—and that is the point

A 2024 review documented that Shilajit can contain multiple metals, including toxic elements, with some reports exceeding permissible levels.[3]

A newer 2026 analytical study of 12 samples found substantial geographic variability but reported toxic metals below the thresholds used by that study.[4]

These findings are not actually contradictory. They show **product variability**.

Safety depends on source, purification, testing, and batch quality.

## "Contains fulvic acid" is not enough

Commercial Shilajit products are often marketed around fulvic-acid percentage.

That does not tell the entire composition, prove purity, or substitute for contaminant testing.

A chemically complex geologic-biological material needs stronger quality control than a single isolated molecule.

## What would make the human results easier to generalize?

Replication should start with product identity. A future trial should report the source material, purification process, relevant chemical characterization, contaminant testing, dose, and batch consistency. Without those details, a positive result may be real for the studied preparation while remaining difficult to apply to the wider Shilajit market.

The testosterone finding also needs population-specific replication. A change in healthy middle-aged men does not establish the same hormonal response in younger adults, people with clinically low testosterone, women, or athletes. The strength-fatigue result is similarly narrow: it measures a particular response under a particular protocol rather than proving greater muscle growth or sport performance.

Safety evidence should travel with efficacy evidence. Because composition varies, a clean result from one purified batch cannot serve as a contaminant guarantee for unrelated products. That makes third-party analytical quality a separate evidence question, not a footnote to the clinical trials.

## Verdict

Shilajit has **preliminary human evidence** for a few outcomes, including testosterone and fatigue-related strength.

Its biggest evidence lesson is that the studied preparation matters enormously. Human-trial findings and product-quality testing need to stay connected on the same page.
