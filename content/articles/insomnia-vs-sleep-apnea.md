> **Bottom line:** Insomnia and obstructive sleep apnea can produce some of the same complaints — fragmented sleep, repeated awakenings, fatigue and poor daytime function — and they can occur together. The important difference is mechanism: insomnia is not an airway-obstruction disorder, while obstructive sleep apnea is. A person can therefore need CBT-I, apnea treatment, or both rather than a stronger sedative.

## The overlap is bigger than most people expect

A stereotypical picture of sleep apnea is loud snoring plus someone falling asleep during the day. A stereotypical picture of insomnia is lying awake for hours. Real presentations are messier.

Obstructive sleep apnea repeatedly narrows or closes the upper airway during sleep. Those respiratory events can trigger brief arousals that fragment the night. A person may remember some awakenings and not others.

That means apnea can sometimes look like **sleep-maintenance insomnia**: falling asleep reasonably well, then waking repeatedly or feeling as if sleep never becomes restorative.

Insomnia, meanwhile, can include trouble falling asleep, trouble staying asleep, waking too early, and conditioned arousal around sleep.

## COMISA means both conditions are present

Researchers use **COMISA** for comorbid insomnia and sleep apnea.

A 2024 quantitative synthesis found that people with COMISA showed worse sleep quality than people with obstructive sleep apnea alone and greater sleep fragmentation than people with insomnia alone.[1] Reviews increasingly emphasize that treating the overlap as either "just insomnia" or "just apnea" can miss part of the problem.[1][2]

The practical implication is simple: symptoms do not always belong to one bucket.

## Clues that push apnea higher on the list

No single symptom diagnoses sleep apnea, but several features increase suspicion:

- loud, habitual snoring;
- witnessed pauses in breathing;
- choking or gasping during sleep;
- repeated unexplained awakenings;
- morning headaches or dry mouth;
- substantial daytime sleepiness;
- resistant hypertension or other cardiometabolic risk factors; and
- a bed partner repeatedly noticing abnormal breathing.

A normal-looking wearable sleep score does not rule out apnea. Consumer trackers are not substitutes for a diagnostic sleep study.

## Clues that fit chronic insomnia more closely

Chronic insomnia becomes more plausible when the pattern centers on persistent difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep despite adequate opportunity, with meaningful daytime consequences.

Common features include:

- increasing alertness or frustration after getting into bed;
- worry about whether sleep will happen;
- spending progressively longer periods awake in bed;
- variable sleep that improves when pressure to sleep is lower; and
- a long-standing learned association between bed and wakefulness.

For chronic insomnia, major guidelines place **CBT-I** near the top of treatment because it targets the behavioral and cognitive mechanisms that maintain the disorder.[3]

## Why the distinction changes treatment

CBT-I can improve chronic insomnia. It does not mechanically splint open a collapsing airway.

Positive airway pressure, oral appliances, weight-management strategies in appropriate patients, positional approaches, surgery in selected cases, and other apnea-directed treatments target a different mechanism.

Likewise, treating apnea alone may not fully resolve conditioned insomnia in someone who has COMISA.

This is why "what is the strongest sleep aid?" is a poor starting question when breathing-related sleep fragmentation is possible.

## Sedation is not the same as treating apnea

A supplement or sedating medication may change how sleepy someone feels without fixing obstruction.

That matters because adding sedatives can make symptoms harder to interpret, may add next-day impairment, and can interact with alcohol or other depressants. Anyone with suspected sleep-disordered breathing should be cautious about assuming that making themselves more sedated solves the underlying problem.

## A useful decision path

If the main complaint is repeated awakening, ask:

1. **Is breathing abnormal?** Snoring, gasping, pauses, or choking increase the value of apnea evaluation.
2. **Is there strong conditioned wakefulness?** Long periods awake in bed and escalating sleep anxiety fit an insomnia loop.
3. **Could both be true?** COMISA is common enough that an either-or assumption can be wrong.
4. **Is daytime sleepiness severe?** Falling asleep while driving or during safety-critical tasks needs prompt attention.

## When evaluation matters most

Formal evaluation becomes especially important with witnessed breathing pauses, gasping, major daytime sleepiness, cardiovascular risk, or persistent insomnia that does not make sense from schedule and behavior alone.

A clinician may use history, validated screening tools and, when appropriate, home sleep apnea testing or laboratory polysomnography. Which test fits depends on the clinical situation.

## Bottom line

Insomnia and sleep apnea overlap in symptoms but not in mechanism. Sleep apnea can fragment sleep enough to resemble insomnia, and both disorders can coexist as COMISA.

The highest-value move is therefore not automatically adding sedation. It is identifying whether the dominant problem is **conditioned insomnia, airway obstruction, or both** and matching treatment to that mechanism.
