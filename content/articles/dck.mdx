# DCK (Deschloroketamine)

## Quick answer

**DCK, or deschloroketamine, is a ketamine analogue and arylcyclohexylamine dissociative.**

It is analytically established in illicit products and human specimens, but compound-specific clinical toxicology remains limited.

## Why “ketamine analogue” is not enough

DCK removes ketamine's chlorine substituent.

That structural change can alter metabolism, persistence, and pharmacologic behavior.

It therefore should not be treated as a clinically interchangeable form of ketamine.

## Human and forensic evidence

In a 2020 cluster of 20 analytically confirmed 2F-DCK cases, DCK was co-detected in **50%** of patients.

Forensic studies have also identified DCK in blood, hair, and seized products.

A 2026 French addictovigilance study included DCK among ketamine analogues involved in serious cases and documented that some ketamine analogues were unintentionally consumed because products were substituted or mislabeled.

## Sold as ketamine

Published research notes that DCK has been sold as “ketamine,” creating a direct product-identity problem.

That matters because onset, duration, and impairment may not match the user's expectation.

## Evidence limits

DCK has substantially less clean human single-agent evidence than O-PCE or 2F-DCK.

That means forum duration and potency claims should not be converted into clinical equivalence.

## Bottom line

DCK is a real, analytically confirmed dissociative with human exposure evidence and a documented substitution problem.

The main safety message is uncertainty: similar chemistry to ketamine does not make the exposure predictable.
