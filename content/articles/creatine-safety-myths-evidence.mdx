**Important safety notice**
This page is an educational evidence review, not medical advice. People with kidney disease, on nephrotoxic medications, or with abnormal kidney labs should discuss any supplement with their clinician. Tell your doctor you take creatine before kidney blood work — it changes the numbers, not necessarily the kidneys.

---

## Key numbers — the citable core

- **26 randomized studies; 1,036 participants:** a July 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis of creatine and kidney-health outcomes (de Souza Almeida et al., PMID 42507286).
- **+0.14 mg/dL serum creatinine:** pooled mean difference in that analysis, alongside **−10.75 mL/min** for creatinine-derived GFR; neither by itself establishes structural kidney injury.
- **No statistically significant difference in chromium-51 EDTA-measured filtration** in that meta-analysis (mean difference +5.89 mL/min, 95% CI −0.30 to +12.08). The interval and study limitations mean this does not prove lifetime zero risk.
- **96.1 versus 96.4 mL/min/1.73 m²:** post-intervention chromium-51 EDTA clearance in selected people with type 2 diabetes (creatine versus placebo) in the Gualano 2011 trial; p=0.58 (PMID 20976468).
- **1998:** the year Pritchard and Kalra published a *Lancet* case letter, not a randomized trial, about a patient with existing kidney disease (PMID 9643752).
- **2008 versus 2011:** Gualano 2008 assessed **cystatin C**, whereas Gualano 2011 used **directly measured chromium-51 EDTA clearance** (PMIDs 18188581, 20976468).

## The 1998 letter that started it all

**A widely cited early warning was a 1998 *Lancet* case letter by Pritchard and Kalra (PMID 9643752), not a controlled study of causation.**

The letter described a 25-year-old soccer player with "strong circumstantial evidence" of renal dysfunction after eight weeks of recommended-dose creatine. What the letter also disclosed, and what the headlines dropped:

- The patient had **pre-existing focal segmental glomerulosclerosis** with relapsing nephrotic syndrome — he already had kidney disease.
- He had spent **five years on cyclosporin**, a drug with well-known nephrotoxicity.
- Media commentary also associated creatine with **three wrestler deaths** during dangerous weight-cutting and dehydration; a causal contribution from creatine was not established.

Poortmans & Francaux (2000) later confirmed this letter "initiated" the media concern, amplified by coverage in *L'Equipe* and *The Independent* in 1998. Subsequent controlled studies have generally been reassuring for studied populations, but the single case cannot establish causation and later studies do not establish zero risk.

> **Myth:** "A Lancet study proved creatine damages kidneys."
> **Fact:** A 1998 *Lancet* case letter described a patient with pre-existing kidney disease and cyclosporine exposure. This report is hypothesis-generating, not proof of a general causal effect.

## The kidney question: what the trials actually show

## Does creatine cause permanent kidney damage in healthy adults?

**Controlled studies have not found a consistent impairment signal over observed study periods, but permanent lifetime safety cannot be established from finite follow-up.** Findings are population- and exposure-specific (PMIDs 20976468, 42507286).

## Can creatine increase serum creatinine without kidney injury?

**Yes.** Supplementation can alter creatinine production and estimated GFR even if directly measured filtration is stable (PMIDs 10893433, 42507286).

## Is creatine bad for your kidneys?

**Most controlled trials in generally healthy adults have not found evidence of kidney injury from studied creatine-monohydrate regimens.** Study designs and endpoints vary and cannot rule out every rare, long-term or medically complex risk (PMIDs 31375416, 42507286).

## Which creatine trials directly measured kidney filtration?

**Gualano 2011 and Lugaresi 2013 used chromium-51 EDTA clearance. Gualano 2008 measured cystatin C instead.** These are different renal endpoints (PMIDs 20976468, 23680457, 18188581).

## What did the July 2026 creatine kidney meta-analysis find?

**Across 26 randomized studies and 1,036 participants, creatinine increased and creatinine-derived eGFR decreased, but chromium-51 EDTA clearance did not differ significantly.** Results favor a marker-related explanation without proving absence of rare harms (PMID 42507286).

## Is cystatin C the same thing as measured GFR?

**No.** Cystatin C is a circulating filtration biomarker; directly measured GFR uses tracer clearance. Both can help assess kidney function, but they are not interchangeable (PMID 18188581).

## Has creatine been tested with measured GFR — not just creatinine?

**Yes; some trials measured filtration directly, which helps distinguish metabolic effects on creatinine from filtration changes.** Not every trial used directly measured GFR, and measured GFR is not the same as cystatin C or estimated GFR:

- **Directly measured GFR (chromium-51 EDTA clearance):** used in Gualano 2011 (selected patients with type 2 diabetes; 96.1 vs 96.4 mL/min/1.73m² post-treatment) and Lugaresi 2013 (resistance-trained, high-protein diet; PMIDs 20976468, 23680457). **Gualano 2008 did not directly measure GFR** (PMID 18188581).
- **Cystatin C:** an alternative blood filtration marker assessed in Gualano 2008. It is not a direct clearance test, and interpretation depends on its own nonrenal influences (PMID 18188581).
- **Tubular-injury biomarkers KIM-1 and MCP-1**: unchanged on 3 or 5 g/day for 35 days (Vilar Neto 2020, PMID 32670557).
- **Proteinuria and albuminuria:** not significantly different in selected studies and pooled analyses, but many studies were small or short (PMID 42507286).

## What is the difference between creatinine and creatine?

**Creatine buffers cellular energy; creatinine is a metabolic product used for kidney testing.** Serum creatinine reflects production as well as clearance (PMID 10893433).

## What is eGFR and why can creatine affect it?

**eGFR is an estimated filtration value, commonly calculated using serum creatinine.** Changing creatinine production can change an estimate without changing true filtration equivalently (PMID 42507286).

## Should a low eGFR while taking creatine always be ignored?

**No.** A concerning result requires clinical interpretation using prior results, symptoms, urine testing and sometimes cystatin C or measured clearance.

## Why does my creatinine go up on creatine — and does it mean damage?

**Creatine can convert into creatinine, changing serum creatinine without an equivalent change in measured filtration.** Creatinine-derived eGFR assumes typical creatinine generation; supplementation can complicate the estimate. **Do not automatically dismiss abnormal kidney labs as an artifact** without other clinical assessment (PMIDs 10893433, 42507286).

> **Myth:** "My creatinine is high on creatine, so my kidneys are struggling."
> **Fact:** Higher creatinine can reflect changed creatinine production rather than true filtration loss; persistent or concerning abnormalities still need appropriate medical assessment.

## Can a cystatin C test help interpret creatine-related lab changes?

**Sometimes.** Because its production does not directly depend on creatine-to-creatinine conversion, cystatin C can provide complementary information, although it has other influences (PMID 18188581).

## Should I tell my doctor I take creatine before blood work?

**Yes.** Not because it's dangerous, but because an uninformed creatinine reading can trigger unnecessary worry, repeat testing, or misdiagnosis. One sentence — "I supplement creatine monohydrate" — lets the clinician interpret the labs correctly, potentially ordering cystatin C instead.

## Did a 1998 Lancet article prove that creatine damages kidneys?

**No.** Pritchard and Kalra described a single patient in a case letter, not a randomized test of causation (PMID 9643752).

## What conditions complicated the 1998 creatine case?

**Pre-existing focal segmental glomerulosclerosis and prior cyclosporine exposure complicate causal interpretation.** The report raises a concern but cannot by itself establish a generalized renal risk (PMID 9643752).

## Are there any real case reports of creatine kidney injury?

**Published case reports exist, with substantial limitations for causal attribution:** one 1999 NEJM case of interstitial nephritis in a healthy 20-year-old that resolved after stopping (Koshy et al.); a 2006 case with acute interstitial nephritis in a patient taking **multiple other bodybuilding supplements** (Thorsteinsdottir et al., PMID 17046619); a 2012 case after an aggressive 20 g/day loading protocol, resolved with corticosteroids. Against decades of controlled null data, this is what a weak, unconfirmed signal looks like.

## Is creatine proven safe for chronic kidney disease?

**No.** Evidence remains insufficient across CKD severities, co-medications and long durations, and healthy-athlete findings cannot resolve those questions (PMID 42507286).

## Is type 2 diabetes the same as chronic kidney disease?

**No.** The Gualano 2011 trial studied selected adults with type 2 diabetes and cannot establish safety for every person with diabetic kidney disease (PMID 20976468).

## Should people with kidney disease take creatine?

**Evidence in established chronic kidney disease is insufficient to claim safety across disease stages and treatments.** Limited renal-population trials do not justify extrapolating healthy-adult findings to CKD, transplant, or nephrotoxic medications. Discuss individual decisions with a nephrologist.

## Do creatine safety trials measure urine protein and albumin?

**Some do.** Selected trials and the 2026 meta-analysis found no significant pooled injury signal on these markers, but normal urine protein does not rule out kidney stones (PMID 42507286).

## Can creatine cause kidney stones?

**A causal kidney-stone link has not been established, but the available studies were not designed to exclude every stone-related risk.** Individual history matters; urine albumin or protein testing is not itself a kidney-stone study.

## Myths, one by one

## Does creatine cause exertional heat stroke?

**A causal association has not been established in reviewed controlled studies.** Heat stroke can result from training conditions, dehydration and dangerous weight-cutting regardless of supplement use (PMID 10999421).

## Does creatine dehydrate you or cause cramps?

**The opposite is better supported: creatine increases intracellular water retention, and controlled trials find cramping and dehydration rates no higher — sometimes lower — than placebo.** This myth likely descends from the same 1998-era confusion as the kidney scare.

## Is creatine a steroid?

**No.** Creatine is a nitrogenous organic acid the body synthesizes from arginine, glycine, and methionine, stored mostly in muscle as phosphocreatine. It is not a hormone, not an anabolic steroid, and doesn't behave like one. It is permitted by WADA and not banned in collegiate or professional sports.

## Has creatine been proven to cause baldness by raising DHT?

**No randomized clinical evidence cited here establishes hair loss caused by creatine.** A hormone-marker observation is not the same as a demonstrated clinical outcome (PMID 36986197).

## Does creatine cause hair loss?

**No established causal link.** This traces to a single small 2009 rugby-player study that found a DHT increase within the normal range — DHT wasn't even the primary endpoint, hair loss wasn't measured, and the finding hasn't been replicated as a clinical outcome. An unreplicated hormone wiggle is not a baldness mechanism.

## Does creatine stunt growth or shrink testicles?

**Neither has any evidentiary basis.** No mechanism, no trials, no case series. These appear to be pure steroid-confusion spillover.

## Were the wrestler deaths caused by creatine?

**A causal role for creatine was not established.** The widely cited deaths involved extreme weight-cutting and heat-related risks (PMID 10999421).

## Has anyone died from creatine?

**Three widely discussed wrestler deaths occurred in the context of dangerous weight-cutting, dehydration and heat exposure; a causal role for creatine was not established.** This does not mean no risk could ever occur under other conditions (Poortmans & Francaux, 2000, PMID 10999421).

## Do doctors still worry about creatine and kidneys?

**Many reviews are reassuring in healthy adults, but clinicians may appropriately investigate abnormal laboratory results or exercise caution with kidney disease.** A 2023 narrative review by Gualano's group (PMID 36986197) states it directly: case reports and animal studies suggest concern, but "cumulative evidence from independent, randomized controlled trials clearly show this is not the case." What persists is folk caution, not literature caution.

## What creatine actually does (efficacy, briefly)

This page is about safety, but the safety question only matters because creatine *works* — it's among the most studied ergogenic aids in history. The efficacy literature (including International Society of Sports Nutrition position stands) supports creatine monohydrate for increasing muscle phosphocreatine stores, improving high-intensity exercise performance, and augmenting lean-mass gains with resistance training. Effect sizes vary by individual, training status, and protocol — and nothing here is a training recommendation.

The safety relevance: **Substantial sports-performance evidence coexists with gaps in long-duration, high-risk and formulation-specific safety data.**

## Forms, dosing, and practical questions

## What is creatine monohydrate?

**Creatine bound to a water molecule — the form used in nearly all safety and efficacy trials.** When this page says "creatine is safe," it means creatine monohydrate specifically.

## Is creatine HCl proven safer for kidneys than monohydrate?

**No convincing comparative renal-safety evidence establishes superiority.** Monohydrate's safety findings should not be casually generalized to newer formulations (PMID 36986197).

## Is creatine ethyl ester equivalent to monohydrate for lab results?

**Not necessarily.** Different formulations may produce different creatinine measurements, so their pharmacokinetic effects should not be presumed identical (PMID 10893433).

## Is monohydrate the best form?

**Creatine monohydrate has the most extensive safety data, but the longest published follow-up should not be mistaken for a lifetime safety guarantee.** Creatine HCl, ethyl ester, buffered ("kre-alkalyn"), and other variants have far less studied — and sometimes different — pharmacokinetics. Novel forms don't inherit monohydrate's evidence base by branding.

## What doses have been studied?

**3–10 g/day in many trials, with higher short loading phases in some protocols; a **21-month open-label athletic cohort** (Kreider 2003, PMID 12701816) and observational data extending to five years (Poortmans & Francaux, 1999, PMID 10449011) are not equivalent to decades-long randomized safety follow-up.** These are *studied* doses, not recommendations — individual needs vary and this page doesn't prescribe.

## Does cycling creatine prevent kidney harm?

**There is no established evidence that scheduled cycling prevents kidney injury.** Reviewed trials do not demonstrate a renal advantage to cycling (PMID 31375416).

## Do you need to load? Cycle? Time it?

**Loading phases have been studied, and cycling has not been shown to protect the kidneys.** The long-term open-label and shorter randomized studies assessed different protocols and do not establish a single ideal regimen. Timing relative to workouts shows small, inconsistent differences; consistency matters more than timing.

## What happens when you stop?

**Muscle creatine gradually returns to baseline over ~4–6 weeks. Strength and water-weight gains associated with supplementation fade accordingly.** There's no withdrawal syndrome — creatine isn't a drug of dependence.

## Is early creatine weight gain the same as fat gain?

**Not usually.** Early gains frequently involve increased body water and stored creatine rather than additional fat mass (PMID 36986197).

## Does creatine cause bloating or weight gain?

**Expect 1–2 kg of early weight gain — that's intracellular water in muscle, not fat.** GI discomfort is usually a dose-fractionation issue (splitting a large single dose) rather than a creatine problem per se.

## Special populations and interactions

## Has creatine kidney safety been studied equally in women and men?

**No.** Available trial populations differ, with fewer data in some groups; a pooled finding is not equally strong evidence for every age and sex.

## Can people who are pregnant or breastfeeding assume creatine is safe?

**No.** Controlled human safety evidence is insufficient to justify a general recommendation in pregnancy or lactation.

## Is creatine safe for teenagers? Women? Older adults?

**Creatine has been studied in adults of different ages, with less representative safety information for adolescents, pregnancy, and some medically complex populations.** Absence of a detected adverse-event signal in small or short studies is not proof of safety for every group. Anyone under 18 should involve a parent and a qualified clinician rather than relying on internet dosing advice.

## Does creatine cause high blood pressure?

**A consistent increase has not been demonstrated across cited studies.** Rare or population-specific effects remain incompletely assessed (PMID 36986197).

## Does creatine affect the liver or blood pressure?

**No consistent liver-injury or blood-pressure harm signal emerged in the cited controlled literature, but rare events and high-risk populations remain incompletely studied.** In a 21-month **open-label** collegiate-athlete comparison, Kreider and colleagues found no clinically significant group differences in a 69-marker panel; this was not a long-term randomized trial (PMID 12701816).

## Can creatine safely be combined with nephrotoxic medications?

**No blanket safety claim is justified.** Medication-specific and kidney-disease risks require clinician assessment.

## Should someone taking diuretics consult a clinician before using creatine?

**Yes, when fluid or electrolyte balance matters clinically.** A proven specific interaction is not established, but comorbidity and co-medications can materially change risks.

## Does creatine interact with medications?

**Clinical interaction evidence is limited, not universally negative.** People taking potentially nephrotoxic medications, diuretics, or medicines affecting fluid and electrolyte balance should have their clinical circumstances reviewed with a pharmacist or prescriber. Do not extrapolate healthy-adult trial results to medication-stressed kidneys.

---

## The verdict

**Decades of controlled evidence do not show a consistent kidney-harm signal in generally healthy adults using studied creatine-monohydrate regimens.** Creatinine-based eGFR changes can differ from directly measured filtration, but an abnormal test should never automatically be ignored.

What remains genuinely unknown: established kidney disease (limited, heterogeneous evidence — precaution stands), multi-decade continuous use beyond 5 years (no data — absence of evidence, not evidence of absence), and novel creatine formulations (different molecules, different evidence requirements).
