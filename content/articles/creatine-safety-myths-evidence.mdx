**Important safety notice**
This page is an educational evidence review, not medical advice. People with kidney disease, on nephrotoxic medications, or with abnormal kidney labs should discuss any supplement with their clinician. Tell your doctor you take creatine before kidney blood work — it changes the numbers, not necessarily the kidneys.

---

> **Editorial status:** Owner-authorized AI scientific editorial review. Human scientific approval remains required before publication; safety results in healthy adults do not automatically apply to chronic kidney disease.

## Key numbers — the citable core

- **26 studies / 1,036 participants:** the July 2026 meta-analysis found higher serum creatinine and lower *creatinine-derived* eGFR, but not a statistically significant change in **51Cr-EDTA-measured GFR** (de Souza Almeida et al., 2026, DOI 10.1007/s11255-026-05287-x).
- **96.1 vs 96.4 mL/min/1.73 m²:** reported measured-GFR group means in the 2011 Gualano trial in selected people with type 2 diabetes (PMID 20976468).
- **Cystatin C, not measured GFR:** Gualano 2008 assessed cystatin C in 18 men over 12 weeks (PMID 18188581).
- **1998:** publication of an individual kidney case letter (Pritchard and Kalra, PMID 9643752), not a randomized study establishing harm.
- **2019 / 2026:** a systematic review (PMID 31375416) and a larger meta-analysis in 2026 summarize the accumulating renal evidence.


---

## The 1998 letter that started it all

**The frequently cited 1998 Lancet report was a clinical case letter, not an experiment showing that creatine causes kidney damage in healthy people** (Pritchard and Kalra, PMID 9643752). The reported patient already had glomerular kidney disease and had received cyclosporine. Such confounders complicate causal attribution without making the clinical observation irrelevant.

Separate contemporaneous reports of three U.S. collegiate wrestler deaths were linked to severe dehydration and weight cutting rather than proven creatine toxicity. Poortmans and Francaux reviewed creatine safety concerns in 2000 (PMID 10999421).

> **Myth:** "The 1998 Lancet paper proved that creatine harms healthy kidneys."
> **Fact:** It was a case report with pre-existing illness, not an RCT, and cannot establish a general causal risk.

## The kidney question: what the trials actually show

## Can creatine cause permanent kidney damage in healthy adults?

**No permanent harm was demonstrated in the healthy adults and follow-up periods studied, but this does not prove lifelong zero risk.** (PMIDs 20976468, 23680457.)

## Can creatine raise blood creatinine without kidney damage?

**Yes.** Supplementation can affect creatinine generation without corresponding direct-GFR change (PMID 10893433; de Souza Almeida et al., 2026).

## Is creatine bad for your kidneys?

**Kidney harm is not consistently observed in controlled studies of generally healthy adults taking creatine monohydrate at studied regimens.** This does not rule out rare outcomes, very long exposure, established renal disease or unusual medication combinations (PMID 31375416; de Souza Almeida et al., 2026).

## Which trials directly measured kidney filtration after creatine?

**Gualano 2011 and Lugaresi 2013** used 51Cr-EDTA clearance. Gualano 2008 used cystatin C, not direct clearance (PMIDs 20976468, 23680457, 18188581).

## What did the July 2026 creatine kidney meta-analysis find?

**The 26-study/1,036-person synthesis found increased serum creatinine and lower creatinine-derived eGFR, with no significant decline in 51Cr-EDTA GFR.** (de Souza Almeida et al., 2026.)

## Is cystatin C the same as directly measured GFR?

**No.** Cystatin C is a serum filtration marker, not the same procedure as directly measuring GFR with a tracer (PMID 18188581).

## Has creatine been tested with measured GFR — not just creatinine?

**Yes, in Gualano 2011 and Lugaresi 2013 using 51Cr-EDTA clearance** (PMIDs 20976468 and 23680457). **Gualano 2008 measured cystatin C, not direct GFR** (PMID 18188581). Vilar Neto 2020 investigated urine KIM-1 and MCP-1 injury biomarkers in a short randomized trial (PMID 32670557). These different methods help separate creatinine-related artifacts from loss of filtration.

## What is the difference between creatine and creatinine?

**Creatine supports cellular energy storage; creatinine is a metabolic breakdown product used in kidney tests.** (PMID 10893433.)

## What does eGFR estimate in a creatine user?

**Creatinine-based eGFR estimates kidney filtration and can change when creatinine production changes.** (de Souza Almeida et al., 2026.)

## Should a low eGFR while taking creatine be ignored?

**No.** An abnormal kidney result needs medical interpretation; supplementation is only one possible explanation.

## Why does my creatinine go up on creatine — and does it mean damage?

**Creatine can increase creatinine generation, and creatinine-based eGFR can therefore change without the same change in measured filtration.** That is an important *possible* explanation, not grounds to disregard abnormal kidney tests. Symptoms, medical history, repeat studies, urine protein and cystatin C may be clinically relevant (PMID 10893433; de Souza Almeida et al., 2026).

> **Myth:** "A lower creatinine-estimated eGFR proves creatine damaged the kidneys."
> **Fact:** Measured GFR and complementary markers are needed when creatinine production may have changed; abnormal results still require appropriate medical assessment.

## Can cystatin C help interpret creatine-related kidney lab changes?

**Sometimes.** Cystatin C provides a complementary estimate less directly tied to creatine breakdown; other confounders remain possible (PMID 18188581).

## Should I tell my doctor I take creatine before blood work?

**Yes.** Supplementation can alter the interpretation of blood creatinine; clinicians can consider prior values, urine findings and other tests if appropriate.

## Did the 1998 Lancet letter prove creatine harms kidneys?

**No.** The 1998 report was a case letter, not a controlled causal experiment (PMID 9643752).

## What complicated the 1998 creatine kidney case?

**The patient had pre-existing glomerular kidney disease and cyclosporine exposure**, complicating attribution (PMID 9643752; Poortmans & Francaux, 2000).

## Are there any real case reports of creatine kidney injury?

**Yes, case reports exist, but they cannot establish the incidence of kidney injury or causation on their own.** Pre-existing disease, other supplements, training and medication exposures complicate many reports (Pritchard and Kalra, PMID 9643752; additional case literature is listed in the references).

## Is creatine proven safe in chronic kidney disease?

**No general safety claim is established across CKD stages.** Nephrology advice is appropriate before supplementation.

## Does evidence from type 2 diabetes apply to every kidney disease?

**No.** A selected trial in type 2 diabetes does not prove safety across all forms of chronic kidney disease (PMID 20976468).

## Should people with kidney disease take creatine?

**Healthy-adult studies do not establish safety across the stages of chronic kidney disease.** A nephrologist should evaluate kidney function, co-medications and the intended reason for using it; evidence remains insufficient for a universal yes or no.

## Do studies check urine protein and albumin in creatine users?

**Some do.** Albuminuria and proteinuria are complementary injury markers, not kidney-stone tests; the 2026 meta-analysis reported no consistent adverse signal (de Souza Almeida et al., 2026).

## Can creatine cause kidney stones?

**No consistent causal link has been established in the evidence reviewed, but most supplementation trials were not designed to measure kidney-stone incidence.** People with a stone history need individualized medical advice.

## Myths, one by one

## Does creatine cause heat stroke during exercise?

**No causal heatstroke signal is established from the controlled creatine evidence.** Heat illness risks from environment and severe weight cutting remain serious (PMID 10999421).

## Does creatine dehydrate you or cause cramps?

**The opposite is better supported: creatine increases intracellular water retention, and controlled trials find cramping and dehydration rates no higher — sometimes lower — than placebo.** This myth likely descends from the same 1998-era confusion as the kidney scare.

## Is creatine a steroid?

**No.** Creatine is a nitrogenous organic acid the body synthesizes from arginine, glycine, and methionine, stored mostly in muscle as phosphocreatine. It is not a hormone, not an anabolic steroid, and doesn't behave like one. It is permitted by WADA and not banned in collegiate or professional sports.

## Has creatine been shown to cause balding through DHT?

**No clinical baldness outcome has been established as caused by creatine.** A hormone observation alone is not proof of hair loss (PMID 36986197).

## Does creatine cause hair loss?

**No established causal link.** This traces to a single small 2009 rugby-player study that found a DHT increase within the normal range — DHT wasn't even the primary endpoint, hair loss wasn't measured, and the finding hasn't been replicated as a clinical outcome. An unreplicated hormone wiggle is not a baldness mechanism.

## Does creatine stunt growth or shrink testicles?

**Neither has any evidentiary basis.** No mechanism, no trials, no case series. These appear to be pure steroid-confusion spillover.

## Were the 1998 wrestler deaths caused by creatine?

**A causative role for creatine was not established.** The cases involved dehydration and heat illness during weight cutting (PMID 10999421).

## Has anyone died from creatine?

**A causal role for creatine in the widely cited collegiate wrestler deaths was not established.** Extreme dehydration and heat illness in weight cutting were implicated; this is not proof of impossibility of all future harms (Poortmans and Francaux, PMID 10999421).

## Do doctors still worry about creatine and kidneys?

**Reviews are generally reassuring for healthy adults, but clinicians appropriately assess pre-existing disease and abnormal renal tests.** A 2023 narrative review by Gualano's group (PMID 36986197) states it directly: case reports and animal studies suggest concern, but "cumulative evidence from independent, randomized controlled trials clearly show this is not the case." What persists is folk caution, not literature caution.

## What creatine actually does (efficacy, briefly)

This page is about safety, but the safety question only matters because creatine *works* — it's among the most studied ergogenic aids in history. The efficacy literature (including International Society of Sports Nutrition position stands) supports creatine monohydrate for increasing muscle phosphocreatine stores, improving high-intensity exercise performance, and augmenting lean-mass gains with resistance training. Effect sizes vary by individual, training status, and protocol — and nothing here is a training recommendation.

The safety relevance: **the compound with the best efficacy evidence in sports nutrition also has the best long-term safety evidence.** That combination is rare.

## Forms, dosing, and practical questions

## What is creatine monohydrate?

**Creatine bound to a water molecule — the form used in nearly all safety and efficacy trials.** When this page says "creatine is safe," it means creatine monohydrate specifically.

## Is creatine HCl proven safer for kidneys than monohydrate?

**No.** Kidney-safety superiority of creatine HCl over monohydrate is not demonstrated in comparative human trials (PMID 36986197).

## Does creatine ethyl ester have the same kidney-lab effects?

**Not necessarily.** Formulation differences may alter metabolite behavior; do not assume equivalent renal lab effects without direct studies (PMID 10893433).

## Is monohydrate the best form?

**It's the only form with decades of safety data.** Creatine HCl, ethyl ester, buffered ("kre-alkalyn"), and other variants have far less studied — and sometimes different — pharmacokinetics. Novel forms don't inherit monohydrate's evidence base by branding.

## What doses have been studied?

**3–10 g/day in most trials; 20 g/day short loading phases in some; up to ~21 months continuously in controlled data and 5 years observationally.** These are *studied* doses, not recommendations — individual needs vary and this page doesn't prescribe.

## Does cycling creatine protect the kidneys?

**There is no demonstrated kidney-protective benefit from cycling.** (PMID 31375416.)

## Do you need to load? Cycle? Time it?

**Loading (20 g/day for ~5 days) saturates muscle faster; 3–5 g/day gets there in ~3–4 weeks. Cycling has no evidentiary basis — the long-term trials didn't cycle.** Timing relative to workouts shows small, inconsistent differences; consistency matters more than timing.

## What happens when you stop?

**Muscle creatine gradually returns to baseline over ~4–6 weeks. Strength and water-weight gains associated with supplementation fade accordingly.** There's no withdrawal syndrome — creatine isn't a drug of dependence.

## Does early creatine weight gain mean body-fat gain?

**No.** Early weight gain often involves fluid retention in muscle, not proven body-fat increase (PMID 36986197).

## Does creatine cause bloating or weight gain?

**Expect 1–2 kg of early weight gain — that's intracellular water in muscle, not fat.** GI discomfort is usually a dose-fractionation issue (splitting a large single dose) rather than a creatine problem per se.

## Special populations and interactions

## Is the kidney safety evidence equally strong for women and men?

**No.** Research groups are not equally representative across sex and age, limiting generalization.

## Can pregnancy or breastfeeding safety be assumed?

**No.** Human evidence is insufficient for broad pregnancy or breastfeeding safety assurances.

## Is creatine safe for teenagers? Women? Older adults?

**The trial base is overwhelmingly adult men, with growing data in women, adolescents in sport settings, and older adults — no population-specific safety signal has emerged, but the evidence is thinnest for adolescents.** Anyone under 18 should involve a parent and ideally a clinician or sports-medicine professional rather than dosing off internet advice.

## Is creatine proven to increase blood pressure?

**A consistent blood-pressure harm signal has not been established by the reviewed trials.** This is not a guarantee for all health conditions (PMID 36986197).

## Does creatine affect the liver or blood pressure?

**The cited trials do not establish a consistent liver or blood-pressure harm signal, but are not large enough to exclude all rare events or medically complex cases.** The safety monitoring in long-term trials (including 69-marker panels in Kreider 2003) found no differences vs non-users.

## Is creatine proven safe with kidney-toxic drugs?

**No universal guarantee applies.** Existing kidney disease and medication-specific risks require clinical review.

## Should people taking diuretics discuss creatine with a clinician?

**Yes, particularly when medical fluid or electrolyte management matters.** Evidence for a specific interaction is limited.

## Does creatine interact with medications?

**No well-established major interactions, but two common-sense cautions: nephrotoxic drugs (the kidney question is settled for healthy people, not for drug-stressed kidneys) and diuretics (theoretical fluid-balance interplay, unproven).** As always, a pharmacist beats a forum.

---

## The verdict

**Creatine monohydrate has not shown a consistent kidney-injury signal at studied regimens in generally healthy adults.** Creatinine-based eGFR must be distinguished from directly measured filtration, while persistent kidney abnormalities deserve clinical care.

What remains genuinely unknown: established kidney disease (evidence insufficient across stages; precaution stands), multi-decade continuous use beyond 5 years (no data — absence of evidence, not evidence of absence), and novel creatine formulations (different molecules, different evidence requirements).
