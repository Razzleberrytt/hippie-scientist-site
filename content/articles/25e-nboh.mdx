# 25E-NBOH

## Quick answer

**25E-NBOH is an emerging psychedelic N-benzyl phenethylamine with very thin human evidence.**

A severe published poisoning involved serotonin-syndrome features and loss of consciousness. Crucially, the powder had been labeled as a different compound.

## The mislabeling case

A 23-year-old presented with severe intoxication after using powder labeled “25I-NBOH.”

High-resolution toxicology found **25E-NBOH and MDPHP**; 25I-NBOH was specifically tested for and not detected.

This is an unusually clear demonstration that an RC label can be chemically wrong.

## New 2026 metabolism research

A 2026 study mapped extensive 25E-NBOH metabolism in experimental systems and explicitly noted that pharmacology and toxicology remain insufficiently characterized.

That work improves analytical detection more than it answers human-safety questions.

## Bottom line

25E-NBOH is a high-uncertainty psychedelic where the strongest human lesson currently comes from a poisoning case involving a mislabeled product.

## What the evidence can and cannot establish

For **25E-NBOH**, certainty depends heavily on study type. Analytical chemistry can tell investigators what was present. A clinical case can show that serious toxicity is possible, but a single case cannot give an incidence rate or a universal threshold. Animal and receptor studies can clarify serotonergic or related mechanisms, but those experiments do not translate cleanly into a human dose comparison. When these evidence layers disagree or leave a gap, the gap is more informative than a confident-sounding estimate.

## Why familiar psychedelic effects do not guarantee a familiar risk profile

Visual effects, altered perception, stimulation, or a recognizable “psychedelic” headspace can occur across chemically different drugs. Those similarities do not establish equivalent cardiovascular effects, seizure risk, duration, active metabolites, or interaction profiles. 25E-NBOH should therefore be judged by evidence specific to the compound or its well-defined chemical family, not by a forum statement that it feels like LSD, psilocybin, or another familiar drug.

The same caution applies to potency rankings. A receptor-affinity number describes a particular experimental system. It is not a clinically validated conversion factor and should never be used to infer a human-equivalent amount.

## Safety signals worth taking seriously

A psychedelic label should not cause people to dismiss objective medical warning signs. Seizures, severe hyperthermia, chest pain, collapse, prolonged unconsciousness, extreme agitation, or new focal neurologic symptoms need medical evaluation regardless of what the product was supposed to contain. These signs can reflect direct toxicity, an interaction, a substituted compound, or another medical problem.

Polysubstance exposure also makes retrospective interpretation difficult. If alcohol, stimulants, sedatives, or other serotonergic drugs were involved, a case may still be important without proving that 25E-NBOH alone caused the event. This page keeps that distinction explicit rather than forcing a single-drug explanation.

## What would make this evidence stronger?

The main limitation for 25E-NBOH is not a lack of anecdotes; it is a lack of denominator-based and controlled human data. Case reports can establish possibility, while surveillance can establish patterns, but neither automatically defines probability for an individual exposure. Better evidence would pair verified chemical identity with timing, serial vital signs, validated concentrations, co-exposure testing, and prospective follow-up.

Until that exists, this page intentionally avoids “safe range,” “best dose,” or universal potency claims. Those would imply a precision the literature does not provide.
