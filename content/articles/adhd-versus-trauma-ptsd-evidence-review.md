## When concentration problems have more than one explanation

A person who cannot follow a conversation may be distracted by unrelated thoughts, monitoring for danger, exhausted after nightmares, dissociating, or dealing with several of these experiences together. The outward behavior—losing track—does not reveal the mechanism. This is why “ADHD or trauma?” is often a more complicated question than an online symptom comparison suggests. [1,2]

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, or ADHD, is a neurodevelopmental condition. Post-traumatic stress disorder, or PTSD, is a trauma-related disorder with a characteristic pattern of symptoms after qualifying exposure. Trauma exposure itself is not identical to PTSD. Many people experience trauma without developing the disorder, and some people with ADHD also have PTSD. [2,4,5]

This article is a critical narrative synthesis of published research and guidelines, **not a new statistical meta-analysis**. It focuses on adult differential assessment and co-occurrence. It does not claim that all childhood adversity causes ADHD, that every distracted trauma survivor has PTSD, or that one diagnosis makes the other impossible.

## Shared symptoms are the starting point

Concentration difficulties, restlessness, disturbed sleep and problems managing emotions can appear in several clinical conditions. Someone may interrupt because they are impulsive, because they feel unsafe and need an immediate answer, or because a situation has become unusually stressful. The same behavior can have different functions on different occasions. [2]

A useful assessment goes beyond counting overlapping symptoms. It asks when they began, where they occur, whether there are periods of relative absence, and how they relate to reminders, sleep, substances or medications. Depression, anxiety, medical problems and sleep disorders can also affect attention. A trauma explanation should be investigated carefully rather than assumed from a difficult biography. [4,5]

Equally, a history of good grades does not by itself settle the ADHD question. People may have benefited from strong external structure or worked disproportionately hard to compensate. Assessment concerns the developmental pattern and impairment, not whether a person fits a stereotype of poor academic performance. [4]

## What the developmental history contributes

NICE ADHD guidance calls for a full clinical and psychosocial assessment, developmental and psychiatric history, observer information and impairment across settings. Diagnosis should not rely solely on a rating scale. In adults, the clinician looks for evidence that typical manifestations began in childhood and continued, while considering alternative and coexisting conditions. [4]

This does not require a person to remember every year accurately. School reports, earlier evaluations or observations from someone who knew them can sometimes help, with consent. Lack of surviving records should be discussed openly. A missing report card is missing information, not proof of absence; confident reconstruction without evidence is not a substitute either.

The most useful history may distinguish longstanding organizational difficulties from a later change. For example, repeated childhood trouble finishing routine tasks may predate a traumatic event, while nightmares and cue-linked fear may have started afterward. That pattern could support investigating two problems rather than explaining the entire history with whichever diagnosis was considered most recently.

## What a PTSD assessment adds

PTSD evaluation examines the trauma exposure and symptoms such as intrusive re-experiencing, avoidance, changes in mood or thinking, and heightened arousal. The precise diagnostic framework matters. Distressing memories alone are not enough, and a clinician must consider duration, impairment and other explanations. [5]

Triggers can clarify the pattern without providing a perfect dividing line. A person might lose concentration especially near a place associated with an assault, or feel on guard during sounds resembling an earlier danger. ADHD difficulties may also become worse in stressful contexts. “Only happens when stressed” and “happens everywhere” are useful descriptions to investigate, not definitive diagnostic rules.

An assessment should not require someone to recount graphic details to strangers or to an online forum. It is possible to ask for trauma-informed care while deciding, with a clinician, what information is necessary and how to discuss it safely.

## What the meta-analysis actually found

Spencer and colleagues' review included 28 eligible studies and computed meta-analyses from 22. PTSD was more common in ADHD samples, and ADHD was more common in PTSD samples. Crucially, the association changed with the comparison group: the PTSD relative risk in ADHD was 3.7 with healthy controls and 1.6 with trauma-exposed controls; the association was not significant with psychiatric controls. [1]

Those estimates are not interchangeable. Comparing a clinical group with healthy volunteers can capture broad psychiatric vulnerability, referral differences and differences in trauma exposure. Comparing with trauma-exposed people asks a narrower question. Relative risk also does not provide an individual's absolute probability without the relevant baseline risk.

“Bidirectional association” describes the observed relationship across the literature; it does not establish that ADHD causes PTSD and PTSD causes childhood-onset ADHD. Cross-sectional information cannot reconstruct every person's causal sequence. Nor does this review demonstrate that treating ADHD prevents PTSD after a future traumatic event. That prevention claim requires a different study design.

## The newer adult review: useful but not a pooled prevalence estimate

A 2025 systematic review included 21 studies of adult ADHD–PTSD comorbidity. The authors did not conduct a meta-analysis because study designs were too heterogeneous. They reported greater functional difficulties when the disorders co-occurred, alongside preliminary treatment observations. [2]

The frequently repeated comorbidity range in that review should not be turned into a universal population prevalence. Samples differ in whether participants started in ADHD clinics, PTSD services or other selected settings. Different denominators answer different questions: “PTSD among adults with ADHD” differs from “ADHD among adults with PTSD.”

This is also why an apparent increase in a particular clinic cannot establish a new epidemic. Referral practice, awareness, diagnostic methods and local patient characteristics can change the observed proportion. The practical conclusion is to assess relevant comorbidity, not to assume it in everyone.

## The family-study evidence

Antshel and colleagues compared adults with ADHD with controls and examined first-degree relatives. Adults with both ADHD and PTSD had more psychiatric comorbidity and poorer quality-of-life ratings than adults with ADHD alone. The family findings supported shared familial vulnerability rather than a simple assumption that every overlap represented diagnostic error. [3]

Family association still does not isolate genes from environment or prove a particular mechanism. Members of families can share experiences, access to care and reporting patterns as well as inherited liability. The study also involved a selected clinical population. It supports taking the overlap seriously while leaving causal questions open.

## Treatment must track separate outcomes

NICE recommendations support evidence-based ADHD care and trauma-focused treatments for PTSD. Their existence does not establish one universal combined-treatment sequence. A clinician may need to address immediate safety, sleep, substance-related problems and barriers to attendance while planning care for both conditions. [4,5]

In a randomized crossover pilot among veterans with both ADHD and PTSD, atomoxetine showed a modest benefit on one ADHD symptom measure. PTSD symptoms and quality-of-life measures did not significantly improve. Other ADHD and cognitive outcomes did not reach conventional statistical significance. [6]

That is a narrower result than “atomoxetine treats ADHD and trauma.” A small feasibility study can help justify further research without establishing broad effectiveness in civilians, women, people with different comorbidities or long-term care. Absence of serious adverse events in a small trial is also insufficient to establish safety for every patient.

Medication response should not be used as a home diagnostic experiment. Do not take another person's medication or change a prescribed regimen to see which diagnosis fits. Bring benefits, adverse effects and timing to the prescriber; those observations inform care without becoming a definitive diagnostic test.

## A practical comparison for an appointment

| Information to bring | ADHD assessment question | Trauma/PTSD assessment question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Earlier functioning | Were attention and activity difficulties present developmentally? | Was there a later change after exposure? |
| Situations and triggers | How broad is impairment across settings? | Are reminders linked to intrusions, avoidance or arousal? |
| Sleep pattern | Is sleep disruption worsening daytime symptoms? | Are nightmares or fear contributing? |
| Treatment history | Which attention and functioning outcomes changed? | Which trauma-related outcomes changed? |
| Current priorities | What support makes daily tasks manageable? | What increases safety and access to treatment? |

No row independently decides the diagnosis. The table helps avoid a vague conversation in which every difficulty is described simply as poor focus.

## Reducing shame without inventing certainty

A person whose symptoms have been misunderstood may understandably want one explanation that makes the whole story coherent. But an accurate formulation can include several contributing processes and can change as better information emerges. Uncertainty is not a judgment that someone is exaggerating or failing.

For example, a practical plan might address reminders and avoidance while also using organizational accommodations. Whether each intervention helps should be tracked. Improvement does not require proving that every missed appointment had one exclusive biological cause. The person remains responsible for their actions while deserving care suited to the barriers they actually face.

If there is suicidal intent, immediate danger, severe confusion or inability to care for basic needs, seek urgent assessment. In the United States, call or text 988 for a mental-health crisis and use emergency services for immediate danger. A routine ADHD-versus-PTSD comparison should never delay that support.
