# Phenazepam: Complete Long-Acting Benzodiazepine Toxicology, Dependence & Forensic Monograph

> **Emergency safety:** Call emergency services for inability to wake, abnormal or slowed breathing, repeated vomiting with reduced consciousness, severe confusion, collapse, seizure, or suspected mixed-drug poisoning. Phenazepam can persist for a long time, and opioids, alcohol, and other sedatives can greatly increase danger. Severe benzodiazepine withdrawal can also cause seizures or delirium [10-13].

## Quick answer

**Phenazepam is a long-acting 1,4-benzodiazepine developed in the Soviet Union and used medically in Russia and some neighboring countries.** It later became important in Western toxicology because internet and gray-market availability produced prolonged intoxication, impaired driving, hospitalizations, and deaths [1-5,14].

Unlike many newer RC benzodiazepines, phenazepam has direct human pharmacokinetic data. An older comparative study reported a human elimination half-life of about **60 hours**, consistent with its ability to accumulate and to produce impairment that can extend well beyond the period a person expects [6].

Phenazepam therefore illustrates a central harm-reduction lesson: **long duration can magnify ordinary benzodiazepine risks.** Repeated exposure may accumulate; impairment can persist after subjective sedation improves; and withdrawal can begin later than expected after stopping [2,3,6,13].

## History: from pharmaceutical to gray-market benzodiazepine

Phenazepam was developed in the Soviet Union in the 1970s and entered medical use in 1978 according to DEA's current drug-information sheet [14].

It later became accessible through internet markets and appeared in Western clinical and forensic literature. Early reviews documented recreational use, serious adverse events, and deaths [1,4]. One feature of that era was sale as bulk powder or nonstandard preparations, which removed pharmaceutical safeguards around tablet content and labeling.

DEA reports that U.S. forensic laboratories first recorded phenazepam in NFLIS-Drug in 2005 and continued to receive reports in later years, although counts fluctuated substantially over time [14]. Those detection counts describe submitted forensic drug items—not population prevalence.

## Chemistry and pharmacology

Phenazepam is a 1,4-benzodiazepine that acts at the benzodiazepine site of GABA-A receptors, enhancing inhibitory GABAergic signaling [14].

Clinically relevant effects include:
- sedation and hypnosis;
- anxiolysis;
- muscle relaxation;
- impaired coordination and reaction time;
- memory impairment and amnesia;
- reduced behavioral inhibition.

The same mechanism that produces sedation also explains the danger of combining phenazepam with other CNS depressants.

Phenazepam also has a hydroxylated metabolite, 3-hydroxyphenazepam. Comparative pharmacokinetic work found important species differences, including much lower measurable metabolite exposure in humans than in several studied animals [6]. Animal metabolite findings therefore should not be imported directly into human potency claims.

## Pharmacokinetics: why persistence matters

Human pharmacokinetic data reported a phenazepam elimination half-life of approximately **60 hours** [6]. DEA's current fact sheet likewise describes a half-life up to about 60 hours [14].

A long half-life creates several practical safety consequences:

- drug from prior exposure can remain when another exposure occurs;
- subjective alertness can return before elimination is complete;
- impairment can persist into the next day or longer;
- repeated exposure can accumulate;
- withdrawal after dependence may be delayed.

The exact duration in an individual can vary with exposure history, age, liver function, co-medications, and product identity. A half-life is not a countdown timer to sobriety.

## Human toxicity

### Prolonged intoxication

A 2012 UK case described analytically confirmed phenazepam-associated neuropsychiatric toxicity with confusion/disorientation lasting roughly 60 hours [2].

That case cannot define a universal duration, but it demonstrates why phenazepam should not be approached like a short-acting sedative.

### Impaired driving

A 2013 impaired-driving case reported slurred speech and profound psychomotor impairment with phenazepam identified in blood and no other drugs detected [3].

A larger forensic study later examined designer-benzodiazepine concentrations in relation to impairment and again underscored how difficult it is to infer functional state from one concentration alone [7].

### Mass poisoning

A 2024 report described 14 people hospitalized across six hospitals after exposure to a drink containing crushed phenazepam tablets; blood and urine were analyzed with HPLC-MS/MS [9].

This is valuable direct-human evidence that phenazepam can produce severe nonfatal poisoning in a cluster. It does not create a general concentration threshold for all users.

## Fatalities and postmortem interpretation

Phenazepam has been documented in medico-legal deaths [4,5].

The South Scotland series included cases in which phenazepam was considered the sole cause, part of a mixed-drug cause, or an incidental/coexisting finding [5]. That distinction is crucial.

A postmortem concentration cannot be read as a universal lethal concentration because:
- tolerance differs;
- co-drugs differ;
- postmortem redistribution can alter concentrations;
- specimen site and timing matter;
- underlying disease and trauma matter;
- the relationship between antemortem impairment and postmortem concentration is indirect.

The correct forensic question is not “Was phenazepam detected?” but “What role does all available evidence support?”

## Interactions and polysubstance risk

### Opioids

Opioids suppress breathing; benzodiazepines suppress arousal and protective responses. Combined exposure increases overdose risk.

Modern healthcare toxicology studies of designer benzodiazepines have frequently found opioid co-detection [8], and broader 2025-2026 reviews identify polysubstance use as a defining feature of designer-benzodiazepine morbidity and mortality [10-12].

### Alcohol

Alcohol adds sedation, disinhibition, impaired coordination, and respiratory risk. Long phenazepam persistence means alcohol consumed later may still overlap pharmacologically.

### Other sedatives

Other benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, gabapentinoids, sedating antihistamines, and other CNS depressants can add to impairment.

There is no scientifically valid “safe stacking” formula.

## Tolerance, physical dependence, and benzodiazepine use disorder

Phenazepam can produce tolerance and physical dependence, consistent with benzodiazepine pharmacology and its international scheduling history [13,15,16].

These concepts should not be collapsed:

- **Tolerance:** reduced effect from the same exposure after adaptation.
- **Physical dependence:** withdrawal appears when exposure stops or drops substantially.
- **Benzodiazepine use disorder:** impaired control, craving, continued use despite harm, or major functional disruption.

Physical dependence can occur without compulsive use. Conversely, a use disorder is broader than withdrawal alone.

## Withdrawal: why it can be delayed

Phenazepam's long elimination can delay the onset of withdrawal after the last exposure [6,14].

Possible benzodiazepine withdrawal manifestations include:
- rebound anxiety or insomnia;
- tremor and autonomic activation;
- agitation;
- perceptual disturbance;
- confusion or delirium;
- seizures.

There is no evidence-based universal “day 1/day 2/day 3” phenazepam withdrawal calendar.

The 2025 multisociety benzodiazepine tapering guideline advises against abrupt discontinuation in patients likely to be physically dependent and emphasizes individualized clinical supervision [13].

That guideline does not validate a self-directed phenazepam-to-diazepam conversion for gray-market products.

## Treatment and support

### Acute poisoning

Management is primarily supportive:
- airway and breathing assessment;
- circulation and neurologic monitoring;
- evaluation for trauma;
- identification and treatment of co-intoxicants;
- prolonged observation when the clinical course warrants it.

Flumazenil has specialized clinical indications and important seizure/withdrawal risks; it is not a home antidote and should not be treated like naloxone [11].

### Dependence and withdrawal

Repeated phenazepam exposure deserves medically supervised assessment because:
- the molecule can persist for days;
- actual product concentration may be unknown;
- co-dependence on alcohol, opioids, or other sedatives changes risk;
- abrupt discontinuation can be dangerous.

For U.S. support:
- [SAMHSA FindTreatment.gov](https://findtreatment.gov/)
- SAMHSA National Helpline: 1-800-662-HELP (4357)

Seizure, delirium, severe confusion, inability to wake, or abnormal breathing warrants emergency care.

## Drug testing and analytical detection

Phenazepam and related metabolites can be measured using chromatographic mass spectrometry. However, routine benzodiazepine testing is not a universal detector for every non-U.S.-approved or designer compound.

Healthcare studies have shown that patients can have designer benzodiazepines present even when traditional definitive panels would not have identified them [8].

Interpretation should consider:
- parent drug versus metabolites;
- assay scope;
- hydrolysis/preparation method;
- specimen type;
- time since exposure;
- co-detected compounds.

## Counterfeit and product-identity risk

DEA reports phenazepam has appeared as powder, tablets, solutions, and even in preparations mixed with synthetic cannabinoids or applied to other products [14].

That history matters because a consumer label does not guarantee:
- correct molecule;
- correct concentration;
- single-drug composition;
- pharmaceutical manufacturing quality.

Bulk powder historically amplified risk because small measurement errors could translate into much larger exposure, especially with a potent, long-lasting compound.

## Epidemiology and current trends

Phenazepam is no longer a “new” NPS, but it remains relevant because long-acting benzodiazepines continue to appear in forensic and clinical contexts.

DEA reports more than 670 NFLIS-Drug phenazepam reports historically, with a large early-2010s signal and smaller numbers in later years, including reports in 2023 and 2024 [14].

Those counts are not national prevalence estimates. They are forensic submissions from participating laboratories.

## Legal and regulatory history

### United States

DEA's September 2025 phenazepam fact sheet states:

- phenazepam has no accepted medical use in the United States;
- it has appeared in the illicit market;
- **it is not controlled under the federal Controlled Substances Act** [14].

That statement does not preempt stricter state law, and legal status can change.

### International control

WHO recommended phenazepam for international control because of evidence of abuse, dependence, traffic risk, overdose, and some therapeutic usefulness. The UN Commission on Narcotic Drugs placed phenazepam in **Schedule IV of the 1971 Convention on Psychotropic Substances**, with the decision entering into force in November 2016 [15,16].

## Special populations

Phenazepam-specific modern data are limited for pregnancy, breastfeeding, older adults, chronic respiratory disease, and liver/kidney impairment.

The drug's long persistence raises particular concern where:
- falls or delirium would be especially dangerous;
- respiratory reserve is limited;
- multiple sedating medications are used;
- pregnancy or breastfeeding makes fetal/infant exposure relevant;
- impaired driving would endanger others.

## Myths versus evidence

### “Phenazepam is federally scheduled in the U.S. because it is internationally controlled.”
**Incorrect as of the current DEA fact sheet.** DEA states phenazepam is not controlled under the U.S. CSA, while UNODC lists it in Schedule IV of the 1971 Convention [14-16].

### “If the main sedation is gone, the drug is gone.”
**False.** Human pharmacokinetics show prolonged elimination, and impairment can outlast the most obvious subjective effects [2,3,6].

### “A fatal concentration tells you the lethal dose.”
**False.** Postmortem cases show broad context dependence and mixed-drug involvement [5,7].

### “Long-acting means withdrawal is easier.”
**Not necessarily.** Long elimination may delay withdrawal, but severe benzodiazepine withdrawal can still include seizure or delirium [13].

### “Phenazepam is just a historical internet drug.”
**Outdated.** DEA still documented forensic reports in 2023-2024, and a 2024 mass-poisoning report provides recent direct-human evidence [9,14].

## What remains unknown

Important gaps include:
- modern controlled pharmacodynamic studies;
- population variability in half-life;
- concentration-response relationships in tolerant vs non-tolerant users;
- frequency and severity of phenazepam-specific withdrawal;
- best clinical conversion strategy when the gray-market product is uncertain;
- pregnancy and breastfeeding outcomes;
- current U.S. prevalence outside forensic submissions;
- how often products sold as phenazepam contain something else.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long can phenazepam last?
Human pharmacokinetic data report an elimination half-life around 60 hours, and case literature documents prolonged clinical toxicity [2,6]. That does not mean every person's impairment follows the same clock.

### Can phenazepam withdrawal start late?
Yes, delayed onset is pharmacologically plausible because phenazepam is long acting. The exact timing varies and there is no validated universal schedule [6,13].

### Can phenazepam cause death by itself?
Phenazepam has been implicated in deaths, including some cases interpreted as phenazepam-only causes, but many fatal investigations involve multiple drugs. Causality must be assessed case by case [5].

### Is it legal in the United States?
DEA's September 2025 fact sheet states phenazepam is not controlled under the federal CSA. State law can differ and the status can change [14].

### Is it internationally controlled?
Yes. UNODC lists phenazepam in Schedule IV of the 1971 Convention, with control entering into force in 2016 [15,16].

## Bottom line

Phenazepam is unusually well suited to show why **duration is itself a hazard**.

It has direct human pharmacokinetic data, prolonged-intoxication cases, impaired-driving evidence, postmortem series, and recent mass-poisoning evidence. The roughly 60-hour human half-life helps explain accumulation, extended impairment, and delayed withdrawal [2,3,6,9].

The evidence supports strong warnings about mixed depressants, driving, physical dependence, and abrupt cessation. It does **not** support consumer dose tables, a universal “safe blood level,” or a one-size-fits-all withdrawal clock.

Phenazepam's federal U.S. status is also a useful reminder that **legal control and pharmacologic danger are separate questions**: DEA currently describes it as not controlled under the CSA, while UNODC lists it under international Schedule IV control [14-16].
