# Phenazepam: Complete Pharmacology, Toxicology, Dependence, Withdrawal & Forensic Monograph

> **Emergency safety:** A person who cannot be awakened, has slow/irregular breathing, blue or gray lips, choking/gurgling while unresponsive, seizure, severe confusion, collapse, or major trauma after phenazepam or an unknown sedative needs emergency care. Alcohol, opioids and other depressants can greatly magnify sedation and respiratory risk. Naloxone does not reverse phenazepam, but it should be given if opioid exposure is possible.

## Quick answer

**Phenazepam (fenazepam) is a long-acting 1,4-benzodiazepine developed in the former Soviet Union.** Unlike many designer benzodiazepines, it has a genuine medical-use history and direct human pharmacokinetic data. It later became prominent in Western internet drug markets and forensic toxicology.

Its unusually long persistence is central to its risk profile. Human data from early pharmacokinetic work found an elimination half-life of roughly **60 hours** in the studied participants, and modern case literature documents prolonged confusion, amnesia, psychomotor impairment, impaired driving and deaths.

Phenazepam is therefore not well described as merely an “old RC benzo.” It is better understood as a medically developed long-acting benzodiazepine that entered an unregulated market where long duration, bulk-powder sales, product uncertainty and polysubstance use created substantial harm.

## Identity

**Canonical name:** Phenazepam / Fenazepam  
**CAS:** 51753-57-2  
**Class:** 1,4-benzodiazepine  
**Related metabolite:** 3-hydroxyphenazepam  
**Medical history:** anxiolytic/anticonvulsant and other indications in parts of the former Soviet Union  
**U.S. medical approval:** none

UNODC lists phenazepam as a benzodiazepine and sedative/hypnotic, with international Schedule IV control under the 1971 Convention.

## History: from Soviet medicine to internet-market toxicology

Phenazepam was synthesized in the 1970s and became a prescription benzodiazepine in the Soviet Union and successor states. It was used for anxiety and neurological indications and has also been described in treatment contexts involving epilepsy and alcohol withdrawal.

By the late 2000s and early 2010s, phenazepam appeared in Western online drug markets, often as bulk powder. That market transition mattered: pharmaceutical use involves regulated identity and formulation, while bulk-powder sales created a setting where measurement error, uncertain purity and prolonged redosing could produce unexpectedly long intoxication.

The scientific literature from the UK, Scandinavia and North America subsequently shifted toward toxicology, impaired driving and death investigation.

## Pharmacodynamics

Phenazepam is a benzodiazepine-site **positive allosteric modulator of GABA-A receptors**. Like other drugs in this class, it enhances inhibitory GABA signaling rather than acting as a simple direct GABA receptor agonist.

Expected effects include:
- anxiolysis;
- sedation and hypnosis;
- anticonvulsant activity;
- muscle relaxation;
- impaired coordination;
- slowed reaction time;
- impaired memory and judgment.

The same mechanism that explains therapeutic effects also explains tolerance, dependence and dangerous withdrawal after sustained exposure.

## Pharmacokinetics: long persistence is not folklore

Early comparative pharmacokinetic research included human participants. In one study, peak blood concentrations in humans occurred later than in the tested animal species, and the reported human elimination half-life was approximately **60 hours**.

A separate human/animal pharmacokinetic report likewise found very slow human elimination.

These studies are old and small, so they should not be treated as modern population-PK estimates. But they strongly support the clinical observation that phenazepam can persist for days.

### 3-hydroxyphenazepam

3-hydroxyphenazepam is an important metabolite and related benzodiazepine. The extent to which it contributes to phenazepam's effects in humans appears less dramatic than in some animal species, but it remains analytically important.

### CYP3A4

A 2018 translational study using in-silico, in-vitro and patient data identified **CYP3A4** as an important phenazepam-metabolizing enzyme. That provides a mechanistic reason to be cautious about assuming identical kinetics across patients or drug combinations.

## Why intoxication can last for days

A UK case report documented prolonged neuropsychiatric toxicity with confusion and disorientation lasting roughly **60 hours** after analytically confirmed recreational phenazepam exposure.

Long elimination can create several hazards:
- impairment may continue after a person feels partly recovered;
- repeated use can accumulate;
- memory for earlier doses may be unreliable;
- driving or work risk can extend into the following day or longer;
- withdrawal onset may be delayed.

A long half-life does not guarantee a smooth or predictable experience. It mainly increases the time over which mistakes can matter.

## Impaired driving

A published impaired-driving case involved a 24-year-old driver after a crash. Phenazepam was detected in blood and no other relevant drugs were found. Clinical signs included slurred speech and profound psychomotor impairment.

Broader forensic research on designer-benzodiazepine blood concentrations also supports an association between concentrations seen in casework and observed impairment, while emphasizing that tolerance and timing complicate interpretation.

A blood concentration is therefore evidence to interpret in context—not a universal switch between “safe” and “impaired.”

## Acute toxicity and overdose

Phenazepam toxicity can include:
- extreme drowsiness;
- confusion;
- ataxia;
- slurred speech;
- amnesia;
- behavioral disinhibition;
- reduced consciousness;
- prolonged psychomotor impairment.

Respiratory depression is especially concerning when opioids, alcohol or other sedatives are present.

Because phenazepam can persist so long, supportive monitoring may need to continue longer than someone expects based on short-acting benzodiazepines.

## Fatality evidence

Phenazepam has been identified in fatal cases in the UK, Scotland and North America.

One U.S. report involved phenazepam with poppy-seed tea/opioid exposure. Scottish medico-legal series documented phenazepam in drug-related deaths, both alone and with other drugs.

The key scientific point is not that every detection equals causation. It is that phenazepam has enough real-world mortality evidence that it cannot be treated as an innocuous or purely theoretical RC.

## Why there is no single “fatal level”

A postmortem distribution study quantified phenazepam and 3-hydroxyphenazepam across fluids and tissues in 29 cases. Most deaths were not directly attributed to phenazepam, while two had phenazepam as a contributing or certified cause.

Concentrations overlapped across categories. A South Scotland death review similarly found substantial variation.

That means:
- a high concentration may support toxicity but is not self-interpreting;
- tolerance matters;
- co-drugs matter;
- postmortem matrix and preservation matter;
- cause of death requires the entire case.

The site therefore does not publish a “lethal phenazepam blood level.”

## Interactions and polysubstance risk

### Opioids
Benzodiazepines plus opioids increase risk of profound sedation, respiratory depression, coma and death.

### Alcohol
Alcohol can add to sedation, ataxia, amnesia and respiratory compromise.

### Other sedatives
Other benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, gabapentinoids, barbiturates and sedating medications can compound impairment.

### CYP interactions
Because CYP3A4 appears important in phenazepam metabolism, strong inhibitors or inducers could plausibly alter exposure. Direct interaction trials are limited, so specific magnitude predictions are not justified.

## Tolerance

Tolerance can develop with repeated benzodiazepine exposure, but not necessarily equally for every effect.

A person may feel less sedated while still having impaired memory, judgment or coordination. Tolerance to some subjective effects also does not eliminate overdose risk from alcohol/opioid combinations.

## Physical dependence

Regular phenazepam exposure can produce **physical dependence**, meaning the nervous system adapts and withdrawal can occur when exposure falls.

Physical dependence is not identical to addiction. It can occur even in medically directed benzodiazepine treatment.

## Withdrawal

Benzodiazepine withdrawal can include:
- rebound anxiety;
- insomnia;
- tremor;
- sweating and autonomic symptoms;
- agitation;
- perceptual disturbances;
- confusion;
- seizures;
- delirium.

Phenazepam's long persistence can delay onset compared with shorter-acting benzodiazepines. A person may initially feel relatively well after stopping and then deteriorate later.

The 2025 multisociety benzodiazepine-tapering guideline advises against abrupt discontinuation in people likely to be physically dependent. No phenazepam-specific DIY taper schedule has been validated, so this page does not provide one.

## Benzodiazepine use disorder

Warning signs of a broader benzodiazepine use disorder can include:
- inability to cut down;
- craving;
- escalating or compulsive use;
- repeated blackouts;
- use despite injuries or relationship/work harm;
- hazardous driving;
- substantial time spent obtaining or recovering from the drug.

Tolerance or withdrawal alone does not establish the diagnosis.

## Treatment and support

### Acute intoxication
Care focuses on airway, breathing, circulation, mental status, injury and co-exposures.

Flumazenil can reverse benzodiazepine effects in selected clinical situations, but seizure and withdrawal risks make it unsuitable as a universal antidote.

### Dependence
People with likely benzodiazepine dependence should be medically assessed before abrupt cessation. Treatment can include individualized tapering, management of co-occurring anxiety/insomnia and treatment for other substance use.

U.S. readers can use **FindTreatment.gov** and SAMHSA treatment resources.

## Drug testing and toxicology

Phenazepam can be measured with chromatographic mass-spectrometric methods. Analytical work may also include 3-hydroxyphenazepam.

Routine benzodiazepine immunoassays are not designed to quantify every benzodiazepine accurately, and a positive class screen does not identify which benzodiazepine is present.

Forensic interpretation should use definitive methods and specimen context rather than relying on a screening result alone.

## Counterfeit, bulk powder and product identity

Phenazepam's internet-market history included bulk powder. That matters because:
- a small measurement error can become a large exposure;
- users may re-dose before appreciating the full duration;
- powder labels do not prove identity or concentration;
- other sedatives can be substituted or mixed in.

This is one reason historical phenazepam harms cannot be separated from the way it was sold.

## Postmortem interpretation

Phenazepam appears not to undergo the dramatic postmortem redistribution seen with some drugs, but concentration interpretation remains complex.

Postmortem studies show:
- substantial concentration overlap;
- mixed-drug deaths are common;
- tissue and fluid concentrations do not map perfectly;
- specimen preservation matters.

A toxicology result should therefore be described as **detected**, **contributing**, or **causal** only when the total evidence supports that wording.

## Epidemiology and trend

Phenazepam's prominence in Western RC markets peaked earlier than newer benzodiazepines such as bromazolam.

Historical UK and Scottish series demonstrate a meaningful period of morbidity and mortality, but those data should not be presented as current global prevalence.

Current relevance is partly educational: phenazepam remains a strong example of how a long-acting pharmaceutical benzodiazepine can become much riskier when moved into an unregulated powder/counterfeit market.

## Legal and regulatory history

### International
Phenazepam was placed in **Schedule IV of the 1971 Convention on Psychotropic Substances** after the 2016 Commission on Narcotic Drugs decision. UNODC states the decision became fully effective for parties on **November 13, 2016**.

### United States
Phenazepam is not FDA-approved for medical use in the United States. DEA maintains a dedicated phenazepam drug-information entry and tracks it in forensic systems.

U.S. control questions should be checked against the current DEA/CSA schedules and applicable state law rather than inferred from international scheduling alone. International Schedule IV does not automatically map to a particular U.S. federal schedule.

## Special populations

Direct phenazepam-specific modern data are limited in:
- pregnancy;
- breastfeeding;
- older adults;
- adolescents;
- severe liver disease;
- chronic respiratory disease.

Class-level benzodiazepine concerns include fetal/neonatal exposure, falls and cognitive impairment in older adults, and higher respiratory risk with opioids or pulmonary disease.

Long elimination may further magnify accumulation in vulnerable patients.

## Myths versus evidence

### “Phenazepam lasts a long time only because internet users say so.”
**False.** Human pharmacokinetic studies and prolonged clinical cases support slow elimination.

### “If someone is awake the next morning, they are safe to drive.”
**False.** Long persistence and direct impaired-driving evidence make residual psychomotor impairment a real concern.

### “There is a known lethal blood concentration.”
**False.** Postmortem concentration ranges overlap.

### “A long half-life prevents withdrawal.”
**False.** It may delay withdrawal; it does not prevent physical dependence.

### “Because it was a medicine somewhere, internet powder is pharmaceutical-grade.”
**False.** Medical history says nothing about the identity or concentration of an unregulated product.

## Major evidence gaps

Important unanswered or incompletely answered questions include:
- modern population pharmacokinetics;
- contemporary interaction studies;
- prospective withdrawal characterization;
- pregnancy and organ-impairment PK;
- concentration-response relationships for impairment;
- current geographic prevalence;
- outcomes in contemporary fentanyl-era polysubstance exposure.

## Bottom line

Phenazepam has a stronger human evidence base than most RC benzodiazepines, and that evidence makes its central hazard unusually clear: **it can persist for a very long time**.

That long persistence can turn one episode into prolonged impairment, accumulation, blackout, delayed driving risk and delayed withdrawal. Forensic research also shows why simplistic concentration cutoffs are unsafe: fatal, contributory and incidental concentrations overlap.

The right safety frame is not “strong benzo” or “old Russian Xanax.” It is **a long-acting benzodiazepine with real human PK, real driving and mortality evidence, dependence risk, and unusually high consequences when sold outside a regulated medical system**.
