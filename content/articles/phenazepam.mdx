# Phenazepam: Complete Evidence, Pharmacology, Toxicology, Dependence & Safety Monograph

> **Emergency safety:** A person who cannot be awakened, has slow or abnormal breathing, blue/gray lips, repeated vomiting while profoundly sedated, a seizure, collapse or severe confusion after phenazepam or an unknown sedative needs emergency care. Alcohol, opioids and other sedatives can substantially increase risk. Naloxone does not reverse phenazepam, but should be given when opioid exposure is possible.

## Quick answer

**Phenazepam (fenazepam) is a long-acting 1,4-benzodiazepine developed in the Soviet Union and used medically in Russia and other countries since the late 1970s.** It later entered online and illicit markets in Western countries, where forensic literature documented prolonged intoxication, impaired driving, hospitalization and deaths.

Phenazepam is better characterized than many newer RC benzodiazepines. Human and forensic evidence supports a **very prolonged clinical effect**, active metabolism to 3-hydroxyphenazepam, accumulation risk, major psychomotor impairment and benzodiazepine-type physical dependence.

The practical danger is not that phenazepam is chemically exotic. It is that a potent, long-acting benzodiazepine can remain clinically relevant long after a user expects the effect to be over, especially when repeated exposure or additional depressants are involved.

## Identity and medical history

**Canonical name:** Phenazepam / fenazepam  
**CAS:** 51753-57-2  
**Class:** 1,4-benzodiazepine  
**Active metabolite:** 3-hydroxyphenazepam  
**Medical history:** prescribed since 1978 in Russia/CIS for anxiety, epilepsy, insomnia, alcohol-withdrawal-related indications and perioperative use  
**U.S. approved medical use:** none

Phenazepam is not a newly invented internet compound. It began as a pharmaceutical benzodiazepine and later became an NPS/imported-drug problem in jurisdictions where it was not approved.

## History and market emergence

Phenazepam was developed in the Soviet Union and entered clinical use in 1978. By the late 2000s and early 2010s, it was being sold online internationally as powder, tablets, liquid and sometimes on blotter or in other products.

That market context mattered because bulk powder removed normal pharmaceutical dose controls. For a long-acting sedative, measurement errors and repeated dosing before full effects became apparent could produce prolonged impairment.

DEA reports U.S. forensic-laboratory detections beginning in 2005, with hundreds of NFLIS reports over subsequent years. The trend has fluctuated rather than rising continuously.

## Pharmacology

Phenazepam binds the **benzodiazepine site of GABA-A receptors**, enhancing inhibitory GABA signaling. Its active metabolite, 3-hydroxyphenazepam, also has benzodiazepine-site activity.

Expected effects include:
- anxiolysis;
- sedation;
- hypnosis;
- muscle relaxation;
- anticonvulsant effects;
- memory impairment;
- slowed psychomotor performance;
- impaired coordination and judgment.

Animal discrimination work cited by DEA shows classic benzodiazepine-like pharmacology and antagonism by flumazenil. Those data support mechanism, not a recreational potency conversion.

## Metabolism and pharmacokinetics: why duration matters

DEA's 2025 evaluation describes a delayed oral onset and very prolonged elimination. Published reviews likewise emphasize that phenazepam can persist far longer than shorter-acting benzodiazepines.

A long half-life changes the risk profile:
- effects can persist into the next day or beyond;
- repeated doses can accumulate;
- impairment can outlast subjective intoxication;
- withdrawal after dependence may begin later than with short-acting benzodiazepines;
- a person may re-dose before the full effect of an earlier exposure is apparent.

A half-life is not the same thing as a guaranteed duration of intoxication. Individual metabolism, dose, repeated exposure, active metabolites and co-drugs matter.

## Human effects and prolonged intoxication

A well-known UK case documented analytically confirmed phenazepam toxicity with confusion and neuropsychiatric impairment lasting roughly **60 hours**.

Other reports and reviews describe:
- extreme drowsiness;
- ataxia;
- slurred speech;
- amnesia;
- confusion;
- prolonged behavioral impairment;
- hospitalization.

These reports establish real clinical toxicity but should not be converted into a universal “X amount causes Y hours” rule.

## Impaired driving

Phenazepam has been documented in impaired-driving investigations. Published casework describes major psychomotor impairment, including slurred speech and poor coordination.

Long persistence creates a particular traffic-safety problem: someone may no longer feel acutely intoxicated while reaction time, coordination and judgment remain impaired.

A blood concentration should not be treated as a standalone impairment calculator because tolerance, timing, specimen collection and co-drugs alter interpretation.

## Acute toxicity and overdose

Expected benzodiazepine toxicity includes:
- profound sedation;
- ataxia;
- dysarthria;
- amnesia;
- confusion;
- reduced consciousness.

DEA notes that death from phenazepam alone, as with many benzodiazepines, is uncommon relative to opioid overdose. That should not be misread as harmlessness.

The risk becomes much greater with:
- opioids;
- alcohol;
- other benzodiazepines;
- Z-drugs;
- other CNS depressants.

Mixed-drug deaths require careful attribution rather than assigning causality to every detected compound.

## Fatality evidence

Phenazepam has appeared in fatal toxicology and was specifically cited in early UK reports of serious adverse events and deaths.

The important forensic rule is simple: **detection is not automatically sole causation**. In mixed-drug deaths, respiratory depression may reflect multiple depressants, underlying disease and other circumstances.

There is no single phenazepam blood concentration that can be treated as a universal lethal threshold.

## Interactions and polysubstance risk

**Opioids:** Benzodiazepine-type sedation plus opioids can produce profound CNS and respiratory depression. Naloxone reverses the opioid component, not phenazepam itself.

**Alcohol:** Alcohol can add to psychomotor impairment, amnesia, disinhibition and respiratory risk.

**Other sedatives:** Other benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, gabapentinoids and sedating medications can compound impairment and make phenazepam's already long persistence harder to interpret.

Because CYP3A4 appears important to phenazepam metabolism, metabolic inhibitors or inducers could plausibly alter exposure; the magnitude of specific interactions is not sufficiently characterized to support a consumer interaction calculator.

## Tolerance and physical dependence

Repeated exposure can produce:
- tolerance to some effects;
- physical dependence;
- escalating or more frequent use.

Tolerance does not make someone immune to blackouts, falls, respiratory risk with opioids or accumulation from repeated dosing.

Physical dependence is also not identical to a benzodiazepine use disorder. A patient taking a benzodiazepine as prescribed can become physically dependent without compulsive use.

## Withdrawal

Phenazepam's long elimination can make withdrawal **delayed** relative to shorter-acting benzodiazepines.

Benzodiazepine withdrawal can include:
- rebound anxiety;
- insomnia;
- tremor;
- agitation;
- autonomic symptoms;
- perceptual disturbance;
- confusion;
- seizures;
- delirium.

The 2025 multisociety benzodiazepine-tapering guideline recommends avoiding abrupt discontinuation in people likely to be physically dependent.

No universal phenazepam withdrawal calendar or self-directed taper percentage can be justified. Exposure duration, repeated dosing, co-use and individual pharmacokinetics all matter.

## Benzodiazepine use disorder

A use disorder involves more than tolerance or withdrawal. Warning signs include:
- persistent inability to cut down;
- craving;
- repeated hazardous use;
- use despite medical, work or relationship consequences;
- substantial time spent obtaining, using or recovering;
- recurrent blackouts or injuries with continued use.

Long-acting intoxication can make these consequences especially disruptive because one exposure can affect functioning across multiple days.

## Treatment and recovery support

Acute phenazepam poisoning is managed according to airway, breathing, mental status, injury and co-exposures.

Flumazenil exists as a benzodiazepine antagonist but is **not** a universal field antidote; seizure and withdrawal risks make patient selection important.

For likely physical dependence, medically supervised benzodiazepine-tapering principles are safer than abrupt discontinuation or improvised internet conversion tables.

U.S. readers can use:
- **FindTreatment.gov**
- SAMHSA substance-use treatment resources
- emergency services for seizure, delirium, unresponsiveness or abnormal breathing.

## Testing and toxicology

Phenazepam can be measured with chromatographic mass-spectrometry methods. Analytical targets may include:
- parent phenazepam;
- 3-hydroxyphenazepam;
- other validated metabolites depending on specimen and method.

Routine benzodiazepine immunoassays are not a complete phenazepam rule-out strategy. Cross-reactivity varies, and definitive toxicology should use validated targeted or broad high-resolution methods when exposure is suspected.

## Forensic interpretation

Interpretation depends on:
- specimen type and timing;
- parent/metabolite pattern;
- tolerance;
- co-detected drugs;
- clinical observations;
- postmortem redistribution and degradation issues;
- scene/investigative evidence.

A concentration measured after death or hours after driving cannot automatically reconstruct the concentration or impairment at an earlier moment.

## Epidemiology and current trend

DEA's September 2025 factsheet reports phenazepam first appearing in NFLIS-Drug in **2005** and more than **670 total reports**, including 15 reports in 2023 and 15 in 2024.

Those figures describe submissions to participating U.S. forensic laboratories, not national prevalence or number of users.

## Legal and regulatory status

### United States
DEA's September 2025 Drug & Chemical Evaluation Section factsheet states explicitly:

**Phenazepam is not controlled under the federal Controlled Substances Act.**

It also has no accepted medical use in the United States.

State law may be more restrictive, so federal status should not be treated as a universal state-level legal answer.

### International
WHO recommended international control after its 2015 critical review, and the UN Commission on Narcotic Drugs placed phenazepam in **Schedule IV of the 1971 Convention on Psychotropic Substances in 2016**.

UNODC currently lists phenazepam with control status **71/IV** and year of scheduling **2016**.

## Special populations

Direct modern phenazepam evidence is limited in:
- pregnancy and breastfeeding;
- children/adolescents;
- older adults;
- significant liver or kidney impairment.

Benzodiazepine-class concerns include neonatal effects during pregnancy, increased fall/cognitive risk in older adults and increased respiratory danger in people using opioids or with significant respiratory disease.

## Myths versus evidence

### “Phenazepam is just an old prescription benzo, so it is predictable.”
**False.** Medical history improves the evidence base, but internet/bulk products add identity and concentration uncertainty.

### “Long half-life means it is safer because levels change slowly.”
**False.** Long persistence can increase accumulation and prolonged impairment.

### “If I feel awake, I am safe to drive.”
**False.** Psychomotor impairment can persist beyond the strongest subjective effects.

### “A fatal concentration defines a lethal dose.”
**False.** Tolerance, specimen timing and co-drugs produce substantial overlap and uncertainty.

### “Withdrawal starts immediately after stopping.”
**Not necessarily.** Long elimination can delay onset.

### “Phenazepam is federally Schedule IV because it is Schedule IV internationally.”
**False.** International Schedule IV and the U.S. Controlled Substances Act are separate systems; DEA currently states phenazepam is not controlled under the CSA.

## Major evidence gaps

Important unresolved questions include:
- contemporary controlled human PK/PD studies;
- concentration-response models for impairment;
- prospective withdrawal timing after chronic exposure;
- modern U.S. clinical incidence;
- pregnancy/organ-impairment PK;
- current counterfeit-product composition;
- long-term cognitive outcomes after heavy nonmedical use.

For U.S. treatment referrals, [SAMHSA’s National Helpline](https://www.samhsa.gov/find-help/helplines/national-helpline), **1-800-662-HELP (4357)**, is available 24 hours a day. [FindTreatment.gov](https://findtreatment.gov/) lists treatment services. Referral services do not replace emergency care.

## Bottom line

Phenazepam is a useful reminder that “research chemical” and “unknown drug” are not synonyms. It has decades of pharmaceutical history and substantially more human evidence than many modern designer benzodiazepines.

Its defining safety problem is **duration**: prolonged elimination, active metabolism and repeated exposure can produce multi-day impairment and delayed withdrawal. Mixed depressants sharply raise risk, and forensic concentrations require context.

The evidence is strong enough to say phenazepam is clinically important, dependence-capable and capable of serious prolonged toxicity. It is **not** strong enough to justify a recreational dosing guide, a universal toxic concentration or a DIY withdrawal schedule.
