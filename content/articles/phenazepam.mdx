# Phenazepam: Complete Evidence, Toxicology, Dependence, Withdrawal & Safety Monograph

> **Emergency safety:** A person who cannot be awakened, has slow or irregular breathing, is blue/gray around the lips, has a seizure, collapses, aspirates or remains profoundly confused after phenazepam or an unknown sedative needs emergency care. **Naloxone reverses opioids, not phenazepam**, but it should still be given when opioid exposure is possible.

## Quick answer

**Phenazepam (fenazepam) is a long-acting 1,4-benzodiazepine developed in the former Soviet Union and used medically in Russia and some neighboring countries.** It later became an internet-sold and illicit-market drug in Europe and North America.

Phenazepam is unusually important within the RC/NPS benzodiazepine cluster because it has **far more human evidence than many newer designer benzodiazepines**. Published literature includes hospital intoxications, impaired-driving series, police seizures, postmortem detections and deaths.

The most important safety feature is **persistence**. An analytically confirmed UK case reported confusion and disorientation lasting about 60 hours. Forensic driving studies also document marked psychomotor impairment.

The biggest misconception is that a long history of medical use makes nonmedical phenazepam predictable. It does not. Unregulated products, long persistence, accumulation, physical dependence, co-use with opioids/alcohol and wide concentration overlap make simple “equivalent dose” or “lethal level” rules unsafe.

## History: from Soviet medicine to internet drug markets

Phenazepam was developed in the Soviet pharmaceutical system in the 1970s and entered clinical use in the late 1970s. WHO documents therapeutic use for anxiety, insomnia, epilepsy, alcohol-withdrawal-related indications and perioperative contexts in countries where it was approved.

Outside those medical systems, phenazepam later became available through internet and gray-market channels. Early Western concern centered on **bulk powder, long duration and severe prolonged intoxication**.

The DEA reported in its September 2025 evaluation that phenazepam had first appeared in the U.S. NFLIS forensic laboratory database in **2005** and accumulated more than 670 reports over time, with detections continuing into 2024.

This trajectory makes phenazepam an early example of a pattern now common in the RC/NPS market: an existing pharmacologic molecule migrates into unregulated channels, where product identity and user expectations become less reliable.

---

## Chemistry and relationship to other benzodiazepines

Phenazepam is a **1,4-benzodiazepine**, structurally related to diazepam-class sedatives but distinguished by bromine and chlorine substitution.

Its structure supports benzodiazepine-site activity at GABA-A receptors. Structural similarity helps identify the class and expected toxidrome, but does **not** establish a safe conversion to diazepam, clonazepam or any other benzodiazepine.

---

## Pharmacodynamics

### GABA-A positive allosteric modulation

WHO's review describes phenazepam and 3-hydroxyphenazepam as binding to the benzodiazepine site of the **GABA-A receptor**.

Benzodiazepine-site positive allosteric modulation enhances GABA-mediated inhibition and can produce:

- anxiolysis;
- sedation;
- hypnosis;
- anticonvulsant effects;
- muscle relaxation;
- impaired coordination;
- impaired reaction time;
- anterograde amnesia;
- confusion or stupor at high exposure.

### Why long persistence matters pharmacodynamically

A drug can continue impairing cognition and coordination even when the user feels less sedated than earlier. Long persistence also creates accumulation risk when exposure repeats before the prior amount has cleared.

That makes “I feel awake now” a poor proxy for driving safety.

---

## Pharmacokinetics and metabolism

Phenazepam has a reputation as a long-acting benzodiazepine, supported by clinical and forensic observations of prolonged effects.

Older human pharmacokinetic literature exists, but modern controlled studies are limited and formulations/populations differ. The site therefore avoids presenting one half-life as a universal prediction for every person or illicit product.

What is well supported:

- phenazepam can remain clinically relevant for a prolonged period;
- 3-hydroxyphenazepam is pharmacologically relevant;
- repeated exposure can lead to accumulation;
- prolonged detection complicates interpretation of when impairment occurred;
- metabolism and elimination vary with individual factors and co-medications.

---

## Human effects: what is actually documented

### Prolonged clinical toxicity

Dargan and colleagues described a 42-year-old man with analytically confirmed phenazepam exposure and no other drugs detected on extensive screening. Confusion and disorientation persisted for approximately **60 hours**.

That case does not define a universal duration, but it strongly demonstrates that phenazepam intoxication can be prolonged.

### Impaired driving

A U.S. impaired-driving report described slurred speech and profound psychomotor impairment with phenazepam detected and no other drugs found.

A separate Georgia review examined **11 impaired-driving cases**; five involved phenazepam alone and six involved additional drugs. Reported signs were consistent with CNS depression: slurred speech, poor balance, slowed reactions, drowsiness and confusion.

### Finland: a larger real-world dataset

A Finnish study found phenazepam in **141 apprehended drivers** during one year, plus **17 medico-legal autopsy cases** and 26 police seizures.

Concentrations varied widely and co-drugs were common. That is exactly why the study should not be converted into a simplistic “impaired above X” or “fatal above Y” rule.

---

## Acute toxicity and overdose

Phenazepam toxicity can include:

- profound drowsiness;
- dysarthria/slurred speech;
- ataxia;
- confusion;
- memory impairment;
- delayed reactions;
- stupor or coma;
- aspiration risk;
- severe impairment lasting far longer than expected.

Benzodiazepines alone are often less directly respiratory-depressing than opioids, but **mixed depressant exposure changes the risk substantially**.

---

## Interactions and polysubstance risk

### Opioids

The most dangerous real-world combination is often benzodiazepine plus opioid. Opioids suppress respiratory drive while benzodiazepines add sedation, impaired airway protection and reduced arousal.

Finnish postmortem data found phenazepam commonly alongside other psychoactive drugs, and opioid overdoses were prominent among autopsy cases.

### Alcohol

Alcohol adds CNS depression, worsens coordination and memory, and increases the risk of aspiration, falls and loss of consciousness.

### Other sedatives

Other benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, sedating gabapentinoids and related depressants can produce additive impairment.

### Stimulants do not “cancel out” phenazepam

Stimulant co-use may make a person feel more awake while leaving judgment, coordination and memory impaired. Mixed stimulant/depressant exposures also complicate cardiovascular and behavioral risk.

---

## Tolerance

Tolerance can develop with repeated benzodiazepine exposure, but not necessarily equally to every effect.

A tolerant person may appear less sleepy while remaining impaired, physically dependent or vulnerable to overdose when other depressants are added.

Tolerance is therefore not a reliable safety shield.

---

## Physical dependence

Phenazepam can produce **physical dependence** with repeated exposure.

WHO's scheduling review explicitly considered evidence of dependence and harm. Dependence means neuroadaptation sufficient for withdrawal to emerge when the drug is reduced or stopped.

It does not automatically mean the person has a benzodiazepine use disorder.

---

## Withdrawal

Phenazepam's long action can make withdrawal **delayed**, which may falsely reassure someone during the first period after stopping.

Benzodiazepine withdrawal can include:

- severe rebound anxiety;
- insomnia;
- tremor;
- autonomic symptoms;
- perceptual disturbance;
- agitation;
- confusion;
- seizures;
- delirium.

The 2025 multisociety benzodiazepine guideline advises against abrupt discontinuation in people likely to be physically dependent.

### No universal phenazepam withdrawal clock

The evidence does **not** support a fixed “day 1–day 7” timeline for everyone.

Timing and severity depend on:

- duration and frequency of exposure;
- cumulative amount;
- individual metabolism;
- co-use of alcohol or other sedatives;
- seizure history;
- medical/psychiatric conditions;
- actual product identity.

A delayed onset should not be interpreted as proof that dependence is mild.

---

## Physical dependence vs benzodiazepine use disorder

Physical dependence can happen without compulsive use.

A benzodiazepine/sedative use disorder is suggested by a broader pattern such as:

- inability to cut down;
- craving;
- escalating use;
- hazardous use;
- repeated blackouts or injuries;
- continued use despite medical or relationship harm;
- major time spent obtaining, using or recovering;
- interference with work, family or responsibilities.

The distinction matters because someone can need a medically managed taper without meeting criteria for a substance use disorder—and someone with a use disorder needs treatment beyond simply completing withdrawal.

---

## Treatment and recovery support

There is no phenazepam-specific antidote for routine use and no evidence-based DIY taper schedule.

When physical dependence is likely, treatment should use established benzodiazepine-withdrawal principles under clinical supervision. Severe withdrawal, seizures, delirium, unstable vital signs or complicated polysubstance exposure can require urgent or inpatient care.

For U.S. readers:

- [SAMHSA substance-use treatment resources](https://www.samhsa.gov/substance-use/treatment)
- [FindTreatment.gov](https://findtreatment.gov/)
- emergency symptoms warrant 911 or local emergency services.

Recovery planning should also address insomnia, anxiety, trauma, alcohol/opioid use and other conditions that may have driven sedative use.

## Drug checking, toxicology and detection

Phenazepam can be identified by chromatographic mass spectrometry in blood, urine and seized materials.

Important interpretive points:

- routine benzodiazepine immunoassays do not have uniform sensitivity for every benzodiazepine or metabolite;
- targeted LC-MS/MS or GC-MS can provide more specific identification;
- metabolite patterns can support exposure interpretation;
- long persistence means detection does not automatically prove impairment at the exact moment of an event.

---

## Counterfeit and unregulated product risk

DEA has reported phenazepam in:

- powder;
- tablets;
- dropper-bottle solutions;
- products marketed with misleading non-drug labels;
- mixtures involving other psychoactive substances.

Historically, bulk powder created special risk because small measurement errors with a potent sedative can produce very different exposures.

Unregulated product identity remains an independent hazard even when the molecule itself is well characterized.

---

## Forensic interpretation: why concentration is not destiny

Phenazepam case series show **wide blood-concentration ranges** in both impaired-driving and postmortem investigations.

Interpretation must consider:

- tolerance;
- co-drugs;
- time between use and sampling;
- specimen type;
- postmortem changes;
- chronic vs acute exposure;
- clinical observations.

A blood concentration from a death does not define a universal lethal concentration. A concentration from an impaired driver does not create a universal legal impairment threshold.

---

## Epidemiology and market trends

Phenazepam was a prominent early internet benzodiazepine in the late 2000s and early 2010s.

The DEA's 2025 evaluation reported:

- first NFLIS-Drug report in 2005;
- more than 670 total reports;
- high annual counts in the early/mid-2010s;
- continued but lower detections in 2023 and 2024.

This pattern suggests a drug that has declined from its earlier peak but has **not disappeared**.

## Legal and regulatory history

### United States federal status

The DEA's **September 2025** phenazepam evaluation states that phenazepam **is not controlled under the federal Controlled Substances Act** and has no accepted medical use in the United States.

That does not mean it is legal in every state or for every purpose. State scheduling and other federal/state laws can apply.

### International control

WHO's Expert Committee recommended phenazepam for Schedule IV of the **1971 Convention on Psychotropic Substances**. The UN Commission on Narcotic Drugs adopted that scheduling decision in **2016**.

UNODC currently lists phenazepam as **71/IV**, reflecting Schedule IV control under the 1971 Convention.

---

## Special populations

Direct modern phenazepam evidence is limited for pregnancy, breastfeeding, pediatrics and major organ impairment.

Class-level concerns are especially important for:

- older adults with fall/cognitive risk;
- people with sleep apnea or respiratory disease;
- people using opioids or alcohol;
- people with seizure disorders;
- people taking multiple sedating medications.

Absence of phenazepam-specific data should not be interpreted as safety.

---

## Myths vs evidence

### “Phenazepam is old, so it is predictable.”

**False.** Medical history improves the evidence base, but unregulated products, long persistence and polysubstance use remain major hazards.

### “If I feel awake, I am safe to drive.”

**False.** Forensic cases show psychomotor impairment; subjective alertness is not a validated driving test.

### “There is a lethal phenazepam blood level.”

**False.** Forensic concentrations overlap and must be interpreted in context.

### “Because withdrawal is delayed, I am not dependent.”

**False.** Long-acting benzodiazepines can produce delayed withdrawal.

### “Benzodiazepines do not cause overdose.”

**Misleading.** Severe CNS depression occurs, and risk rises sharply with opioids, alcohol and other sedatives.

### “Dependence equals addiction.”

**False.** Physical dependence and benzodiazepine use disorder overlap but are not identical.

## What remains unknown

Despite decades of use, important gaps remain:

- modern controlled pharmacokinetic studies across diverse populations;
- concentration-response thresholds for impairment;
- reliable isolated-overdose toxic concentration ranges;
- detailed pregnancy and breastfeeding outcomes;
- phenazepam-specific withdrawal trajectories;
- validated conversion/taper equivalence;
- current prevalence outside forensic reporting systems;
- interaction magnitude with specific opioids and other sedatives.

---

## Bottom line

Phenazepam sits between two worlds: **an established benzodiazepine medicine in some countries and a historically important unregulated/RC-market sedative elsewhere**.

Its human evidence is much stronger than that of many designer benzodiazepines. That evidence documents **prolonged intoxication, marked psychomotor impairment, mixed-drug fatalities, physical dependence and clinically dangerous withdrawal risk**.

The key harm-reduction lesson is not a dose number. It is that **long persistence makes accumulation, delayed impairment and delayed withdrawal unusually important**, while forensic concentration data remain too context-dependent to turn into simple safety or lethality thresholds.
