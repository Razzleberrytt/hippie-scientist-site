# Kratom and 7-OH Withdrawal & Recovery

> **Emergency safety:** Unresponsiveness, slow or irregular breathing, blue/gray lips or skin, repeated choking or gurgling while unconscious, seizure, severe confusion, chest pain, or collapse after a kratom-derived product is **not ordinary uncomplicated withdrawal**. Call emergency services. If opioid exposure is possible, naloxone is appropriate while help is on the way. A product sold as “kratom,” “7-OH,” “enhanced leaf,” or a botanical blend may contain unexpected opioid-active compounds or other drugs.

## Executive answer

Kratom withdrawal is real, but **“kratom withdrawal” no longer describes one uniform exposure**.

Traditional kratom leaf and tea contain a mixture of alkaloids dominated by mitragynine, with much smaller natural amounts of 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH). Modern extracts can concentrate those alkaloids, while a newer retail category contains **isolated, enriched, or chemically transformed 7-OH at levels that are not comparable with ordinary leaf**. Human evidence now documents tolerance, physical dependence, craving, DSM-style kratom use disorder, opioid-like withdrawal, and treatment with opioid-use-disorder medications in selected patients [1-8,14-19].

The strongest evidence for ordinary kratom comes from observational surveys, Southeast Asian cohorts, systematic reviews, and clinical case series. The strongest evidence for concentrated 7-OH is newer and thinner: 2025-2026 case reports, poison-center surveillance, and a small retrospective treatment series [20-27].

The practical summary is:

- **ordinary kratom leaf can cause dependence and withdrawal**, especially with frequent long-term use;
- **concentrated 7-OH can produce a more clearly opioid-like dependence syndrome**, but prospective timelines and treatment trials do not yet exist;
- withdrawal severity depends on product chemistry, frequency, duration, prior opioid exposure, co-use, medical/psychiatric illness, and product uncertainty—not one gram or tablet number;
- acute withdrawal management is **not the same as treatment of kratom or 7-OH use disorder**;
- buprenorphine has the largest published clinical experience for more severe kratom/7-OH use disorder, but the evidence remains case-series level and there is no standardized kratom-specific medication guideline [14-23];
- a person using concentrated 7-OH should not assume that information from traditional kratom tea or leaf predicts their withdrawal course.

---

## Kratom leaf, extracts, and concentrated 7-OH are not interchangeable

A major source of confusion is treating all products under one word: **kratom**.

### Traditional leaf or tea

Traditional products contain multiple *Mitragyna speciosa* alkaloids. Mitragynine is usually the dominant alkaloid. Human pharmacokinetic studies show prolonged and variable mitragynine elimination, while different alkaloids have different disposition profiles [10-12].

### Kratom extracts

Extracts can deliver a different alkaloid concentration and exposure profile from plain leaf. A 2026 human pharmacokinetic study showed measurable mitragynine and 7-OH after repeated concentrated-extract exposure, reinforcing that “extract” is not simply a smaller serving of leaf [12].

### Concentrated or enriched 7-OH products

Retail 7-OH products can expose users to 7-hydroxymitragynine far above the small concentrations naturally present in unmodified leaf. FDA and medical-toxicology sources now treat these as a distinct public-health concern [27-29].

That distinction matters for withdrawal because a dependence syndrome reflects **what the nervous system was repeatedly exposed to**, not what the front label called the product.

---

## Dependence is not the same as addiction

These concepts overlap but are not identical.

**Tolerance** means repeated exposure produces less effect from the same exposure or a need for greater exposure to achieve a previous effect.

**Physical dependence** means the nervous system has adapted enough that reducing or stopping exposure can produce withdrawal.

**Withdrawal** is the symptom syndrome that appears as drug effect falls after physical dependence.

**Craving** is a motivational state that can occur with or without obvious physical withdrawal.

**Kratom use disorder (KUD)** is a broader behavioral syndrome involving impaired control, continued use despite harm, craving, unsuccessful attempts to reduce use, hazardous use, role impairment, tolerance and/or withdrawal.

A person can be physically dependent without meeting criteria for a severe use disorder. Conversely, a person can have serious compulsive use even when the physical withdrawal syndrome is not dramatic.

Large U.S. surveys find that many people who meet adapted DSM-5 KUD criteria fall in the mild-to-moderate range, and tolerance and withdrawal are among the most commonly reported criteria [5-7].

---

## What causes kratom and 7-OH withdrawal?

The biology is partly opioid and partly more complicated.

Mitragynine and 7-OH interact with the **mu-opioid receptor (MOR)**. 7-OH has stronger opioid-receptor activity than mitragynine in many experimental systems. Kratom alkaloids also interact with adrenergic, serotonergic, and other signaling systems, although the clinical importance of those non-opioid actions is less certain [8,9,13].

Repeated opioid-receptor stimulation can produce compensatory changes in neural systems governing:

- autonomic tone;
- pain sensitivity;
- stress response;
- sleep;
- reward and motivation;
- gastrointestinal activity;
- mood.

When exposure falls, those adaptations can temporarily become unmasked as restlessness, autonomic activation, pain, sleep disruption, gastrointestinal symptoms, dysphoria, and craving.

The mechanism is biologically plausible and supported by human withdrawal reports, but the exact molecular adaptation pattern has **not** been mapped in humans for ordinary kratom, concentrated 7-OH, MGM-15, MGM-16, or mitragynine pseudoindoxyl.

## Symptom map

### Commonly reported physical symptoms

Human kratom studies and reviews describe:

- muscle aches or spasms;
- joint or body pain;
- restlessness;
- sweating or hot/cold sensations;
- watery eyes or runny nose;
- yawning;
- reduced appetite;
- abdominal discomfort;
- nausea;
- diarrhea;
- tremor;
- sleep disruption.

### Commonly reported psychological symptoms

These include:

- anxiety;
- irritability;
- tension;
- low mood;
- restlessness;
- craving;
- difficulty concentrating;
- reduced motivation.

Traditional kratom withdrawal is often described as **opioid-like**, but it is not proven to be identical to heroin, fentanyl, methadone, or prescription-opioid withdrawal [1-4,13].

### Concentrated 7-OH

Recent 7-OH cases describe opioid-like syndromes including gastrointestinal symptoms, chills, sweating, restlessness, anxiety, craving and difficulty stopping repeated use [20-26].

Because those reports involve small numbers of patients and heterogeneous products, they establish that clinically important withdrawal **can occur**; they do not define a universal symptom frequency or severity.

---

## How severe is kratom withdrawal?

The answer depends heavily on the population being studied.

Traditional Southeast Asian cohorts include long-term daily users, some of whom report substantial dependence and withdrawal [4]. U.S. online studies find a wide spectrum: many users report little or no withdrawal, while a subset meet criteria for KUD and report clinically meaningful symptoms [5-7].

A useful 2024 finding is that **frequency of use may matter at least as much as the amount used at one time**. In a U.S. study examining dose amount versus dosing frequency, more frequent use was associated with greater withdrawal and KUD symptom burden [7].

This is one reason a single “grams per day” threshold is a poor clinical rule. Product alkaloid composition, extract concentration, number of exposures per day, duration of use, prior opioid tolerance and co-exposures can all change risk.

---

## Timeline: what is known and what is not

There is no validated universal kratom or 7-OH withdrawal clock.

### Traditional kratom

Observational reports generally suggest an opioid-like syndrome after cessation in physically dependent users, but studies use different products, populations, symptom scales, and definitions [1-7].

Mitragynine itself can have a prolonged terminal elimination phase in human pharmacokinetic studies, but **half-life does not equal withdrawal onset or duration** [10-12]. Active metabolites, repeated exposure, tissue distribution, receptor dynamics and product composition matter.

### Concentrated 7-OH

Direct evidence is newer.

One 2026 hospitalized case described withdrawal emerging within hours after concentrated 7-OH cessation [22]. Another 2026 report documented ongoing symptoms after a longer interval from the last reported exposure [20]. A nine-patient 2026 treatment series showed clinically significant problematic 7-OH use but was not designed to define a universal withdrawal timeline [23].

The correct conclusion is **variability**, not a precise countdown.

| Exposure pattern | Evidence-supported expectation | Major uncertainty |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Traditional leaf/tea, intermittent | Withdrawal may be absent or mild | Product composition and baseline opioid history vary |
| Traditional leaf/tea, frequent long-term use | Opioid-like symptoms and craving can occur | Timing and severity vary widely |
| Concentrated extracts | Potentially greater dependence burden than low-intensity leaf use | Extract chemistry is inconsistent |
| Concentrated/enriched 7-OH | Clinically important opioid-like withdrawal can occur | Only small human case series define the current evidence |
| Mixed kratom + 7-OH + other opioids | Withdrawal can be difficult to attribute to one product | Co-dependence and product mislabeling can dominate the presentation |

## What predicts a harder withdrawal?

Evidence supports several practical risk factors, although none produces a perfect prediction.

### More frequent exposure

Frequent dosing gives the nervous system less time without receptor-active drug exposure and is associated with greater dependence/withdrawal burden in human kratom data [4,7].

### Longer duration of use

Longer repeated exposure increases the opportunity for physical dependence and learned behavioral reinforcement.

### Concentrated products

Extracts and high-7-OH products can produce alkaloid exposures that are not comparable to ordinary leaf. The 2025-2026 7-OH literature specifically describes escalation, compulsive use and withdrawal after concentrated-product use [20-26].

### Prior opioid use disorder or opioid tolerance

A person who transitions from prescription opioids, heroin/fentanyl, or another opioid into kratom or 7-OH may have pre-existing opioid neuroadaptation. Withdrawal can therefore reflect more than the newest product alone.

### Pain, anxiety, depression and insomnia

These are common reasons people report using kratom. When kratom is stopped, the underlying condition may re-emerge at the same time as withdrawal, making the syndrome feel more persistent or severe [2,3,30,31].

### Polysubstance use

Alcohol, benzodiazepines, stimulants, gabapentinoids, opioids and other sedatives can complicate both intoxication and withdrawal.

---

## The product itself may be the biggest unknown

A major 2026 harm-reduction issue is that “kratom-derived” products increasingly include:

- ordinary leaf;
- concentrated extract;
- enhanced 7-OH;
- semi-synthetic 7-OH products;
- mitragynine pseudoindoxyl;
- MGM-15;
- MGM-16;
- products with incomplete or inaccurate labels.

A withdrawal history should therefore include the **exact product, formulation, label, frequency, duration, and whether the product changed over time**.

If someone believed they were using ordinary kratom but actually used a concentrated 7-OH or pseudoindoxyl product, a withdrawal course that seems “too severe for kratom” may reflect a different opioid-active exposure.

---

## Polysubstance and co-withdrawal reality

Not every symptom after stopping kratom is caused by kratom.

### Alcohol or sedative-hypnotic withdrawal

Seizures, delirium, severe autonomic instability or marked confusion should raise concern for alcohol, benzodiazepine or other sedative withdrawal rather than being attributed to uncomplicated kratom withdrawal.

### Opioid co-use

Someone using fentanyl, prescription opioids, tianeptine, loperamide at high doses, or other novel opioids can have overlapping opioid withdrawal.

### Stimulants

Exhaustion, depression, hypersomnia or stimulant craving can coexist with the restlessness and gastrointestinal symptoms of opioid-like withdrawal.

### Medical illness

Vomiting, diarrhea, fever, severe pain, agitation and sleep loss have many causes. A withdrawal label should not replace basic medical assessment when the presentation is atypical or severe.

---

## When urgent medical evaluation matters

Seek urgent assessment for:

- inability to stay awake or abnormal breathing;
- seizure;
- severe confusion or delirium;
- chest pain;
- fainting or dangerous palpitations;
- severe dehydration or inability to keep fluids down;
- severe uncontrolled vomiting or diarrhea;
- suicidal thoughts or inability to stay safe;
- pregnancy with significant withdrawal;
- serious infection, liver symptoms, or another medical illness;
- suspected exposure to an unknown or mislabeled product.

Profound sedation and respiratory depression point toward **intoxication, co-exposure, or another emergency**, not uncomplicated withdrawal.

## Acute management: what evidence actually supports

There is **no FDA-approved medication specifically for kratom withdrawal or 7-OH withdrawal**, and there is no universally accepted kratom-specific detox protocol.

### Supportive and symptom-focused care

Milder cases may be managed with clinical monitoring, hydration, sleep support, gastrointestinal symptom treatment, and treatment of anxiety or pain when appropriate.

The evidence base for specific non-opioid medications is largely extrapolated from opioid withdrawal and case reports rather than kratom-specific randomized trials [1,2,14].

### Buprenorphine

Buprenorphine or buprenorphine-naloxone has the largest published clinical experience for more severe kratom use disorder and now for concentrated 7-OH use disorder.

Evidence includes:

- case reports;
- small case series;
- a systematic review combined with case series;
- a 28-patient long-term kratom series;
- a 2026 nine-patient 7-OH retrospective series [14-18,23].

These reports are clinically important, but they are **not randomized trials**, and they do not create a universal conversion from grams of kratom or milligrams of 7-OH to a buprenorphine regimen.

### Methadone

A 2026 case report describes treatment of kratom use disorder with methadone in an opioid treatment program [19]. Another 7-OH case incorporated methadone during medically supervised care [22].

That establishes clinical precedent, not a standardized kratom-specific methadone protocol.

### Why this page does not publish medication doses

Medication selection and initiation can be affected by:

- current opioid receptor occupancy;
- other opioid exposure;
- co-use of sedatives;
- pregnancy;
- liver disease;
- withdrawal severity;
- prior treatment history;
- uncertain product chemistry.

A reader-facing evidence page should explain the options without pretending a case-report dose is a safe self-treatment recipe.

---

## Withdrawal management is not the same as treating KUD

A person can finish acute withdrawal and still face:

- craving;
- cue-triggered return to use;
- untreated pain;
- anxiety or depression;
- sleep disruption;
- easy retail access;
- social routines built around use;
- reduced opioid tolerance.

For people who meet criteria for kratom or 7-OH use disorder, continuing treatment may include addiction-medicine follow-up, behavioral treatment, management of co-occurring psychiatric or pain conditions, and—when clinically appropriate—medication treatment using opioid-use-disorder principles.

The evidence for long-term medication treatment is still observational, but the 28-patient buprenorphine series is important because it followed patients for months rather than treating withdrawal as the endpoint [15].

## Recovery after the acute syndrome

### Sleep

Insomnia is a common withdrawal complaint. Sleep can remain unstable after the worst physical symptoms improve.

Persistent insomnia can also reflect anxiety, depression, pain, stimulant use, alcohol/sedative withdrawal, or a pre-existing sleep disorder.

### Mood and anhedonia

Low mood, anxiety, irritability and reduced motivation can occur during withdrawal and early recovery [1-5].

Those symptoms should not automatically be labeled “post-acute withdrawal syndrome.” Persistent depression, panic, trauma symptoms or suicidality deserve independent assessment.

### Pain

Some people begin kratom for chronic pain or after losing access to prescription opioids. Stopping kratom can therefore expose both withdrawal-related pain sensitivity and the underlying pain condition [17,31].

A recovery plan that ignores the original pain problem is more fragile.

### Gastrointestinal recovery

Diarrhea, cramping, appetite change and nausea are commonly reported during withdrawal. Persistent or severe gastrointestinal symptoms should not be automatically attributed to kratom.

### Craving and learned cues

Retail access, familiar brands, work routines, online communities and locations associated with use can become strong cues.

Craving can outlast the most obvious physical symptoms.

---

## Does “post-acute withdrawal syndrome” apply?

Possibly—but the evidence is not mature enough for a precise kratom-specific PAWS diagnosis.

People report lingering:

- sleep disturbance;
- low mood;
- anxiety;
- reduced motivation;
- pain sensitivity;
- craving.

But those symptoms overlap heavily with the conditions for which kratom was often used in the first place.

The scientifically safer language is **persistent or post-acute symptoms during recovery**, with attention to alternative explanations, rather than assuming every symptom weeks later is caused by a single withdrawal mechanism.

---

## Return-to-use and overdose risk

This is where concentrated 7-OH changes the conversation.

Traditional kratom has historically been discussed separately from conventional high-potency opioids, but concentrated 7-OH products have stronger opioid-like pharmacology and increasingly documented opioid toxicity [20-29].

After abstinence, reduced opioid tolerance can make return to a previously tolerated opioid exposure more dangerous. Direct prospective data quantifying this risk specifically for 7-OH are not available, so the exact magnitude is unknown.

Practical harm-reduction principles are:

- do not assume a previous product amount remains tolerable after abstinence;
- treat unknown or relabeled products as compositionally uncertain;
- avoid combining opioid-active products with alcohol, benzodiazepines or other sedatives;
- keep naloxone available when opioid exposure is possible;
- remember that naloxone treats opioid toxicity, not product identity.

## Special populations

### Pregnancy and newborns

Case reports describe neonatal opioid-like withdrawal after maternal kratom use. Evidence is too sparse to define a safe exposure pattern.

Significant withdrawal during pregnancy warrants medical care rather than unsupervised cessation.

### Adolescents

The dependence and withdrawal literature is dominated by adults. Retail availability does not imply adolescent safety.

### Chronic pain

Pain can be both a reason for use and a relapse driver. Recovery planning should distinguish withdrawal-related pain amplification from the underlying pain condition.

### Psychiatric illness

Anxiety, depression and trauma symptoms can worsen during withdrawal or re-emerge when self-medication stops. Acute suicidality or severe psychiatric deterioration requires urgent care.

### Liver or kidney disease

Human kratom pharmacokinetics are incompletely characterized in significant organ impairment. Severe illness may alter exposure and complicate symptom attribution.

## Myths vs evidence

### “Kratom cannot cause withdrawal because it is a plant.”

**False.** Human studies document tolerance, dependence, craving and withdrawal after repeated kratom use [1-7].

### “Everyone who uses kratom daily becomes addicted.”

**False.** Physical dependence and KUD are not identical, and risk varies substantially across users.

### “7-OH is just concentrated kratom leaf.”

**Misleading.** Modern 7-OH products can contain opioid-active alkaloid exposure far outside the composition of ordinary leaf [20-29].

### “There is a standard kratom detox protocol.”

**False.** No standardized, FDA-approved kratom-specific withdrawal protocol exists.

### “Buprenorphine is proven for everyone with kratom withdrawal.”

**Overstated.** Published case series are promising for selected patients with more severe use disorder, but randomized trials and formal kratom-specific guidelines are lacking [14-18,23].

### “A mild withdrawal means there was no dependence.”

**False.** Withdrawal severity exists on a spectrum.

### “If symptoms last for weeks, it must be permanent brain damage.”

**Unsupported.** Persistent sleep, mood, pain and craving symptoms have multiple possible causes and should not be interpreted as proof of permanent injury.

## What remains unknown

The highest-value research gaps are:

- prospective withdrawal timelines for traditional leaf;
- head-to-head leaf vs extract vs concentrated 7-OH dependence studies;
- standardized symptom measurement for kratom withdrawal;
- controlled treatment trials;
- long-term recovery outcomes after KUD;
- prospective data on return-to-use and loss of tolerance;
- pregnancy pharmacokinetics and fetal/neonatal outcomes;
- treatment outcomes in people with chronic pain;
- interaction effects with alcohol, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids and stimulants;
- reliable product-level surveillance for 7-OH, pseudoindoxyl and semi-synthetic derivatives;
- whether specific non-opioid kratom pharmacology materially changes withdrawal treatment.

## Bottom line

Kratom withdrawal deserves neither dismissal nor exaggeration.

Traditional kratom can produce a genuine dependence syndrome with opioid-like physical symptoms, craving, sleep disturbance and mood effects. For many users the syndrome appears milder than withdrawal from high-potency conventional opioids, but “usually milder” is not the same as harmless or universal [1-7,13].

Concentrated 7-OH is a newer problem. The 2025-2026 literature documents escalating use, opioid-like withdrawal, use disorder, inpatient treatment and outpatient medication treatment, but the evidence remains too small to define a universal timeline or treatment algorithm [20-29].

The safest evidence-based framework is therefore:

1. identify the **actual product and exposure pattern**;
2. separate physical dependence from addiction;
3. recognize co-withdrawal and medical emergencies;
4. use opioid-withdrawal principles where clinically appropriate without pretending kratom and 7-OH are identical to fentanyl or heroin;
5. treat the underlying use disorder, pain, sleep or psychiatric condition—not just the acute symptoms;
6. preserve uncertainty where the human evidence is still emerging.
