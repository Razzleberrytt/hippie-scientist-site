## Bottom line

**Mitragynine is the most abundant alkaloid in kratom, it genuinely acts on opioid receptors, and your body converts some of it into a stronger opioid-active metabolite. Nearly everything else people claim about it — in both directions — outruns the evidence.**

Two things changed the picture in 2026:

1. **The science matured.** Controlled human pharmacokinetic studies now cover leaf powder *and* concentrated extracts. In-vitro assays using human opioid receptors mapped mitragynine across receptor types. And the NIH cleared an Investigational New Drug application for purified mitragynine, opening a Phase I trial for opioid use disorder. An IND is permission to investigate — not approval, not proof it works.

2. **The federal scheduling process changed in October.** The July 2026 notice concerning 7-OH did not become a temporary scheduling order. MGPI, MGM-15 and MGM-16 *were* temporarily scheduled on August 26. On October 9, HHS and DOJ announced revised **proposed** thresholds for 7-OH and MGPI (concentration or mass-ratio tests). These prospective thresholds are not yet effective, and the revised Federal Register notices must be checked when published. Older reporting may miss these distinctions.

The honest summary: mitragynine is a pharmacologically serious compound with a distinctive, incompletely mapped risk/benefit profile — neither harmless nor "just morphine."

## The distinction that matters most: leaf ≠ concentrate

Most confusion about kratom — online, in news coverage, even in some scientific commentary — comes from treating four different exposures as one thing.

| Exposure | What it is | What the evidence actually covers |
|---|---|---|
| **Kratom leaf** (powder, tea) | Dried botanical material; mitragynine-dominant with dozens of minor alkaloids | Largest human evidence base; composition varies by source and batch |
| **Concentrated kratom extract** | Processed to raise alkaloid content (e.g., ~40% mitragynine in studied extracts) | Growing controlled PK data; extract exposure ≠ leaf exposure |
| **Purified mitragynine** | Isolated compound | Early human experimental data; formal Phase I drug development underway; investigational, not approved |
| **Enhanced 7-OH products** | Products spiked with 7-hydroxymitragynine far above leaf levels | Rising toxicology and clinical-harm evidence; chemically distinct from ordinary leaf |
| **MGPI / MGM-15 / MGM-16** | Lab-derived or semi-synthetic related compounds | Strong preclinical opioid pharmacology; in temporary federal Schedule I since August 26, 2026 |

**The rule:** compound identity, formulation, and product composition determine what can legitimately be inferred. A study of concentrated 7-OH products says little about leaf tea. A leaf-tea study says little about 7-OH tablets. Anyone who blurs these categories — alarmist or enthusiast — is not doing evidence.

## What is mitragynine?

Mitragynine is a corynanthe-type indole alkaloid, the most abundant major alkaloid in most kratom preparations, occurring naturally in *Mitragyna speciosa*, a tree native to Southeast Asia.

Kratom is not a single-compound drug. Leaf and extracts contain mitragynine alongside speciociliatine, speciogynine, paynantheine, corynantheidine, 7-hydroxymitragynine, and numerous minor constituents — and the ratios shift with plant material, preparation, extraction, and processing.

That variability is why "kratom" as a research subject is inherently messier than "mitragynine" as a research subject, and why product-specific evidence matters so much.

---

## Pharmacology: what it does at receptors

Mitragynine binds the **mu-opioid receptor (MOR)** — the same receptor family targeted by morphine, oxycodone, and fentanyl — and interacts with kappa and delta opioid receptors plus non-opioid targets. Experimental work generally characterizes it as a relatively weak or **partial** MOR agonist compared with classical full agonists.

A 2026 **in-vitro receptor study** using human opioid receptor preparations (Hemby et al., PMID 41924140) combined binding assays, cAMP signaling, beta-arrestin recruitment, and molecular docking across kratom alkaloids. It confirmed substantial pharmacological diversity: mitragynine and 7-OH behave differently at the same receptors, which is part of why "kratom pharmacology" can't be reduced to a single mechanism.

### The "biased signaling" claim, honestly assessed

You'll often read that kratom alkaloids are "safer opioids" because some experiments show limited beta-arrestin-2 recruitment — a signaling pathway associated with opioid side effects.

That inference is too strong. Signaling bias is a receptor-level finding in experimental systems. It does not by itself prove a compound can't cause respiratory depression, dependence, withdrawal, or sedation in humans. The accurate description: **mechanistically interesting, clinically incomplete.**

## Pharmacokinetics: what human studies show

Human PK data have grown from tiny tea studies to controlled extract trials.

### Dried leaf powder

A 2024 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study (Huestis et al., PMID 38474495) tested 500–4,000 mg of dried leaf powder (~6.65–53.2 mg mitragynine). Median mitragynine Tmax was ~1.0–1.3 hours; the longest mean half-life was ~43 hours after a single dose and ~68 hours after repeated dosing. Notably, **7-OH was measurable in plasma after leaf administration** even though the product contained little of it — direct evidence of in-human metabolic formation.

### Concentrated extract

A 2026 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study extended the work to a concentrated dried extract (~39.5% mitragynine), characterizing single and repeated exposure with dose-dependent rises in both mitragynine and 7-OH plasma levels.

This matters because **extract PK can't be assumed to equal leaf PK** — concentration changes the exposure profile, not just the dose.

Neither study establishes a therapeutic dose, a safe consumer dose, or long-term safety.

### CYP3A4 and the 7-OH pathway

Human evidence supports CYP3A4-mediated conversion of mitragynine to 7-OH. In a controlled interaction study (PMID 38481700), the CYP3A4 inhibitor itraconazole **reduced 7-OH exposure while raising mitragynine exposure** — direct human proof that CYP3A4 sits on this metabolic pathway.

Practical consequence: enzyme inhibitors or inducers, liver function, genetics, and co-administered drugs can shift the mitragynine/7-OH balance. This section describes pharmacokinetics, not a suggestion to manipulate metabolism.

## The metabolite story: mitragynine → 7-OH → beyond

The parent compound isn't the whole pharmacological picture. Mitragynine is metabolized to **7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH)** — more potent at the mu-opioid receptor — plus additional metabolites including mitragynine 16-carboxylic acid and 9-hydroxycorynantheidine.

A 2026 validated analytical method measured 17 analytes simultaneously in human plasma (12 kratom alkaloids plus five mitragynine metabolites). The takeaway for interpreting any kratom study: **human exposure is a multi-analyte problem**, not "how much mitragynine was in the product."

Mitragynine pseudoindoxyl also appears in the metabolic conversation — but evidence about it shouldn't be used to imply ordinary kratom leaf equals commercial pseudoindoxyl products. Separate compound, separate evidence.

## Human studies: the actual record

### Acute mitragynine exposure

A controlled exploratory study (PMID 39724441) gave single mitragynine doses to healthy adults and measured subjective effects, neurocognition, vital signs, and pain tolerance. Most outcomes didn't change substantially; some low-exposure signals appeared in arousal/attention measures. Small sample, not designed to test efficacy or long-term safety.

### The NIH Phase I milestone

In June 2026, NIH announced an FDA-cleared IND for a purified mitragynine formulation had taken effect, authorizing an NIH-led Phase I trial investigating mitragynine for opioid use disorder.

This is genuinely significant — a specific purified-mitragynine formulation entering formal NIH drug development. It does **not** mean mitragynine is approved, proven effective for OUD, equivalent to any commercial kratom product, or appropriate for self-treatment. Phase I primarily tests safety, not efficacy.

**Important chronology:** The NIH press release suggests isolated mitragynine had not previously been studied in humans, but Prevete et al. (PMID 39724441) published a small placebo-controlled Phase I study in 2025. The 2026 milestone concerns NIH's own IND and planned formulation-specific trial, not the first human administration of purified mitragynine.

## Pain: promising mechanism, unproven medicine

Animal models repeatedly show antinociceptive effects from kratom and mitragynine. But a 2026 systematic review (44 studies: 28 preclinical, 16 human) found the preclinical signal far stronger than the human clinical evidence — human studies are small and mostly test experimental pain in healthy volunteers, not chronic-pain patients.

**Current verdict:** credible pharmacological rationale for analgesic research; insufficient human evidence to claim established efficacy for pain.

## Dependence and withdrawal

Repeated kratom exposure can produce tolerance, physical dependence, and withdrawal, with a presentation that can overlap opioid withdrawal — though severity and pattern vary by person and product. Risk climbs with duration, frequency, product concentration, individual metabolism, and co-use of other psychoactive substances.

What the evidence does **not** support: a universal withdrawal timeline, or reliable dose conversions between kratom, mitragynine, 7-OH, and prescription opioids. Anyone in severe withdrawal — breathing problems, confusion, seizures, inability to stay awake — needs medical assessment, not an internet taper protocol.

## Safety: what the evidence shows and doesn't

### Respiratory effects

Mitragynine shows less respiratory depression than classical full MOR agonists in several experimental models. "Less" ≠ "none" — risk shifts with dose, formulation, 7-OH exposure, and especially co-use with CNS depressants (opioids, benzodiazepines, alcohol). The strongest respiratory-depression signals in the current product market involve **concentrated 7-OH and synthetic derivatives**, not ordinary mitragynine-dominant leaf.

### Liver injury

Kratom-associated liver injury is reported as an uncommon but potentially serious event. Causality is hard to pin down amid product variability, co-medications, contaminants, and individual susceptibility.

### Cardiovascular and neurologic events

Case reports and surveillance literature describe cardiovascular and neurologic adverse events including seizures. Polysubstance use and poorly characterized products make causality difficult to establish in most reports.

### The polydrug multiplier

The single biggest real-world risk amplifier is combining kratom alkaloids with other substances — particularly opioids, benzodiazepines, alcohol, and sedatives. A favorable profile in one controlled experiment is not protection against a dangerous combination.

## Drug interactions

Mitragynine shows CYP3A4-mediated metabolism and CYP2D6 inhibition signals across human and in-vitro work. Consequences can include altered mitragynine exposure, altered 7-OH formation, and changed exposure to co-administered drugs using the same pathways.

Because the evidence mixes human studies, microsomal experiments, and modeling, **no specific interaction's magnitude can be predicted from enzyme tables alone**. For narrow-therapeutic-index or CNS-active medications, interaction risk deserves professional review — not internet reassurance.

## Legal status — current controls versus proposed changes (checked October 12, 2026)

> **Regulatory status is changing.** The HHS and DOJ announcements dated October 9 describe proposed future actions as well as already-operative controls. A notice of intent is **not** a temporary scheduling order. Check the Federal Register for official notice text, publication dates and any later orders.

### What is in effect right now?

- **Mitragynine itself is not federally scheduled** under the Controlled Substances Act as of this date.
- **Mitragynine pseudoindoxyl (MGPI), MGM-15 and MGM-16 have been temporarily in Schedule I since August 26, 2026.** This existing order remains operative while DEA revisits its approach. The newly recommended MGPI concentration and ratio thresholds **are not yet legal exemptions** from that order.
- **7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) has not yet been placed into temporary Schedule I** by a final DEA temporary scheduling order. DEA announced it would issue a revised notice of intent after the earlier July proposal did not result in such an order.

These are federal statements; state and local laws may independently restrict kratom, mitragynine, or related products.

### Federal timeline

| Date | Documented development |
|---|---|
| July 6, 2026 | DEA published notices of intent concerning 7-OH and the three related substances; a notice alone did not schedule 7-OH |
| August 26, 2026 | DEA's temporary scheduling order for **MGPI, MGM-15 and MGM-16** took effect |
| October 9, 2026 | HHS published updated recommendations for 7-OH and MGPI, and DOJ announced revised DEA notices of intent |
| October 14, 2026 | **Anticipated notice-publication date, not yet verified as a published Federal Register notice** when this review was prepared; recheck the actual document |
| November 2026 or later | A possible 7-OH temporary order could follow the statutory notice period; any specific effective date depends on actual publication and a separate order, not an estimate |

### Recommended thresholds — proposals, not yet operative controls

HHS recommended that the prospective 7-OH and MGPI temporary controls apply when either substance individually, or the **combined amount of both**, meets **either** of the following conditions:

- Its concentration **exceeds** 1 mg per gram of solid product or 1 mg per mL of liquid product; **or**
- The **mass ratio** of mitragynine to 7-OH, MGPI, or their combined mass is **less than 100:1**.

Those are **HHS recommendations incorporated into the announced notice approach**, not an already-effective replacement for the August 26 MGPI scheduling order. The exact regulatory text and legal effects must be reassessed against the Federal Register notice and any subsequent temporary order. HHS emphasized that these criteria distinguish product compositions; they are **not safety thresholds**.

### What this means for mitragynine and kratom leaf

The agency approach seeks to distinguish trace constituents of botanical kratom from products intentionally enhanced with more potent compounds. But **not federally scheduled** does not mean FDA-approved, lawful for every use or sale, or free of state restrictions. Evidence on a natural leaf preparation cannot be transferred directly to a purified alkaloid or concentrated enhanced product.

## Research gaps

The most important unanswered questions:

## FAQ

**Is mitragynine the same as kratom?**
No. Mitragynine is one major alkaloid in kratom. Whole-leaf kratom contains many additional constituents, and the evidence for each doesn't automatically transfer.

**Is mitragynine an opioid?**
It's an alkaloid with genuine opioid-receptor activity, especially at the mu-opioid receptor. Its pharmacology differs from classical full agonists — but that doesn't make it non-opioid or risk-free.

**Does mitragynine turn into 7-OH in the body?**
Yes. Human studies confirm metabolic formation of 7-hydroxymitragynine from mitragynine, with CYP3A4 playing a key role. This is different from consuming products spiked with 7-OH.

**Is mitragynine legal?**
As of October 12, 2026, mitragynine is not federally scheduled; MGPI, MGM-15 and MGM-16 are already temporarily Schedule I. Proposed thresholds for 7-OH and MGPI are not yet in effect. State laws may differ.

**Is mitragynine FDA-approved for anything?**
No. The 2026 NIH IND authorizes investigation of a purified formulation in a clinical trial. That's research, not approval.

**Does the NIH trial prove mitragynine treats opioid addiction?**
No. Phase I trials primarily assess safety. Efficacy requires later-phase evidence that doesn't exist yet.

**What's the difference between kratom leaf and 7-OH products?**
Leaf is a mitragynine-dominant botanical mixture. 7-OH products are concentrated/enhanced preparations built around 7-hydroxymitragynine, a more potent opioid-active compound. Different products, different evidence, different risk profiles — and as of October 2026, distinct existing and proposed legal treatment.

**Does "biased signaling" make mitragynine safe?**
No. It's a receptor-level experimental finding, not a guarantee against respiratory depression, dependence, or withdrawal.

**Is there a proven dose?**
No. Research studies use defined exposures, but nothing in the human literature establishes a generally safe or effective consumer dose. This page doesn't provide dosing recommendations.
