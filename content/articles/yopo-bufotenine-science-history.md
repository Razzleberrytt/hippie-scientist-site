# Yopo: a remarkable plant with a long human history

**Quick answer:** Yopo is associated with traditional Indigenous uses of seeds from **Anadenanthera** trees in South America, particularly *Anadenanthera peregrina*. Its chemistry includes **bufotenine**, a molecule related to DMT. But the plant, the historical traditions, and the isolated molecule are not the same thing. [1–4]

## What is yopo?

The name **yopo** is often used for traditions involving a South American tree called *Anadenanthera peregrina*. **Anadenanthera** is a botanical genus—meaning a group of related plant species.

You can think of a genus as a family grouping in a plant identification book. Different species within the group can have related appearances and chemistry while still being distinct plants.

Researchers also describe traditional uses associated with other *Anadenanthera* species under regional names such as **vilca**. Names vary between languages, regions, and communities. A single English-language label does not capture every tradition. [1]

## Why are researchers interested in the seeds?

Scientists studying these plants have identified **tryptamines** in them. Tryptamines are a chemical family whose members share a similar underlying arrangement of atoms.

An **atom** is a small building block of matter. A **molecule** is a group of atoms joined together. Different arrangements of those atoms can give chemicals different properties.

One well-known chemical linked to *Anadenanthera* seeds is **bufotenine**, also named **5-HO-DMT**. The "HO" means it contains a small group of oxygen and hydrogen atoms called a **hydroxy group**. This name describes chemistry. It does not tell us the exact experience a person would have. [2–4]

Seed material can contain more than one chemical. It is inaccurate to describe every yopo-related traditional material as a pure dose of bufotenine.

## Is bufotenine the same as DMT?

No. **N,N-DMT**, the chemical most people mean by the word DMT, is related to bufotenine, but it is a different molecule.

Bufotenine's chemistry affects how it interacts with **receptors**. A receptor is a receiving spot on or in a cell that responds to a chemical message.

Scientists have investigated its action at serotonin receptors. **Serotonin** is one of the body's chemical messengers, involved in several brain and body functions. Laboratory studies can test whether a molecule influences a particular receptor, but they cannot reproduce an entire human experience. [2]

Another difference is how easily a substance enters brain tissue. The **blood–brain barrier** is a selective boundary between circulating blood and the brain. It allows some substances through more readily than others. Bufotenine appears to cross differently from some of its close chemical relatives. [2,4]

## How far back does the history go?

Archaeologists study the human past by examining objects and remains. An **archaeological site** is a place where physical evidence of past human activity has been found.

A 2007 historical and archaeological review described evidence connecting *Anadenanthera* seeds with ritual practice in South America around **3,000 years ago**. The paper discussed seeds and objects from sites including areas of Chile, Argentina, and Bolivia. [1]

That is meaningful evidence of historical practice. But it is not a diary of exactly what every participant saw, felt, or believed.

The physical objects can suggest that certain plants were important in particular communities and times. They cannot prove that every Indigenous society used the same species, had the same beliefs, or experienced identical effects.

## Why cultural context matters

**Ethnobotany** is the study of relationships between people and plants. It includes how plants are named, understood, cared for, and used within communities.

It would be misleading to describe yopo only as an interesting chemical delivery system. Indigenous knowledge and living traditions belong to people and communities with their own histories.

Modern scientific labels are useful for studying molecules. But those labels should not replace the cultural meaning of a plant in a specific setting. The evidence also comes from sources with different limits, such as archaeological finds, written reports, and modern interviews. [1]

## Is yopo the same as ayahuasca?

No. Both names are associated with South American Indigenous traditions and may come up in discussions of psychedelics, but they refer to different botanical and cultural contexts.

A name such as *ayahuasca* does not identify a single universal plant formulation or experience either. Scientists need to specify the plants, chemical composition, and context being studied before making comparisons.

Asking whether two traditions are "the same psychedelic" hides more than it explains.

## What does the science actually establish?

There are at least three levels of evidence:

**1. Plant chemistry.** Researchers can identify molecules present in analyzed samples. But samples can vary, and identifying a compound does not by itself tell us what all historical materials contained.

**2. Laboratory pharmacology.** **Pharmacology** means studying how substances affect the body. Receptor tests can reveal interactions that help explain possible effects, but they cannot reliably reproduce an experience.

**3. Human cultural history.** Archaeology and historical accounts help us understand some practices and contexts. They don't establish medical effectiveness, safety, or every person's subjective response.

The available evidence across these categories is valuable precisely because each answers a different kind of question. [1–4]

## A note on risks and uncertainty

Natural origin is not a guarantee of safety. Some plant-derived chemicals can have powerful effects or interact with medications. Modern controlled human studies of bufotenine are far more limited than the huge range of internet claims about it would suggest.

There is also no reliable basis for equating one plant tradition with another by comparing only a single molecule. This article intentionally does not provide preparation or dosing instructions.

## Common questions

**Does yopo come from a mushroom?** No. It is associated with seeds of *Anadenanthera* trees.

**Is bufotenine another name for 5-HO-DMT?** Yes. These are two names for the same molecule.

**Do all Indigenous groups have the same yopo tradition?** No. Cultures, names, histories, and practices differ.

**Can modern neuroscience tell us what people saw thousands of years ago?** No. Chemistry can help explain possibilities, not reconstruct individual experiences.

## How to check the research on yopo

Numbers like **[1]** refer to papers in the References list. A **PMID** is an identifying number for a paper in **PubMed**, a public research database. A **DOI** is a lasting identifier for a publication. These identifiers let you trace a claim; they are not safety certificates.

This subject combines different ways of knowing. **Archaeology** examines physical traces of the past. **Ethnobotany** studies relationships between people and plants. **Chemistry** identifies molecules and their structure. **Pharmacology** examines how substances act on living systems. An archaeological review can document historical evidence without revealing every person's experience. A chemical analysis may identify bufotenine without proving all plants or traditional materials had the same composition. A receptor experiment cannot replace either cultural context or human safety evidence. [1–4]
