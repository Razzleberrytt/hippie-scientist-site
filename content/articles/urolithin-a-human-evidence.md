> **Bottom line:** Urolithin A is one of the more credible newer healthy-aging compounds because it has randomized human trials. But the best current summary is **promising, not proven**: some muscle and mitochondrial outcomes are favorable, while several important primary endpoints have not clearly beaten placebo.

## What urolithin A is

Urolithin A is a gut-microbiome-derived metabolite that can form after exposure to ellagitannin and ellagic-acid precursors from foods such as pomegranate, walnuts, and some berries.

Interest centers on mitochondrial quality control and mitophagy, the recycling of damaged mitochondria. That mechanism is scientifically plausible. It does not by itself show that a supplement slows human aging or improves physical function.

The human trials matter more.

## The older-adult trial had mixed results

A 2022 randomized clinical trial enrolled adults aged 65 to 90 and tested 1,000 mg/day of urolithin A for four months.[2]

The trial did **not** show a significant advantage over placebo on two major outcomes: six-minute walk distance and maximal ATP production in hand muscle.

Some secondary measures were more encouraging. Muscle endurance improved on selected tests, and several metabolic or inflammatory biomarkers moved in potentially favorable directions.[2]

That is a useful signal, but it is not the same as a clean positive trial.

## The middle-aged trial also missed its primary endpoint

Another 2022 randomized trial tested 500 mg or 1,000 mg/day in middle-aged adults for four months.[3]

The study reported improvements in some muscle-strength measures and mitochondrial or inflammatory biomarkers. However, **peak power output—the primary endpoint—did not significantly improve**.[3]

This is why the page should separate primary outcomes from secondary ones rather than compressing the study into a single “works” verdict.

## What the 2026 systematic review adds

A 2026 systematic review identified only **five randomized trials totaling 236 participants**.[1]

The authors could quantitatively pool only the six-minute walk test, and even that analysis relied on two trials. The pooled result favored urolithin A directionally but was not statistically conclusive, and the certainty of evidence was rated **low**.[1]

Other outcomes were too heterogeneous for a stable pooled estimate.

That is probably the most useful current summary of the field: there are real human trials, but the evidence base is still small, short, and inconsistent across outcomes.

## Is mitophagy proven as the reason for benefit?

Not in a simple clinical sense.

Trials have reported biomarker and molecular changes consistent with mitochondrial effects, but a biomarker moving in the expected direction does not prove that it caused an improvement in strength or endurance.

Mechanism and clinical outcome should stay separate.

## Sponsorship context matters

Several prominent urolithin A trials involve investigators or funding linked to the company developing the commercial ingredient.[2][3]

That does not make the findings invalid.

It does make **independent replication** especially valuable, because a small literature becomes much stronger when outside groups reproduce the result using transparent methods.

## Is urolithin A a longevity supplement?

There is no human evidence showing that urolithin A extends lifespan.

The human studies focus mainly on muscle performance, mitochondrial biology, and related biomarkers. Those are reasonable healthy-aging targets, but they are not interchangeable with longevity itself.

## Safety

Short-term randomized trials generally describe urolithin A as well tolerated in the studied populations, with no major between-group adverse-event signal.[2][3]

That does not establish multi-year safety, pregnancy safety, or comprehensive interaction data.

## Bottom line

Urolithin A deserves a serious compound page because it has moved beyond animal-only research.

The evidence is strongest for **interesting muscle and mitochondrial signals**, not for a universal performance effect or a proven anti-aging outcome. The 2026 review reinforces that caution: small trials, short durations, low-certainty pooled evidence, and a real need for larger independent replication.
