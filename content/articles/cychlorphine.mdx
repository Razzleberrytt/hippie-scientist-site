# Cychlorphine: Emerging Orphine Opioid & Fatal Overdose Signal

> **Emergency warning:** Cychlorphine is an opioid. Suspected poisoning with abnormal breathing or unresponsiveness is an emergency; administer naloxone if available and call emergency services.

## Quick answer

**Cychlorphine is a newer synthetic opioid in the benzimidazol-2-one family increasingly referred to as the “orphines.”**

Its longer chemical name is **N-propionitrile chlorphine**.

CFSRE reported first detecting it in mid-2024 and later issued a January 2026 alert describing an increase in fatal-overdose detections.

## What are orphines?

Orphines are structurally distinct from nitazenes but occupy the same rapidly evolving synthetic-opioid ecosystem.

Examples include chlorphine, cychlorphine, spirochlorphine, spirobrorphine, dichloro desmethylchlorphine, and related analogues.

## Why they are emerging now

Forensic surveillance suggests the opioid market changes when precursor or core-structure controls disrupt an established family.

After nitazene controls expanded, orphine-type opioids became more visible.

That does not prove a simple one-for-one replacement mechanism, but the timing is important for surveillance.

## Why the evidence grade is very low

Cychlorphine is currently an **early-warning compound**.

Evidence is dominated by forensic detections, fatality investigations, analytical identification, and structural/pharmacological inference.

Controlled human pharmacokinetics and clinical dose-response studies are absent.


## Why cychlorphine deserves attention despite limited human research

Cychlorphine moved from first forensic detection to a fatal-overdose signal quickly. That pattern is important because it shows how a novel opioid can become clinically relevant before a conventional research literature develops.

In this situation, forensic surveillance is not a substitute for controlled human studies. It is an early-warning system.

Repeated detection in fatal cases is enough to justify serious concern while still preserving uncertainty about exact human potency, metabolism, and duration.

## Potency estimates need context

Laboratory opioid-receptor assays can identify cychlorphine as a powerful mu-opioid agonist.

Those results should not be converted directly into a fentanyl-equivalent or morphine-equivalent dose for people. Assay system, administration route, absorption, protein binding, active metabolites, and individual physiology can all change real-world toxicity.

The useful conclusion is that **very small errors in concentration may matter**.

## Naloxone and emergency response

Signs of opioid overdose include slow or absent breathing, blue or gray lips, inability to wake, pinpoint pupils, and collapse.

Naloxone is appropriate when cychlorphine or another opioid is suspected. Because the exact dose and duration of novel opioids may be unknown, repeated naloxone and ongoing medical observation can be necessary.

A temporary response to naloxone does not mean the danger has fully passed.

## Why routine testing may miss it

New opioids often appear before standard toxicology panels are updated.

A routine fentanyl or opiate screen may therefore fail to identify cychlorphine. Specialized mass-spectrometry testing may be required.

That makes clinical recognition of an opioid toxidrome especially important.

## The market-shift lesson

Cychlorphine belongs to the newer orphine/benzimidazolone opioid wave that emerged as nitazene availability changed.

Its appearance reinforces a broader safety principle: **controlling one synthetic-opioid family does not guarantee that the illicit market becomes less potent; it may shift to another chemical scaffold instead**.

## Bottom line

Cychlorphine is exactly the kind of substance that justifies maintaining a live NPS section: a new opioid family can begin appearing in fatal casework before most clinicians or consumers even recognize the name.
