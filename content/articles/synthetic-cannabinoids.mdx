# Synthetic Cannabinoids: MDMB-4en-PINACA, 5F-ADB, Spice/K2 and Why They Are Not “Synthetic Weed”

## Quick answer

Synthetic cannabinoids are a large group of laboratory-made drugs that activate cannabinoid receptors, often very differently from THC.

Calling them “synthetic weed” is misleading.

Many modern synthetic cannabinoid receptor agonists can produce **much stronger CB1 receptor activation**, shorter margins between expected and severe effects, and toxic syndromes that are unusual with ordinary cannabis.

## Current examples

Recent U.S. forensic surveillance has continued to detect compounds such as:

- MDMB-4en-PINACA;
- 5F-ADB / 5F-MDMB-PINACA;
- newer INACA/PINACA derivatives;
- related precursor and analogue compounds.

The exact market mix changes over time as controls and precursor availability change.

## Why these compounds can behave so differently from THC

THC is a partial cannabinoid receptor agonist with a broad, well-characterized human exposure history.

Many synthetic cannabinoids are **high-efficacy full agonists** at CB1 receptors.

That can produce a much more abrupt and unpredictable physiological response.

## Severe toxicity

Reported severe effects across the class include:

- seizures;
- extreme agitation;
- delirium;
- rapid heart rate;
- hypertension or hypotension;
- vomiting;
- kidney injury;
- loss of consciousness;
- respiratory complications;
- death.

Different compounds can produce different patterns, so class-level toxicity should not be treated as a precise prediction for every analogue.

## MDMB-4en-PINACA and 5F-ADB remain relevant

CFSRE's 2026 correctional-facility alert highlighted MDMB-4en-PINACA and 5F-ADB as persistent high-potency synthetic cannabinoids.

The alert described numerous death investigations in which metabolites or degradation products of these compounds were identified.

That is important current evidence that these are not merely historical “Spice-era” drugs.

## The hotspot problem

Synthetic cannabinoids are often dissolved and applied onto plant material or paper.

If distribution is uneven, one part of a product can contain far more active drug than another.

That means two pieces from the same package can produce very different exposures.

This product-level variability is a major reason dose assumptions based on prior pieces can fail.

## Correctional-facility paper is a special forensic context

Recent surveillance has found synthetic cannabinoids on impregnated paper products.

The analytical challenge is substantial because small quantities of highly potent compounds may be distributed across paper, letters, or other materials.

The broader lesson applies outside correctional settings too: **appearance is not identity**.

## Bottom line

Synthetic cannabinoids are not safer or more predictable versions of cannabis.

The combination of high receptor efficacy, rapidly changing chemistry, tiny active quantities, and uneven product distribution can make severe toxicity much easier to trigger than a casual “synthetic weed” label suggests.
