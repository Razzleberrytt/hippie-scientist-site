# Novel Sedatives Beyond Benzos: Dicloqualone, 2-Methoxyqualone and Quaalude Analogues

## Quick answer

The research-chemical depressant market is broader than designer benzodiazepines.

One smaller but recurring family is made up of **quinazolinone sedatives related to methaqualone**, the drug historically sold as Quaalude in the United States and Mandrax in some other markets. Modern names include **dicloqualone (SL-164)**, **2-methoxyqualone**, etaqualone, mebroqualone, methylmethaqualone and other analogues.

The evidence for many of these compounds is dramatically thinner than the evidence for prescription benzodiazepines. Some have old medicinal-chemistry or animal data; others have only recent forensic identification.

That means the correct default is **uncertainty**, not the assumption that an analogue is a predictable recreation of historical methaqualone.

## Why old sedative chemistry keeps resurfacing

Research-chemical markets often revisit molecules from older pharmaceutical programs. A compound can exist in patents or pharmacology literature for decades before appearing in a modern online or illicit-drug market.

That history can create a misleading story: if chemists studied it long ago, people may assume its human effects must be well understood.

Often they are not.

A compound may have enough historical data to suggest sedative-hypnotic activity while lacking modern controlled human pharmacokinetics, dose-response data, long-term safety studies, or systematic interaction research.

## Dicloqualone / SL-164

CFSRE confirmed dicloqualone in U.S. drug material and describes it as a quinazolinone structurally related to methaqualone.

Available evidence supports sedative GABAergic activity. But modern controlled human safety data are sparse.

The sample history is also instructive: forensic drug material can contain multiple active substances, so discovering dicloqualone does not imply that users are always encountering it as a pure isolated powder.

See the dedicated [dicloqualone / SL-164 evidence review](/articles/dicloqualone/).

## 2-Methoxyqualone

2-Methoxyqualone is an even newer signal. CFSRE reported confirmed U.S. drug-material detections in 2026.

At the time of the monograph, published pharmacological potency data and CFSRE toxicology cases were not available.

That does not make the substance harmless. It means **market availability has outrun the evidence**.

See [2-methoxyqualone: the 2026 evidence gap](/articles/2-methoxyqualone/).

## Why “Quaalude analogue” is not a safety conclusion

Methaqualone is familiar culturally, so the phrase “Quaalude analogue” can sound almost like a standardized product class.

It is not.

Structural analogues may differ in receptor activity, metabolism, active metabolites, elimination, seizure risk, organ toxicity, and therapeutic index.

Even if two compounds produce broadly sedative effects, that does not create a valid human potency conversion.

## Depressant combinations are the clearest cross-family hazard

The exact pharmacology of a new qualone analogue may be poorly characterized, but one safety principle is straightforward: combining sedatives can make impairment much more dangerous.

Important co-exposures include:

- opioids;
- alcohol;
- benzodiazepines and thienodiazepines;
- gabapentinoids;
- sleep medications;
- sedating antihistamines;
- other novel depressants.

With opioids, profound sedation can overlap with respiratory depression. Naloxone can reverse an opioid component but does not reverse a non-opioid sedative.

## Product identity remains uncertain

A powder or tablet sold as a methaqualone analogue cannot be identified by appearance.

The label may name the wrong analogue, the concentration may be inaccurate, or another depressant may be present.

For a novel sedative, subjective effects are especially poor evidence of identity because many depressants can produce overlapping drowsiness, disinhibition, amnesia, and poor coordination.

## When sedation becomes an emergency

Urgent medical care is appropriate for inability to wake, abnormal breathing, cyanosis, seizure, collapse, severe confusion, or repeated vomiting with reduced consciousness.

If an opioid may be involved, administer naloxone when available while emergency help is contacted.

## Bottom line

Research-chemical sedatives should not be reduced to “RC benzos.”

Methaqualone-related compounds are reappearing, including molecules with almost no modern human evidence. A historical drug name or structural resemblance does not make a new analogue predictable.

The useful approach is the same one used across the NPS library: **confirm identity when possible, separate structural analogy from human evidence, assume combination risk is real, and make uncertainty visible rather than filling it with confident guesses.**
