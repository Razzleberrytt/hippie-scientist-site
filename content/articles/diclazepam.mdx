# Diclazepam: Complete Pharmacokinetics, Active Metabolites, Toxicology & Withdrawal Monograph

> **Emergency safety:** Severe sleepiness, inability to wake, abnormal or slowed breathing, collapse, repeated vomiting with reduced consciousness, severe confusion, or suspected mixed opioid/sedative exposure requires emergency care. If opioid exposure is possible, naloxone can reverse the opioid component while emergency services are contacted. Abrupt cessation after benzodiazepine-type dependence can also cause seizures or delirium [9-12].

## Quick answer

**Diclazepam—also called 2-chlorodiazepam—is a diazepam-related designer benzodiazepine with unusually informative human metabolism data.** A controlled analytical self-experiment estimated a long parent elimination half-life of roughly **42 hours** and identified the active benzodiazepines **delorazepam, lorazepam, and lormetazepam** as metabolites [1].

That metabolite pattern makes diclazepam more complicated than a label suggests. A toxicology result may reflect parent drug, active metabolites, or both. Detection of lorazepam-related analytes does not automatically mean a pharmaceutical lorazepam product was taken.

Real-world evidence is substantial for an RC benzodiazepine: diclazepam appears in poison-center surveillance, drug-facilitated-crime investigations, hundreds of forensic blood samples, and multi-year impaired-driving datasets [2-6].

The core risk profile is therefore well supported: **benzodiazepine-type impairment + long persistence + active metabolites + polysubstance risk + dependence/withdrawal potential**.

## History and market emergence

Diclazepam was known in pharmaceutical research before the modern internet RC market. It later resurfaced as a designer benzodiazepine sold through online NPS channels.

By 2014, researchers had analytically characterized marketed material and published direct human metabolism/pharmacokinetic data [1].

Subsequent surveillance showed that diclazepam was not merely an online curiosity:
- it appeared in poison-center exposure reports [3];
- it was identified in a drug-facilitated sexual-assault investigation [2];
- it became common enough to appear in hundreds of forensic blood cases [5];
- it was included in multi-year U.S. impaired-driving surveillance [6].

International control followed in 2021, and permanent U.S. Schedule I control became effective in 2026 [13-15].

## Chemistry and relationship to diazepam

Diclazepam is structurally related to diazepam, with chlorine substitution altering the molecule.

That comparison is useful for orientation. It is **not** a validated potency conversion.

Small structural changes can alter:
- receptor binding;
- distribution;
- metabolism;
- active-metabolite profile;
- duration;
- accumulation.

In diclazepam's case, metabolism is especially important because the body forms several pharmacologically active benzodiazepines [1].

## Pharmacodynamics

Diclazepam produces benzodiazepine-type GABA-A positive modulation.

Expected pharmacodynamic consequences include:
- anxiolysis;
- sedation;
- impaired memory;
- psychomotor slowing;
- reduced coordination;
- impaired judgment;
- muscle relaxation.

The available human literature is much stronger on metabolism and forensic detection than on controlled receptor/pharmacodynamic dose-response studies.

Therefore, this page does not provide a numerical diazepam equivalence or a recreational potency ranking.

## Pharmacokinetics and active metabolites

The landmark 2014 study provides the best direct human PK evidence [1].

### Long parent elimination

The study estimated an elimination half-life of approximately **42 hours** in the single studied participant.

That is preliminary, not a population average. Still, it establishes that diclazepam can persist far longer than many short-acting benzodiazepine-type drugs.

### Active metabolites

Investigators identified:
- **delorazepam**;
- **lorazepam**;
- **lormetazepam**.

These metabolites extended the analytical footprint after parent diclazepam declined [1].

This matters clinically and forensically because:
- impairment may outlast obvious parent-drug effects;
- repeated exposure can produce accumulation;
- metabolite results can complicate source attribution;
- a long metabolite tail can delay or blur withdrawal onset.

## Testing lesson from the human study

The 2014 study also tested immunochemical detection.

Only one evaluated immunoassay reliably detected the exposure, and positive results were limited to selected urine samples [1].

That finding directly supports a broader rule:

**A negative routine benzodiazepine screen does not reliably exclude diclazepam exposure.**

Modern definitive LC-MS/MS or LC-HRMS testing with current parent/metabolite targets is more informative.

## Human toxicity and poison-center evidence

The U.S. National Poison Data System study reviewed 234 single-agent designer-benzodiazepine exposures reported from 2014-2017 [3].

Across the DBZD group:
- drowsiness/lethargy was common;
- slurred speech occurred;
- some cases required intubation;
- hospital and ICU admissions occurred;
- one death was reported.

Because this dataset pooled multiple designer benzodiazepines, those percentages should not be re-labeled as diclazepam-specific incidence.

The correct inference is that designer-benzodiazepine poisoning can become clinically significant even when the individual compound has limited trial data.

## Impaired driving and forensic evidence

Diclazepam has one of the stronger forensic datasets among RC benzodiazepines.

A 2020 study reported **334 diclazepam-positive cases** among a larger set of living forensic and medico-legal samples [5].

An earlier study also linked designer-benzodiazepine blood findings with clinical impairment in drivers and other offenders [4].

The 2017-2021 U.S. DUID study identified diclazepam/delorazepam among the designer-benzodiazepine pairs found in blood samples from suspected impaired drivers [6].

These datasets establish real-world impairment relevance.

They do **not** create a universal concentration at which every person is impaired.

## Drug-facilitated crime

A 2018 forensic report identified diclazepam in material from a drug-facilitated sexual-assault investigation using multiple analytical techniques [2].

This case highlights two issues:
1. designer benzodiazepines can be intentionally or unintentionally involved in incapacitation contexts;
2. unambiguous chemical identification may require multiple analytical methods because related compounds and isomers can complicate interpretation.

A single case cannot establish prevalence, but it is high-value evidence for forensic relevance.

## Acute toxicity

Expected acute toxicity includes:
- somnolence;
- slurred speech;
- impaired coordination;
- amnesia;
- confusion;
- falls and crashes;
- reduced consciousness.

Severe respiratory depression is most concerning in mixed exposures—especially opioids, alcohol, or other sedatives [8-11].

Because active metabolites persist, the clinical course may outlast the period a person expects from the parent compound alone.

## Interactions and polysubstance risk

### Opioids

Opioids depress breathing; benzodiazepines reduce arousal and protective responses. Co-exposure increases overdose risk.

### Alcohol

Alcohol adds sedation, memory impairment, motor impairment, and respiratory risk.

### Other sedatives

Other benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, gabapentinoids, sedating antihistamines, and other CNS depressants can produce additive effects.

### Metabolic complexity

Diclazepam itself produces active benzodiazepine metabolites, meaning the pharmacologic mixture can evolve over time even without another product being taken [1].

## Tolerance and physical dependence

Repeated diclazepam exposure can reasonably be considered dependence-capable based on:
- direct benzodiazepine pharmacology;
- long persistence;
- active metabolites;
- well-established benzodiazepine-class adaptation.

**Physical dependence is not the same as addiction.**

Dependence means the nervous system has adapted and withdrawal can occur when exposure falls.

A benzodiazepine use disorder involves broader behavioral features such as loss of control, craving, continued use despite harm, or major life disruption.

## Withdrawal

There is no validated diclazepam-specific withdrawal calendar.

Benzodiazepine withdrawal can include:
- rebound anxiety or insomnia;
- tremor;
- autonomic activation;
- agitation;
- perceptual disturbance;
- confusion/delirium;
- seizures.

Diclazepam's long parent half-life and active metabolites make **delayed withdrawal** pharmacologically plausible [1].

The 2025 multisociety benzodiazepine tapering guideline advises against abrupt discontinuation in people likely to be physically dependent and recommends individualized clinical management [12].

This site does not publish a DIY diclazepam-to-diazepam conversion or self-taper schedule.

## Treatment and support

### Acute intoxication

Clinical management is primarily supportive:
- airway and breathing assessment;
- neurologic observation;
- evaluation for injury;
- assessment for opioids/alcohol/other sedatives;
- treatment of complications.

If an opioid may be involved, naloxone can reverse the opioid component.

Flumazenil is a clinician-administered antagonist with important seizure/withdrawal limitations and is not an at-home reversal tool [10].

### Dependence and withdrawal

People using diclazepam regularly should seek clinical assessment before abrupt cessation.

For U.S. treatment access:
- [SAMHSA FindTreatment.gov](https://findtreatment.gov/)
- SAMHSA National Helpline: 1-800-662-HELP (4357)

Seizure, delirium, severe confusion, inability to wake, or abnormal breathing warrants emergency care.

## Drug testing and toxicology

Diclazepam is a good example of why toxicology requires metabolite knowledge.

Useful analytical targets can include:
- parent diclazepam;
- delorazepam;
- lorazepam;
- lormetazepam [1].

This creates a source-attribution problem: some metabolites are also known pharmaceutical benzodiazepines.

A toxicologist must interpret:
- which compounds are present together;
- concentration relationships;
- specimen timing;
- medication history;
- assay scope;
- expected metabolic pathways.

A single metabolite result cannot always identify the source drug with certainty.

## Forensic interpretation

Diclazepam concentrations occur across a wide real-world range [4,5].

A measured concentration does not automatically establish:
- the dose taken;
- exact time of exposure;
- degree of impairment at a previous time;
- whether diclazepam alone caused a crash, assault vulnerability, or death.

Tolerance, active metabolites, co-drugs, and time between event and collection are essential context.

## Epidemiology and current trends

Diclazepam was prominent in some European forensic datasets during the late 2010s, including hundreds of positive cases in one Norwegian study [5].

U.S. DUID surveillance also documented diclazepam/delorazepam during 2017-2021 [6].

However, NPS markets change quickly. Historic prominence should not be presented as current national prevalence without current surveillance.

The broader 2025-2026 literature still treats designer benzodiazepines as a persistent forensic and clinical threat [9-11].

## Legal and regulatory history

### International

The UN Commission on Narcotic Drugs placed diclazepam in **Schedule IV of the 1971 Convention on Psychotropic Substances** in 2021 [14,15].

### United States

DEA temporarily placed diclazepam, clonazolam, etizolam, flualprazolam, and flubromazolam in Schedule I in 2023.

A March 2, 2026 final rule made that control permanent, effective **April 1, 2026** [13].

As of October 3, 2026, diclazepam is federally **Schedule I** in the United States.

State, tribal, and international law can add separate controls.

## Special populations

Direct diclazepam-specific evidence is insufficient for:
- pregnancy;
- breastfeeding;
- older adults;
- chronic respiratory disease;
- significant liver/kidney disease.

Long persistence and active metabolites increase concern when falls, delirium, respiratory compromise, or medication accumulation would have greater consequences.

## Myths versus evidence

### “Diclazepam just turns into lorazepam, so it is basically lorazepam.”
**False.** Lorazepam is one metabolite among several; parent diclazepam and other active metabolites also matter [1].

### “A negative benzodiazepine immunoassay rules it out.”
**False.** Direct human testing showed poor detection by most evaluated immunoassays [1].

### “Long acting means safer because effects are smoother.”
**Unsupported.** Long persistence can increase accumulation, delayed impairment, and delayed withdrawal.

### “A diclazepam blood level tells you exactly how impaired someone was.”
**False.** Forensic concentration ranges overlap and individual tolerance/co-drugs vary [4,5].

### “Diclazepam is still an unscheduled research chemical in the U.S.”
**False as of October 2026.** Permanent Schedule I control took effect April 1, 2026 [13].

### “Dependence and addiction are the same.”
**False.** Physical dependence is neuroadaptation; substance use disorder is a broader behavioral syndrome [12].

## What remains unknown

Important gaps include:
- population pharmacokinetic variability beyond the original self-experiment;
- exact contribution of each active metabolite to impairment;
- prospective dependence incidence;
- molecule-specific withdrawal onset and duration;
- interaction magnitude with opioids/alcohol;
- pregnancy and breastfeeding outcomes;
- current geographic prevalence after scheduling changes;
- validated concentration-impairment models.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does diclazepam metabolize into?
Human analytical work identified delorazepam, lorazepam, and lormetazepam as active metabolites [1].

### How long does diclazepam last?
A preliminary human study estimated a parent half-life around 42 hours, but one-person data cannot define every user's timeline [1].

### Can diclazepam show up as lorazepam?
Lorazepam can be formed as a metabolite, so interpretation requires the full analyte pattern and clinical/medication context [1].

### Can diclazepam withdrawal be dangerous?
Yes, benzodiazepine-type withdrawal after physical dependence can include seizures or delirium. Abrupt cessation should not be assumed safe [12].

### Is diclazepam Schedule I in the U.S.?
Yes. Permanent federal Schedule I placement became effective April 1, 2026 [13].

## Bottom line

Diclazepam is one of the clearest examples of why an RC benzodiazepine cannot be reduced to a potency nickname.

The strongest direct human evidence shows **long parent elimination plus conversion into multiple active benzodiazepine metabolites** [1]. Large forensic datasets then show that diclazepam is not merely a laboratory curiosity: it has appeared repeatedly in impaired-driving and other real-world cases [4-6].

For harm reduction, the useful conclusions are straightforward:
- impairment can persist;
- active metabolites complicate prediction and testing;
- routine screens can miss exposure;
- opioid/alcohol combinations raise danger;
- dependence can make abrupt cessation hazardous;
- and no single blood level, equivalence chart, or withdrawal calendar is scientifically defensible.

As of October 2026, the regulatory status is also unambiguous: **diclazepam is U.S. federal Schedule I and internationally Schedule IV under the 1971 Convention** [13-15].
