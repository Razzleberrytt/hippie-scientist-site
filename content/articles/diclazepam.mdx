# Diclazepam: Complete Active-Metabolite, Toxicology, Dependence & Safety Monograph

> **Emergency safety:** Extreme sedation, inability to wake, slow or abnormal breathing, blue/gray lips, choking or gurgling while unresponsive, seizure, collapse, or severe confusion after a sedative exposure requires emergency care. Diclazepam and its active metabolites can overlap with opioids, alcohol and other sedatives for a prolonged period. Naloxone does not reverse benzodiazepines but should be used when opioid exposure is possible.

## Quick answer

**Diclazepam (2-chlorodiazepam) is a long-acting designer benzodiazepine related to diazepam.** Its most important evidence-based feature is not simply the parent drug's duration: human metabolism work shows conversion to several **active benzodiazepines**, including delorazepam, lorazepam and lormetazepam.

A single-subject human analytical study estimated a diclazepam elimination half-life around 42 hours and found that some metabolites remained detectable much longer.

That creates a layered exposure in which parent drug and metabolites can contribute to sedation, impairment, accumulation and delayed withdrawal.

## Identity and history

**Canonical name:** Diclazepam  
**Alias:** 2-chlorodiazepam / Ro 5-3448  
**Class:** 1,4-benzodiazepine / designer benzodiazepine  
**Structural relationship:** diazepam analogue with an additional chlorine substitution

Diclazepam appeared in online research-chemical markets in the early 2010s as the modern designer-benzodiazepine market expanded.

The structural relationship to diazepam helped drive online “equivalence” claims, but structural similarity is not a validated dosing conversion.

## Pharmacodynamics

Diclazepam acts within the benzodiazepine pharmacology domain, enhancing GABA-A receptor signaling through the benzodiazepine site.

Expected effects include:
- sedation;
- anxiolysis;
- psychomotor slowing;
- impaired coordination;
- amnesia;
- anticonvulsant activity.

Its active metabolites also have established benzodiazepine pharmacology, which makes the overall exposure more complex than the parent molecule alone.

## Human pharmacokinetics

The 2014 analytical study used a controlled self-experiment and serial serum/urine collection.

It estimated:
- an approximate parent half-life of **42 hours**;
- serum detection of diclazepam for roughly four days under the study method;
- much longer detectability of active metabolites.

This is valuable direct human evidence, but it is still **one-person PK**, not a population estimate.

## Active metabolites

The study identified:
- **delorazepam**;
- **lorazepam**;
- **lormetazepam**.

These metabolites matter because they can:
- prolong pharmacologic activity;
- persist after parent diclazepam falls;
- complicate laboratory interpretation;
- make withdrawal timing less predictable.

A toxicology result showing lorazepam or lormetazepam can require careful interpretation if diclazepam exposure is possible.

## Why parent-only testing can mislead

In a later forensic/metabolism study, diclazepam itself was absent in a urine specimen while metabolites were present at high concentrations.

That illustrates a general rule: **failure to detect the parent compound does not necessarily exclude diclazepam exposure**.

Metabolite-aware LC-MS/MS or high-resolution testing is more informative.

## Drug-facilitated assault evidence

Diclazepam has been reported in a drug-facilitated sexual assault investigation.

Benzodiazepine-type drugs are relevant in DFSA because they can impair:
- memory formation;
- judgment;
- coordination;
- the ability to recognize or respond to danger.

A case report cannot establish prevalence, but it confirms real-world forensic relevance.

## Acute toxicity

Expected toxicity includes:
- somnolence;
- ataxia;
- slurred speech;
- confusion;
- amnesia;
- impaired reaction time;
- reduced consciousness.

A long parent half-life plus active metabolites can extend impairment beyond the period a person expects.

## Interactions

### Opioids
Additive respiratory and CNS depression can be fatal.

### Alcohol
Alcohol can increase sedation, amnesia and psychomotor impairment.

### Other benzodiazepines/sedatives
Multiple sedatives can stack parent and metabolite effects.

There is no reliable mathematical conversion that makes these combinations predictable.

## Tolerance and physical dependence

Repeated exposure can produce tolerance and physical dependence.

Because diclazepam produces long-lived active metabolites, a person may remain pharmacologically exposed even when the parent drug is falling.

Physical dependence is distinct from a benzodiazepine use disorder.

## Withdrawal

Benzodiazepine withdrawal can include:
- rebound anxiety;
- insomnia;
- tremor;
- autonomic symptoms;
- perceptual disturbance;
- confusion;
- seizures;
- delirium.

Long-lived metabolites can delay the onset or extend the course.

The 2025 multisociety guideline recommends against abrupt discontinuation when benzodiazepine dependence is likely.

There is **no validated diclazepam self-taper protocol**, and unregulated product strength makes online conversion tables especially unreliable.

## Addiction / use-disorder education

Warning signs of benzodiazepine use disorder include:
- inability to reduce use;
- compulsive or escalating use;
- repeated blackouts;
- hazardous use;
- continued use despite work, relationship or health damage;
- craving or substantial time spent obtaining/recovering from the drug.

Tolerance or withdrawal alone does not establish a use disorder.

## Treatment and support

Acute management is supportive and focuses on airway, breathing, mental status, injury and co-exposures.

Flumazenil may be used in selected clinical circumstances but is not a universal antidote because of seizure and withdrawal risk.

Dependence should be medically assessed rather than managed with an improvised RC-to-prescription conversion.

## Drug testing

The original human study found that **only one of the tested immunoassays** reliably detected the exposure and only in some urine samples.

Definitive analysis may require:
- LC-MS/MS;
- LC-HRMS;
- explicit metabolite targets.

A negative routine benzodiazepine screen does not reliably rule out diclazepam exposure.

## Counterfeit and product identity

Research-chemical products can contain:
- the wrong benzodiazepine;
- variable concentration;
- multiple sedatives;
- opioids or other drug classes.

A product label cannot verify the pharmacokinetic assumptions behind a taper or equivalence calculation.

## Forensic interpretation

A diclazepam-related toxicology profile can include both parent drug and recognized prescription benzodiazepine metabolites.

Interpretation should consider:
- specimen timing;
- whether metabolites could have come from direct prescription-drug use;
- co-detected substances;
- clinical signs;
- tolerance;
- analytical panel design.

Detection proves exposure, not automatically sole causation.

## Epidemiology and trends

Diclazepam was part of the early modern designer-benzodiazepine wave and has appeared in poison-center, clinical and forensic datasets.

Current prevalence varies substantially by region and time. Historical case counts should not be presented as 2026 national prevalence.

## Legal and regulatory status

DEA's final rule published March 2, 2026 **permanently placed diclazepam in Schedule I of the U.S. Controlled Substances Act**, effective **April 1, 2026**.

The same final rule covered clonazolam, etizolam, flualprazolam and flubromazolam.

Legal status outside the United States differs and changes over time.

## Special populations

Direct data are sparse for:
- pregnancy and breastfeeding;
- adolescents;
- older adults;
- liver/kidney disease;
- chronic respiratory disease.

Benzodiazepine-class concerns include fetal/neonatal exposure, falls and cognitive effects in older adults, and greater respiratory risk with opioids or pulmonary disease.

## Myths versus evidence

### “Diclazepam is basically diazepam, so the conversion is obvious.”
**False.** Structural similarity and active metabolites do not create a validated milligram conversion.

### “If diclazepam itself is absent from urine, it was not taken.”
**False.** Active metabolites can outlast the parent.

### “Long-acting makes it automatically safer for tapering.”
**Unsupported.** Long action may reduce fluctuations, but an unregulated product with no validated clinical taper protocol is not equivalent to supervised pharmaceutical treatment.

### “A negative routine benzo screen rules it out.”
**False.** The original study demonstrated poor detection by most tested immunoassays.

## Major evidence gaps

- population pharmacokinetics;
- controlled impairment studies;
- prospective withdrawal course;
- monointoxication severity;
- pregnancy and organ-impairment data;
- current regional prevalence;
- product-content distributions.

## Bottom line

Diclazepam is best understood as **a long-acting benzodiazepine exposure that continues through active metabolites**.

That makes accumulation, testing and withdrawal more complicated than a parent-drug label suggests. The evidence supports real human metabolism and forensic relevance, while rejecting the internet leap from “diazepam analogue” to a precise safe conversion.
