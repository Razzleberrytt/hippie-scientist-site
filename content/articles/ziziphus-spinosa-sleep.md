> **Bottom line:** Ziziphus spinosa seed—often called **Suan Zao Ren** in traditional Chinese medicine—has an intriguing modern sleep signal, but the standalone human evidence is extremely small. The best-known placebo-controlled trial had only 12 completers.

## Why this herb is worth tracking

Ziziphus spinosa seed has a long traditional history in formulas used for restlessness and sleep. Modern supplement discussions sometimes turn that history into a much stronger statement: that jujube seed is an evidence-based insomnia treatment.

Those are different claims.

Traditional use is useful context for deciding what deserves study. Clinical efficacy has to be established in controlled human trials.

## The key randomized trial was designed as a feasibility study

The most useful standalone human trial was published in 2021.[1]

Researchers used a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled **crossover** design. After a run-in period, participants received either encapsulated Ziziphus spinosa granules at 2 g/day or placebo for four weeks, completed a four-week washout, and then crossed to the other condition.

Only **12 participants** were randomized and completed both periods.[1]

That number is important. This was primarily a feasibility trial, not a large definitive efficacy study.

## What improved?

Sleep quality measured with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index improved during the Ziziphus treatment periods compared with placebo, reaching statistical significance.[1]

Sleep diaries also suggested favorable changes in total sleep time, sleep efficiency, and sleep-onset latency during Ziziphus treatment.

But the trial did not show significant differences across most of the other measured outcomes, and the investigators reported **period effects**, which complicate interpretation of a crossover study.[1]

The herb was generally well tolerated over the short trial, with minor adverse events reported.

## Why one positive PSQI result is not enough

A 12-person study can be valuable for deciding whether a larger trial is practical. It is not large enough to give a stable estimate of average benefit or detect uncommon adverse effects.

It also cannot tell us whether the effect persists for months, whether different extracts are equivalent, or which people are most likely to respond.

Replication matters even more when an intervention comes from a complex botanical. Species identity, seed processing, extraction, dose, and combination formulas can all change what was actually tested.

## What about multi-herb formulas?

Ziziphus appears in traditional formulas and combination products, but a positive trial of a multi-ingredient product cannot establish that Ziziphus itself caused the outcome.

For an evidence database, standalone-herb evidence and formula evidence should remain separate rather than being silently pooled.

## Is it an insomnia treatment?

Not on the basis of the current evidence.

For persistent chronic insomnia, [CBT-I has a much stronger evidence base](/articles/cbt-i-vs-sleep-supplements/). A tiny botanical feasibility trial should not be placed on the same evidentiary level.

A better framing is that Ziziphus is **research-worthy and clinically unconfirmed**.

## Safety context

Short-term tolerability in 12 trial completers is reassuring only in a very narrow sense.[1] It does not establish safety during pregnancy, breastfeeding, long-term use, complex medical illness, or combination with sedating medications and supplements.

Botanical identity and product quality also matter.

## Verdict

Ziziphus spinosa is exactly the sort of herb that belongs in a research database: it has historical relevance, a plausible modern research question, and at least one controlled human signal.

It should also carry one of the site's lowest evidence grades until larger independent trials replicate the sleep result.
