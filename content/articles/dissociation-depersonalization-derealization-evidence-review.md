## Understanding the experience before assigning a cause

**Depersonalization** can feel as though one's body, emotions or thoughts are distant, mechanical or strangely unfamiliar. **Derealization** can make the room, street or people around someone feel dreamlike, flat or unreal. These descriptions do not mean the person is inventing symptoms, and they do not by themselves prove a brain disorder, trauma history, psychiatric diagnosis or loss of reality testing. [1,2]

**Dissociation** is a wider clinical and research term covering disruptions or alterations in the integration of awareness, memory, identity and experience. Researchers use differing measures of dissociation, which creates substantial risk when generalizing findings between disorders. A PTSD clinical trial measuring a dissociation score and a study of primary depersonalization-derealization disorder (DDD) are related but not interchangeable sources. [2,3]

An ordinary transient feeling under intense stress or fatigue is not equivalent to an enduring condition that disrupts daily functioning. Assessment asks about frequency, persistence, distress, insight, other symptoms, medical circumstances and possible medication or substance contributors. [5] This is a **critical narrative review of prior research**, not a new quantitative meta-analysis or a method for diagnosing a reader.

## Does feeling unreal mean losing touch with reality?

Many people with derealization can express a distinction between what they know and what they feel: "The world looks fake, but I know it's real." This can differ from firmly held beliefs that conflict with consensual reality. Yet this useful distinction is not a diagnostic shortcut, and an individual's insight may be difficult to evaluate from a short message. [2,5]

If a person reports detachment along with severe confusion, unusual neurological symptoms, intoxication concerns or impaired judgment, the broader picture matters more than selecting a single reassuring label. A mental-health explanation should not prematurely replace medical assessment of new or alarming symptoms.

It is equally important not to stigmatize people. Feeling detached does not prove someone is dangerous, manipulative, emotionally incapable or incapable of caring. Observed distress and functional limitations deserve attention without assumptions about character.

## What the prevalence evidence actually measures

Yang and colleagues systematically reviewed **23 reports** using standardized diagnostic methods. In general-population samples, prevalence of DDD ranged from **0% to 1.9%**. They described approximately **1%** as a reasonable overall general-population characterization, while stressing variation by setting and methodology. [1]

The reported ranges were much broader in treatment populations: **5–20%** among outpatients and **17.5–41.9%** among inpatients in included studies. Those numbers should not be read as the probability that any reader with anxiety or depression has DDD. Clinical settings select people with different levels and types of distress, and studies use varying measurements. [1]

The review did not estimate the lifetime frequency of every momentary depersonalization or derealization experience. Someone who has had a brief unusual sensation cannot simply match it to a prevalence figure for a diagnosed disorder.

## Trauma-related dissociation and uncertain pathways

Trauma exposure and PTSD can be associated with dissociative experiences, but the relationship is not one-to-one. Some people experience dissociation without documented trauma, while many trauma survivors do not develop a chronic dissociative disorder. Formal diagnostic frameworks separate trauma exposure, core symptoms and functional impairment. [4–6]

A 2026 review examined proposed mechanisms linking adverse and benevolent childhood experiences with PTSD or complex PTSD. It included **63 studies involving 31,540 participants**, with dissociation among several potential mediator categories. The authors judged overall evidence insufficient to determine clearly how those pathways work. Statistical mediation in observational studies is not experimental proof of a causal chain. [4]

It is therefore inappropriate to interpret one symptom as a reliable recovered memory of a particular event or proof that another person deliberately caused the detachment. Ethical care validates the present experience while avoiding unsupported assertions about an unknown history.

## What dedicated treatment studies have tested

Wang and colleagues' systematic review searched broadly and found **41 eligible studies involving 300 people** with DDD. Of these, **four were randomized trials**, one was a nonrandomized controlled trial, ten were case series and **26 were case reports**. Across the published literature, many different pharmacological, psychotherapy and neuromodulation approaches were attempted. [2]

A long list of treatments does not mean a long list of proven remedies. Case reports can generate hypotheses but cannot reliably distinguish treatment benefit from changes that might have happened anyway, expectations, concurrent interventions or selective publication of positive stories. The review characterized the quantity and quality of evidence as generally low. [2]

A credible article should not promise that a breathing technique, supplement, specific medicine or brain-stimulation method reliably eliminates derealization. Individualized care may address co-occurring depression, trauma, anxiety and other concerns, but the evidence cited here does not support a universal DDD cure.

## What controlled PTSD treatment research adds

The 2026 review by Akoral and colleagues analyzed **13 controlled studies** of psychological treatments for PTSD or CPTSD reporting dissociation outcomes. Across studies, the pooled mean change favored treatment with a **small effect**, **g = -0.28** and a reported **95% confidence interval of -0.41 to -0.15**. The authors also noted that dissociation was a secondary endpoint in most studies. [3]

This is meaningful because it shows psychological treatment can sometimes influence dissociative experiences within PTSD care. But it does **not** establish the same treatment effects in patients whose main diagnosis is DDD without PTSD, nor does it show one approach is best for all dissociative presentations. [2,3]

A small average benefit can coexist with people who improve substantially, minimally or not at all. Studies need better outcome measurement and longer follow-up. Clinical safety, individual preference and co-occurring conditions remain relevant when choosing treatment.

## Dissociation, numbness and anhedonia are not synonyms

A person describing an inability to feel joy may be experiencing **anhedonia**. Someone describing reduced intensity of most feelings may mean **emotional blunting**. A person describing being outside their body or perceiving the room as dreamlike may mean **depersonalization or derealization**. People can experience overlap, and the words used in casual conversation vary. [2,5]

Careful questions about the actual experience are more useful than asking someone to pick among dramatic labels. What precisely feels different? Is the world visually strange, the sense of self altered, emotion muted, pleasure reduced or memory disrupted? When did it begin? What activities remain possible? What associated symptoms occur?

A symptom emerging in the setting of new medication, changed dosage, substance use, sleep loss or medical illness merits individualized assessment. No static research review can sort those possibilities out for a specific reader.

## What about grounding or mindfulness?

Grounding usually means directing attention toward immediate sensory or practical details: noticing one's surroundings, placing feet on the floor, or returning to a familiar activity. Some people find that supportive, and clinicians may discuss such techniques as coping tools. But the disorder-specific treatment review does not establish that one universal grounding routine cures DDD or quantify a reliable benefit for everyone. [2]

It is also possible to worsen distress by repeatedly checking whether the world feels real. A coping practice should ideally help functioning and reduce fear rather than become a compulsory self-test. A professional familiar with dissociation can help distinguish helpful strategies from those that amplify anxiety.

Claims that all derealization arises from one neurotransmitter or that a particular supplement reverses a precise brain mechanism are not supported by this review's sources. Hypotheses should not be sold as measured clinical certainty.

## How detachment can affect relationships

Dissociation may make someone seem distracted, remote or unresponsive while they are actually frightened or struggling to feel present. An observer cannot conclude from that appearance that the person no longer cares, has a personality disorder or intentionally withholds affection. Clear descriptions of experience and limits on demanding tasks can improve understanding.

At the same time, an experience of dissociation does not automatically excuse insults, coercion or other harm. Relationship support should address observable behavior, communication and safety. A person can deserve compassionate care and still be accountable for how they treat others.

If the experience interferes with driving, hazardous work, childcare or other safety-sensitive responsibilities, attention to immediate functioning matters more than deciding which scholarly label fits best.

## When to seek an assessment

Persistent, recurring or increasingly distressing detachment deserves a clinical conversation. An assessment may consider anxiety, PTSD, depression, dissociative disorders, substances, medications and possible neurological or other medical contributors depending on the presentation. New acute symptoms may need urgent assessment. [2,5,6]

The appropriate clinician can ask about reality testing, functional impairment, time course, trauma symptoms and sleep without assuming one condition from one sensation. Treatment planning should reflect the whole person and safety, not an internet checklist.

People who have experienced trauma should not be pressured to produce a specific memory narrative. People without a known trauma history should not be told their symptoms are unreal or unimportant. Both approaches exceed what the published evidence can establish.

## Research limitations and future priorities

The strongest limitation is **heterogeneity**. The 23-paper prevalence synthesis combined different settings. The 41-study DDD treatment review pooled a body of evidence dominated by uncontrolled case reports, while the better-controlled 13-study treatment meta-analysis concerned dissociation within PTSD and CPTSD. These are different populations and questions. [1–3]

Future studies need preregistered trials with adequate sample sizes, clearer symptom subtypes, transparent safety reporting, follow-up measuring everyday functioning, and comparisons between plausible therapies. Causal mediation claims about trauma should be tested with longitudinal and intervention evidence, not treated as established because an observational path coefficient was significant. [4]

## Bottom line

**Unreality and detachment are experiences that deserve precision rather than panic.** Depersonalization is detachment from self; derealization is detachment from perceived surroundings. Neither identifies a psychiatric diagnosis, trauma history or specific treatment response by itself. [1,2,5]

There is preliminary evidence for therapeutic benefit in some circumstances, including a small average reduction of dissociation in PTSD treatment studies, but primary DDD treatments remain poorly studied. Individualized assessment, careful support and attention to safety are more defensible than a universal online cure. [2,3,6]
