## Direct answer: what does chronic emptiness feel like?

**Chronic emptiness** refers to a recurring subjective sense of inner absence or disconnection. People may describe not feeling connected to their own values, identity, emotional experience, relationships or purpose, sometimes together with numbness or an unsettling sense of "nothing." Unlike an everyday dull afternoon, it may persist, cause distress and interfere with work, connection or self-care. Yet the same phrase covers several experiences, and researchers still disagree about the ideal definition and measurement. [1–3]

A 2020 systematic review examined **99 studies** and found that chronic emptiness had been defined inconsistently, often narrowly through borderline personality disorder (BPD). Subsequent research emphasizes a broader picture. This article is a **critical narrative synthesis of existing reviews and observational studies, not a new quantitative meta-analysis**, a diagnostic instrument, or a substitute for personal assessment. [1,3,6]

## Why it is not just another word for sadness

Some people with emptiness also have depression, loneliness, grief or dissociation. Those experiences can overlap substantially without being identical. Sadness is a felt negative emotion; emptiness may be described as *having little internal emotional content at all*, feeling hollow, or not knowing what gives daily life a sense of significance. Loneliness relates to unmet social connection; emptiness may include feeling disconnected **even when other people are present**. [1–3]

The distinctions come from patients' accounts, not a blood test or a clean neurological boundary. A 2021 qualitative study interviewed **15 people with BPD**, who often described numbness, purposelessness and separation from self and others. Many distinguished emptiness from loneliness, hopelessness and depressive sadness. Fifteen interviews can describe the range of experiences vividly, but cannot establish a universal definition or frequency in all people with BPD. [2]

A 2024 study of **150 treatment-seeking patients** with varied psychiatric diagnoses identified ten recurring descriptions, including purposelessness, disconnection, numbness, self-deprecation, lack of identity, low motivation, hopelessness, loss of pleasure, physical sensations and dissociation. Themes were largely similar across BPD and major-depression groups; people with BPD more often related emptiness to dissociation. This undercuts any claim that emptiness automatically singles out one diagnosis. [3]

## Emptiness is a symptom, not a personality verdict

Chronic emptiness appears among the diagnostic features assessed when considering BPD, but one symptom never establishes a psychiatric diagnosis. Evaluations consider duration, patterns across contexts, functional impairment and alternative explanations. A feeling of disconnection is not proof that someone is manipulative, dishonest, without empathy or incapable of intimacy. Those judgments are not what emptiness research measures. [1,3,6]

The 2024 study by Hopwood and Gjorgjieva surveyed **992 people** in an online sample matched to US census characteristics. Emptiness correlated with negative emotionality, social withdrawal and lower energy across personality measures. The authors argued that emptiness is relevant beyond the BPD category. The sample was observational and self-reported, so these results do not demonstrate that traits cause emptiness or that an individual's motives can be read from a survey. [6]

Relationship distress, trauma, sleep disruption, loss, substance effects and medical concerns may also shape a person's subjective state. Even when BPD is relevant, compassionate assessment should focus on practical suffering and functioning, not treat the diagnostic word as a moral accusation.

## What the long-term course tells us

One of the most encouraging pieces of evidence comes from a study following an inpatient cohort for **24 years**. Hein and colleagues compared **290 patients with BPD** and **72 with other personality disorders**, evaluating emptiness periodically. In the BPD group, average reported time feeling empty fell from approximately **58.5% at baseline to 20.8% at year 24**. The comparison group also improved on average. [4]

The time frame is unusually informative: this symptom is **not necessarily fixed forever**. But these numbers are means from a selected historical cohort, not the probability that a specific person will recover, or a percentage that can be promised after treatment. Different participants, age groups, health systems and treatment pathways may have different trajectories. The study was not a randomized comparison of therapies and could not establish which intervention caused change. [4]

In multivariable analysis, a baseline PTSD history and the number of childhood competencies were among predictors of later emptiness severity. These associations do not mean a person's childhood permanently determines the outcome; they point toward potentially important contextual factors worthy of research. [4]

## How do scientists measure such a subjective feeling?

For years, studies often relied on a single symptom item, such as asking whether someone feels chronically empty. That can miss differences between "nothingness," detachment from identity, social disconnection or loss of purpose. It also makes treatment comparisons difficult: a drop in one item does not reveal which part of the experience improved. [1,5]

The **Psychological Emptiness Scale (PES)** was developed to address some measurement weaknesses. A 2024 psychometric study enrolled **768 respondents**, refined an initial questionnaire into a **19-item scale**, and identified two domains—**nothingness** and **detachment**—with encouraging reliability and validity results. Good questionnaire statistics are valuable, but this is not a medical test for BPD, a stand-alone suicide screening tool or evidence of an underlying brain lesion. [5]

Cross-cultural language, momentary mood, disability, trauma exposure and interpretation of the word "empty" deserve ongoing scrutiny. A person can be uncertain what the term means to them and still have real distress. Clinical conversations may be more informative when they ask for examples: "When do you feel disconnected?" or "What do you stop doing when this happens?"

## Is emptiness linked to self-harm or suicide risk?

The literature includes concerning associations, and they deserve careful interpretation. A **2023 study of 40 adults with BPD** measured emptiness and suicide/self-injury urges before and after an interpersonal stressor. Higher emptiness related to higher **baseline suicide urges**, but did not significantly predict changes in suicide or self-injury urges after the stressor. The distinction is crucial: the study did **not** demonstrate that provoking emptiness directly causes imminent suicidal behavior. [7]

A separate **ten-year longitudinal study**, involving adults with personality disorders, found chronic emptiness among several BPD criteria independently associated with subsequently observed suicide-attempt status after adjustment. Even a statistically significant association does not provide a personalized prediction. Most importantly, **feeling empty is not the same thing as having suicidal intent**, and some people who are suicidal will not report emptiness at all. [8]

This is why questions about actual thoughts of self-harm, plans, means, history, support and present safety cannot be replaced with a single symptom score. If somebody has immediate danger or an active plan, emergency services are appropriate. In the US, people can call or text **988** for crisis support. Everyday distress without an emergency still deserves timely help, rather than waiting for a crisis to become severe.

## What might make emptiness stronger or weaker?

The experience may shift alongside isolation, identity uncertainty, fear, hopelessness, depressive symptoms or lack of meaningful roles. Qualitative descriptions suggest that some people respond by distraction, intense activity, impulsive behavior, social contact or attempts to feel *anything* rather than nothing. These reports help researchers identify possible coping pathways; they do not establish a simple chemical mechanism or prove one coping strategy is effective. [1–3]

A person may find it useful to notice contexts: Is emptiness worse after conflict, extended isolation, unstructured time, overwhelming stress, or an episode of low mood? Is there any reduction during meaningful activity, routine, connection or rest? Such observations can help communicate to a healthcare professional. They are not a self-diagnosis, and taking action that could be dangerous simply to make an emotional state disappear is not a treatment.

**Avoid false certainty about dopamine or serotonin.** The studies reviewed here do not measure a universal "emptiness neurotransmitter." Neither a neurochemical infographic nor a supplement advertisement explains the condition at the level of evidence needed for treatment decisions.

## Can treatment help—and what remains unknown?

There is no single well-established, specifically tested **cure for chronic emptiness** in the studies included here. The literature suggests that improving connection with self and others, purpose, daily function and emotional clarity may be useful targets. However, qualitative recommendations are not randomized trial results. A therapy that improves broad BPD symptoms does not automatically have a proven, separately measured effect on emptiness. [1,2,4]

A comprehensive assessment can consider conditions contributing to distress and established psychotherapy options appropriate for those conditions. For example, borderline personality disorder can be treated using structured psychotherapies, while depression may call for different or overlapping approaches. Which option is suitable depends on the person's symptoms, severity, history, preferences, available support and safety needs. It would be misleading to promise a specific workbook, medication or supplement can reliably remove emptiness. [1–4]

When symptoms affect safety or functioning, working with qualified professionals is more defensible than guessing a diagnosis from online definitions. In less urgent situations, gradual attention to meaningful routines, supportive relationships, values and emotion-labeling may offer useful discussion points, but these should be described as practical possibilities rather than proven standalone interventions.

## Distinguishing emptiness from alexithymia, anhedonia and dissociation

**Alexithymia** concerns difficulty recognizing or describing emotions; **emptiness** concerns a subjective experience of inner absence or disconnection. One person may have both without either being reducible to the other. See the [emotional-awareness evidence review](/articles/alexithymia-emotional-awareness-evidence-review/) for the measurement and psychotherapy literature.

**Anhedonia** usually concerns reduced interest or pleasure. A person might have anhedonia without describing a broad loss of identity or connection. Conversely, a person may report emptiness without meeting criteria for a depressive episode. These distinctions are useful questions to explore but not easy self-diagnostic tests. [1,3]

**Dissociation** concerns alterations in the integration or experience of awareness, identity, memory or surroundings. In the 2024 transdiagnostic study, dissociation co-occurred more often with emptiness among the BPD participants than the depressed participants. That result was drawn from a selected sample and does not mean dissociation is unique to BPD. [3]

## Research strengths, limitations and the next questions

The **99-study systematic review** provides a broad map but inherits inconsistent definitions and often observational designs. The **15-person interviews** give depth of description but not general-population estimates. The **150-person transdiagnostic sample** offers comparative themes without proving categorical boundaries. The **24-year cohort** offers rare temporal context but cannot separate aging, treatment, social changes and attrition in the way a controlled experiment might. [1–4]

Measurement development is progressing, yet scales measure reported experience rather than an objective molecule or brain circuit. The suicide-risk associations argue for thoughtful clinical assessment while explicitly resisting deterministic predictions or stigmatizing people who describe emptiness. [5,7,8]

The next generation of research should examine diverse populations over time, distinguish changes in meaning and connection from general symptom improvement, measure helpful and harmful treatment effects, and include people with lived experience when deciding which outcomes matter. Studies should avoid using a diagnosis as a proxy for character.

## Bottom line

Chronic emptiness is a real and often painful feeling of disconnection or inner absence. It is **not simply sadness, not always loneliness, and not proof of BPD**. The best available evidence shows that people describe several components, that emptiness exists across diagnostic groups, and that average severity can decline substantially across decades. Specific treatment mechanisms and individualized prediction remain unsettled. [1–6]

The most humane and scientifically defensible approach is to take suffering seriously while avoiding an identity-defining label. A person who feels empty can still have meaningful relationships, personal strengths and room for change.
