# Phenazolam (Clobromazolam): Emerging Designer Benzo Masterclass

> **Emergency safety:** Call emergency services for unresponsiveness, very slow or abnormal breathing, blue/gray lips, repeated vomiting with profound sedation, collapse, or inability to stay awake. Risk is especially high when a benzodiazepine-type drug is mixed with opioids, alcohol, xylazine, nitazenes, or other sedatives. If opioid exposure is possible, give naloxone if available; naloxone reverses opioid effects, not phenazolam itself.

## Quick answer

**Phenazolam and clobromazolam are names used for the same emerging designer benzodiazepine.** Current U.S. federal and international-review documents use the combined form **clobromazolam (phenazolam)**.

The strongest human evidence is not a controlled dosing trial. It is **analytically confirmed emergency toxicology and forensic surveillance**. In a 2024 Australian study, clobromazolam was detected in **100 of 993 emergency-department intoxication presentations** and was the most frequently identified designer benzodiazepine in that cohort.

The same study also shows why subjective drug identification is unreliable: no patient reported intentionally taking clobromazolam, while many reported alprazolam exposure even though alprazolam was analytically confirmed far less often. Polysubstance exposure was the rule, not the exception.

The biggest misconception is that a new RC benzodiazepine can be ranked precisely against alprazolam, bromazolam, or clonazolam from online reports. Controlled human pharmacokinetic, dose-response, and impairment data remain too limited for that.

## History and market emergence

U.S. federal review material states that clobromazolam was first synthesized in the **1980s** and later emerged as a novel psychoactive substance distributed through online markets.

Its modern relevance comes from three converging signals:

1. **Australian clinical toxicology** documented frequent analytically confirmed exposure in 2022–2023.
2. **CFSRE/NMS Labs** reported increasing U.S. phenazolam detections during 2025 in both blood specimens and drug materials.
3. **WHO selected clobromazolam (phenazolam) for critical review** at the 49th ECDD meeting in October 2026.

This is the pattern seen repeatedly with designer benzodiazepines: an older chemical enters a modern illicit market, spreads through powders or pressed tablets, and is recognized clinically only after laboratory surveillance catches up.

## Chemistry and relationship to other benzodiazepines

Phenazolam/clobromazolam is a **triazolobenzodiazepine** structurally related to bromazolam and other designer benzodiazepines.

Structural similarity can support a mechanistic hypothesis, but it does **not** establish:

- equal potency;
- equal half-life;
- equal dose-response;
- equal withdrawal timing;
- equal overdose risk.

Federal review material notes evidence suggesting relatively high GABA-A binding affinity, but also emphasizes the lack of controlled human pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic data. Binding affinity should therefore not be turned into a consumer potency-conversion chart.

## Pharmacodynamics

Phenazolam acts as a **positive allosteric modulator of the GABA-A receptor**.

This mechanism is consistent with benzodiazepine-type effects such as:

- sedation;
- anxiolysis;
- impaired coordination;
- muscle relaxation;
- amnesia;
- reduced level of consciousness.

It also provides a biologically plausible basis for **tolerance, physical dependence, and withdrawal** with repeated exposure.

Mechanism alone cannot establish the exact intensity or duration of effects in humans.

## Pharmacokinetics and metabolism

Controlled human pharmacokinetic data are not established well enough to define a reliable half-life, onset, duration, or elimination window for direct phenazolam use.

That uncertainty matters because internet reports often present highly specific timing claims without validated human data.

Clinical and forensic laboratories can identify clobromazolam/phenazolam using high-resolution mass spectrometry, but a toxicology detection does not automatically reveal:

- when the drug was taken;
- how much was taken;
- how impaired the person was at an earlier time;
- whether the detected drug alone caused the clinical presentation.

## Human effects: what is actually documented

### Australian emergency-department evidence

The 2024 Journal of Analytical Toxicology study examined emergency presentations in Victoria, Australia, from April 2022 through March 2023.

Clobromazolam was detected in **100 of 993 cases (10%)** and was the most frequently detected designer benzodiazepine in that cohort.

Critically:

- **98%** of clobromazolam-positive cases involved polydrug exposure;
- phenazepam was frequently co-detected;
- methamphetamine was frequently co-detected;
- other benzodiazepines were common;
- no patient reported intentional clobromazolam administration.

Those findings establish exposure and misidentification risk, but they do not isolate a clean “phenazolam-only” toxidrome.

### U.S. forensic evidence

CFSRE reported growing phenazolam detections during 2025 in toxicology specimens and drug materials. That surveillance supports a genuine U.S. market signal rather than an internet-only trend.

## Acute toxicity and overdose

Expected benzodiazepine-type toxicity includes:

- profound drowsiness;
- slurred speech;
- poor coordination;
- confusion;
- amnesia;
- disinhibition;
- loss of consciousness.

The most dangerous scenarios are mixed-depressant exposures.

The August 2026 FDA notice specifically described increased overdose risk when clobromazolam is combined with:

- opioids;
- alcohol;
- xylazine;
- nitazenes;
- other CNS depressants.

In such combinations, sedation and respiratory depression can amplify one another.

## Interactions and polysubstance risk

### Opioids and nitazenes

This is the highest-priority interaction. Counterfeit or mixed drug supplies can contain both a benzodiazepine-type compound and a potent opioid. Benzodiazepines do not respond to naloxone, but any opioid component may.

### Alcohol

Alcohol can worsen sedation, amnesia, impaired coordination, vomiting/aspiration risk, and respiratory compromise.

### Xylazine and other sedatives

Xylazine, pregabalin/gabapentin, Z-drugs, sedating antihistamines, additional benzodiazepines, and other depressants can deepen CNS depression.

Because phenazolam is often encountered in **polysubstance** contexts, interaction risk is not a theoretical side note; it is central to the evidence base.

## Tolerance

Direct long-term phenazolam studies are lacking.

Benzodiazepine-class pharmacology strongly supports the possibility of tolerance with repeated exposure. Tolerance can also be uneven: reduced subjective sedation does not necessarily mean protection from amnesia, falls, impaired driving, or dangerous drug combinations.

## Physical dependence

Repeated benzodiazepine exposure can produce physical dependence.

Federal review material specifically identifies dependence and addiction potential as concerns for clobromazolam/phenazolam.

Physical dependence means the body has adapted to the drug. It is not the same as compulsive use or benzodiazepine use disorder.

## Withdrawal

Abrupt discontinuation after dependence can produce benzodiazepine withdrawal, including:

- severe anxiety;
- insomnia;
- tremor;
- autonomic symptoms;
- perceptual disturbance;
- confusion;
- seizures;
- delirium.

The FDA's 2026 international-review notice specifically warns that abrupt discontinuation after regular clobromazolam use may precipitate **life-threatening withdrawal including seizures and delirium**.

What is **not** established is a reliable phenazolam-specific day-by-day withdrawal timeline or conversion to another benzodiazepine.

A 2025 multisociety clinical guideline recommends against abrupt benzodiazepine discontinuation in physically dependent patients and supports individualized clinician-guided tapering. That is class-level guidance, not a validated phenazolam conversion protocol.

## Dependence is not the same as benzodiazepine use disorder

Possible signs of a use disorder include:

- repeated inability to cut down;
- craving or loss of control;
- escalating use;
- continued use despite health, work, legal, or relationship harms;
- substantial time spent obtaining, using, or recovering;
- hazardous use despite known risks.

A person can also have physical dependence without meeting criteria for a use disorder.

## Treatment and support

There is no phenazolam-specific evidence-based detox medication or conversion schedule.

People using the drug regularly, especially those with previous withdrawal seizures, heavy alcohol use, multiple sedatives, or uncertain product strength, may need medical assessment before stopping.

Emergency care is appropriate for:

- seizure;
- delirium;
- severe confusion;
- collapse;
- unresponsiveness;
- abnormal breathing.

For U.S. readers, **FindTreatment.gov** and the **SAMHSA National Helpline (1-800-662-HELP)** can connect people with substance-use treatment and withdrawal-management services.

## Drug checking, toxicology, and detection

The Australian study is a strong example of why **high-resolution mass spectrometry** matters for NPS.

Routine benzodiazepine immunoassays are not designed to comprehensively identify every designer analogue. A negative routine screen therefore cannot reliably exclude phenazolam exposure.

Definitive methods can include:

- LC-HRMS;
- LC-MS/MS;
- updated spectral libraries;
- validated analytical standards.

Analytical naming also matters: laboratories and regulators increasingly use **clobromazolam (phenazolam)** to reduce confusion between online and forensic terminology.

## Counterfeit and mislabeled product risk

Counterfeit identity is one of the strongest signals in the evidence.

The Australian cohort found a striking mismatch between reported and measured exposure: many people thought they had taken alprazolam, while clobromazolam was detected much more frequently.

The August 2026 FDA notice also states that clobromazolam has been encountered in:

- powders;
- capsules;
- counterfeit pressed tablets;
- products misrepresented as alprazolam or other licensed benzodiazepines.

A pill's color, imprint, branding, or seller description cannot establish chemical identity.

## Forensic interpretation

A phenazolam-positive toxicology result proves exposure, not sole causation.

Interpretation should account for:

- other benzodiazepines;
- fentanyl or other opioids;
- nitazenes;
- stimulants;
- xylazine;
- alcohol;
- specimen type;
- concentration;
- tolerance;
- timing.

The 98% polysubstance rate in the Australian cohort is a reminder that isolated-drug conclusions may be especially misleading for this compound.

## Epidemiology and current trend

Current evidence supports a **growing but geographically uneven** NPS signal.

- Victoria, Australia: 100/993 ED intoxication cases in the 2022–2023 cohort.
- United States: CFSRE reported increasing phenazolam positivity during 2025.
- International: WHO selected the compound for critical review in 2026.

These datasets do not establish general-population prevalence.

## Legal and regulatory status — dated October 3, 2026

### United States

The FDA's August 2026 Federal Register notice states that clobromazolam/phenazolam has **no approved commercial or medical use in the United States and was not then controlled under the Controlled Substances Act**.

That is a dated federal statement. State law can differ, and federal status can change.

### International

The same notice states that clobromazolam/phenazolam was **not then under international control** and had been selected for **critical review** at the 49th WHO Expert Committee on Drug Dependence on October 19–22, 2026.

Because that meeting was still in the future when this page was updated, no scheduling recommendation is assumed here.

## Special populations

Direct evidence in pregnancy, breastfeeding, adolescents, older adults, liver disease, kidney disease, or chronic respiratory disease is inadequate.

Risk should therefore be interpreted conservatively from benzodiazepine pharmacology:

- older adults may have increased fall and confusion risk;
- chronic respiratory disease may increase the consequences of sedative combinations;
- pregnancy and breastfeeding safety are not established;
- liver disease could alter exposure, but there is no validated phenazolam dosing guidance.

## Myths and common misconceptions

### “Clobromazolam and phenazolam are different drugs.”

Current forensic and regulatory sources use the names for the same substance.

### “If a pill says Xanax, it contains alprazolam.”

Not reliably. The Australian emergency-toxicology study documented substantial mismatch between what people reported taking and what laboratory analysis found.

### “Receptor affinity tells you the human dose.”

No. Binding data do not establish human dose-response, half-life, or a safe exposure.

### “A benzo-only product cannot cause breathing problems.”

Mixed drug supplies are common. Opioids, nitazenes, xylazine, alcohol, or other sedatives can substantially increase respiratory risk.

### “A negative routine benzo screen rules it out.”

No. Designer benzodiazepines may require targeted or high-resolution mass-spectrometry testing.

| **Established** | Polysubstance exposure and counterfeit/misidentified products are major real-world features. |

## FAQ

### Is phenazolam the same as clobromazolam?

Yes. Current forensic and regulatory literature treats clobromazolam and phenazolam as names for the same compound.

### Is it replacing bromazolam?

Surveillance shows phenazolam is rising as bromazolam faces tighter control, but that does not mean every product sold as bromazolam contains phenazolam.

### Has it caused emergency-department presentations?

Yes. It was detected in 100 of 993 intoxication presentations in one Australian emergency-toxicology cohort, although 98% of those cases involved other drugs too.

### Can it cause withdrawal seizures?

Benzodiazepine withdrawal can cause seizures, and the 2026 FDA review specifically identifies seizure and delirium risk after abrupt discontinuation following regular clobromazolam use.

### Is phenazolam federally scheduled in the U.S.?

The August 2026 FDA Federal Register notice stated that it was **not then specifically controlled under the CSA**. Laws change, and state-level rules may differ.

## Bottom line

Phenazolam/clobromazolam has moved from a niche RC name into **real emergency toxicology, U.S. forensic surveillance, counterfeit-tablet investigations, and international scheduling review**.

The evidence is strong enough to say the exposure is real and potentially dangerous—especially in mixed depressant supplies—but still too weak to justify precise human potency, duration, or dose-equivalence claims.
