> **Bottom line:** A person can know that a relationship is causing harm and still feel intensely attached. That contradiction is not proof of weakness, an addiction-like brain disorder or a secret diagnosis. Longing, shared history, children, social pressure, uncertainty, practical dependence and sometimes coercion may all matter. Scientific research supports several **associated mechanisms** and **real-world barriers**, but it does not prove a single explanation for everyone. This is a source-graded synthesis of published findings, **not a newly performed meta-analysis**. [1–7]

## Review question and method

**Question:** What psychological and practical factors help explain why emotional attachment or relationship persistence can coexist with rejection, distress, threats or repeated conflict?

We separate three very different research literatures: observational **attachment and wellbeing** studies; **romantic partnership** studies of reassurance, autonomy and support; and **survivor research** on domestic violence, coercive control and barriers to safe separation. Combining these literatures requires care. An attachment study among ordinary couples is not a study of coercive control, and a survey asking why women stay does not directly measure dopamine, conditioning or an underlying psychiatric diagnosis. [1–6]

Our approach is a targeted evidence review, not a PRISMA systematic review or new quantitative meta-analysis. We have not conducted duplicate study selection, full risk-of-bias extraction, new numerical pooling or original causal estimation. We identify design and population with each important result, and we do not treat personal accounts as diagnostic proof about anyone absent from an assessment.

## Emotional attachment and relationship safety are different things

Attachment describes the way people seek closeness, safety and reassurance in important relationships, particularly under threat. It is possible to feel great love, comfort or familiarity with a partner while also experiencing serious instability or harmful behavior. Feelings give information about one's inner world; they do not independently answer whether the relationship is safe, mutually respectful or sustainable. [1,4,5]

The phrase **“if they loved me, I would feel secure”** captures an understandable wish, but even mutual affection cannot substitute for dependable behavior and boundaries. Conversely, the fact that someone becomes anxious when contact declines does not establish that the other person intended to inflict distress. Fear can reflect a genuine unstable environment, the anxious person's expectations, or both. Context is essential. [1,4,5]

Clinical and public discussions too often ask the wrong binary question: *is this real love or trauma bonding?* Relationships do not come with an objective one-question assay. A useful inquiry examines concrete behaviors, support, consent, practical needs and how the experience affects sleep, work, mental health and connections outside the relationship.

## What the largest attachment evidence review can and cannot say

Zhang and colleagues conducted a meta-analysis of **224 studies**, representing **245 samples and 79,722 people**, on adult attachment and mental health. More insecure attachment was associated with worse mental-health indicators on average. Attachment anxiety correlated with negative mental health at approximately **r = .42** and avoidance at **r = .28**; both were also inversely associated with positive indicators. [1]

These figures describe **between-person associations across research samples**, not the likelihood that someone will return to an emotionally painful relationship or the probability that a partner will change. Correlation does not establish that attachment insecurity caused psychological symptoms; negative life circumstances, earlier experiences and mental illness may affect both. [1]

The larger average association for attachment anxiety does not mean an anxiously attached person is irrational or that a supposedly avoidant partner is responsible for all distress. Measures of attachment are dimensional—not clinical personality-disorder diagnoses or permanent identities. No one should use an attachment label to excuse deception, control or violence. [1,4]

## What support and relationship autonomy add

A systematic review of **43 dyadic studies** by McLeod and colleagues found that anxious and avoidant attachment patterns were associated with differences in how romantic partners provide, seek and interpret support. An insecurely attached partner may perceive support as insufficient or unreliable even when another person thinks they are being helpful. These findings describe trends, not inevitable scripts. [4]

Simpson and Overall reviewed partner buffering: behaviors such as reassurance and responsive support may soften the relational impact of attachment insecurity in some contexts. More recent dyadic longitudinal work by Genesse and colleagues links a sense of autonomy within the relationship with higher satisfaction across days and months. But these outcomes were studied in couple relationships, not in controlled trials of whether reassurance can make an abusive relationship safe. [5,6]

The distinction matters. Mutual reassurance, repair and reliable communication can be constructive when both people respect one another. In a relationship involving threats, intimidation or coercive control, asking one person to become a better source of comfort may create additional risk. **Support should not become a demand that someone accommodate abuse.** [2,5,6]

## Why leaving is not simply a choice to stop loving

A 2024 integrative review by Stulz and colleagues identified **12 relevant studies** exploring women's experiences of escaping domestic violence and finding safe housing. Themes included emotional and material losses, maintaining contact with an abusive ex-partner, financial insecurity, service availability, the importance of respectful providers and concern for children. Seven included studies came from the United States, with others from Australia, Canada, the Netherlands and Italy. [2]

These findings are not a randomized experiment and should not be turned into a percentage claim about all survivors. But they reveal why the popular question *“Why don't they just leave?”* misses the actual problem. Someone may need stable housing, childcare, transportation, financial security or assistance navigating custody and legal systems. Separation itself may increase danger in coercive situations. A support plan needs to consider timing, safety and resources rather than pressure. [2]

A separate **2026 online study of 926 Israeli women** compared reported reasons for staying across recently abused, formerly abused and never-abused groups. Researchers identified positive reasons as well as negative barriers described as fear of failure, fear of terror and captivity. Recently abused participants reported especially high negative barriers; the study complicated the idea that staying is either a simple preference or total lack of agency. [3]

It is important not to claim these self-reported findings represent every gender, country, culture, relationship type or degree of violence. Survey wording, recruitment and recall all matter. The primary lesson is modest but consequential: emotional and practical explanations coexist, and outsiders rarely know them all. [2,3]

## Trauma bonding, intermittent reinforcement and dopamine claims

The term **trauma bond** is used to describe attachment that may develop in an abusive or coercive relationship. It is *not* a distinct DSM diagnosis or a test that can be established from screenshots. Intermittent reinforcement is a real concept in learning science: outcomes delivered unpredictably can influence repeated behavior in controlled settings. But extrapolating that directly to a universal explanation for romantic persistence requires human evidence that isolates the mechanism and its effects. [3,7]

The published attachment and relationship studies above do not randomize people to receive coercion, cruelty or alternating affection; ethical constraints make such experiments unacceptable. Thus claims that a specific partner has created a neurochemical “drug addiction” through inconsistent texts go beyond what the evidence demonstrates. Other explanations, including ordinary longing, attachment insecurity, fear, social expectations and material entanglement, can coexist. [1–5,7]

A person's repeated checking of messages may briefly provide reassurance while keeping attention centered on uncertainty. That is a plausible behavioral maintenance hypothesis, but it does not establish another person's intent, the presence of abuse or the only route to recovery. Distinguish **observable actions** from untestable stories about motives.

## The risk of confusing limerence with coercion

Limerence research concerns persistent romantic preoccupation and intense desire for reciprocation. Its measurement literature is emerging; not every case involves an abusive relationship. Similarly, not every unhealthy relationship involves limerence. A person can experience obsessive longing in a situation where the other person has clearly declined contact, and another can remain with an abusive partner because access to children, money and safe housing is constrained even without obsessive romantic thoughts. [2,3,7]

These are different questions with different ethical responsibilities. Psychological insight does not license surveillance, pressure to respond or attempts to make someone return affection. It also must never shift responsibility for coercion onto the person harmed. Any safety-focused plan should prioritize immediate risk, autonomy, consent and access to appropriate support. [2,7]

## What about childhood experience and attachment insecurity?

Attachment patterns are related to experiences with caregivers and later close relationships, but they do not provide a deterministic account of any individual's childhood. The adult meta-analysis is correlational. It cannot tell us that someone's current attachment is *definitely* a replay of a childhood wound, much less identify a parent's behavior from a brief symptom checklist. [1]

People can develop different patterns across relationships and life stages. A dependable partner, improved social stability, therapy, friendships and other relationships may offer opportunities for new experiences and greater autonomy. None is a guarantee, and support cannot be conditional on immediately changing one's emotional reactions. [4–6]

## A careful framework for understanding a painful relationship

The research is more useful when turned into **questions**, not labels. What behavior is actually occurring? Are there threats, coercion, stalking, restrictions on money or freedom, or physical danger? Are there explicit agreements and repeated repair? What does contact do to daily functioning and to other important relationships? What housing, childcare, transportation, legal or financial barriers exist? What support is available without violating privacy or consent? [2–6]

In lower-risk relationships, practical steps may include setting clear communication expectations, noticing repeated patterns over weeks rather than over-reading one message, restoring independent routines and considering counseling. When violence or control is present, safety planning with a domestic violence service is more appropriate than joint “attachment repair.” A partner's psychiatric diagnosis is not required for either response. [2,4,5]

## Limits and fairness to the evidence

* The 224-study attachment synthesis includes many forms of relationships and mental-health outcomes, not a randomized experiment about leaving a harmful partner. [1]
* The twelve-study housing review concerns survivors seeking housing, not every relationship that feels disappointing or inconsistent. [2]
* The 926-person survey came from a specific geographic and demographic sample and is self-reported. [3]
* Dyadic couple studies are vulnerable to common-method bias and cannot prove that partner reassurance can cure coercive control. [4–6]
* A scoping review of limerence maps possible constructs; it is not a clinical diagnostic manual or treatment meta-analysis. [7]
* “Trauma bond,” “anxious–avoidant trap” and “dopamine addiction” may be evocative descriptions, but none is a substitute for a detailed behavioral and safety assessment.

## Evidence-based conclusion

Feeling attached in a painful relationship can involve **real affection and longing, psychological insecurity, practical constraints and, in some cases, coercion**. Evidence from attachment studies documents associations with distress; survivor research documents barriers outsiders often underestimate. There is no scientific basis for treating every difficult relationship as one neurological mechanism or diagnosing either partner from internet shorthand. [1–7]

A useful next step is not proving whether someone is a “narcissist” or “avoidant.” It is clarifying needs, boundaries, real-world choices and safety. When violence or control is present, specialist domestic-violence support can help with confidential safety planning. In the United States, the National Domestic Violence Hotline can be reached at **800-799-SAFE (7233)** or **thehotline.org**; in an immediate emergency, call local emergency services.
