> **Bottom line:** Ergothioneine is biologically interesting and clearly absorbed by humans, but direct clinical evidence is still early. A one-year randomized pilot in only 19 older adults with mild cognitive impairment reported favorable learning and neurofilament-light findings, while larger observational studies associate lower circulating ergothioneine with faster cognitive decline. Those signals justify more research; they do **not** establish that ergothioneine prevents dementia, treats mild cognitive impairment, or acts as a proven “longevity vitamin.” [2-4]

## What ergothioneine is

Ergothioneine is a diet-derived sulfur-containing amino-acid derivative found in foods and especially discussed in mushroom research. Humans have a dedicated transporter that can concentrate ergothioneine in tissues, which is one reason researchers have become interested in its possible physiological role.

The compound is frequently described as an antioxidant or cytoprotective molecule. Those labels describe chemistry and mechanistic research, not a proven clinical outcome.

For an evidence-first interpretation, three separate questions matter:

1. **Can humans absorb and retain ergothioneine?** Yes.
2. **Do blood levels correlate with health outcomes?** Several studies suggest associations.
3. **Does supplementation prevent or treat disease?** That remains unproven.

Keeping those layers separate prevents an observational biomarker from being turned into a treatment claim.

## Human absorption is well established

A 2017 study administered pure ergothioneine to healthy human volunteers and tracked its uptake, metabolism, and several oxidative or inflammatory biomarkers. [1]

Blood ergothioneine concentrations increased substantially, urinary loss was relatively low, and the compound appeared to be retained by the body. This provides direct human evidence that orally consumed ergothioneine is bioavailable.

The study also reported downward trends in several oxidative-damage or inflammation markers. However, **most of those biomarker changes were not statistically significant**.

That null detail matters. The study supports pharmacokinetics and biological plausibility much more strongly than it supports a claim that ergothioneine produces a clinically meaningful antioxidant benefit in healthy people.

## The mild-cognitive-impairment trial is promising—and tiny

A 2024 double-blind randomized placebo-controlled pilot enrolled 19 adults aged 60 or older with mild cognitive impairment. Participants received ergothioneine or placebo for one year. [2]

The ergothioneine group showed a favorable signal on the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test and stabilization of plasma neurofilament light chain, while the placebo group did not show the same pattern. Routine blood, kidney, and liver safety markers did not reveal toxicity over the study period.

Those findings are worth following. But a **19-person pilot study cannot establish a treatment effect for mild cognitive impairment or dementia prevention**.

With a sample that small:

- chance findings are more plausible;
- subgroup or baseline imbalance can matter more;
- rare adverse effects cannot be characterized;
- one cognitive test cannot establish broad cognitive preservation;
- biomarker stabilization is not the same as preventing dementia.

The correct reading is “promising pilot evidence,” not “ergothioneine prevents cognitive decline.”

## Observational data strengthen the hypothesis, not the treatment claim

A longitudinal study followed 470 older adults attending memory clinics and measured plasma ergothioneine. Lower baseline ergothioneine was associated with poorer cognitive performance and faster decline across several cognitive and functional domains. [4]

This is a meaningful prospective association, but observational studies cannot tell us whether low ergothioneine **causes** decline.

Lower ergothioneine could reflect diet, health status, metabolism, inflammation, socioeconomic factors, other nutritional patterns, or consequences of disease. Supplementing a biomarker that correlates with an outcome does not necessarily reverse the underlying process.

This distinction is common in nutrition science: a useful biomarker can be biologically informative without becoming a validated therapeutic target.

## What the 2025 systematic review concluded

A 2025 systematic review examined 19 studies spanning human, animal, and mechanistic evidence on ergothioneine, cognition, and age-related neurodegenerative disease. [3]

The review described plausible antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antisenescence, neurotransmission, and neuroprotective mechanisms and concluded that ergothioneine is a promising area for further research.

The evidence mix is important. When preclinical, observational, and small intervention studies are combined in a narrative review, the total literature can look larger than the **direct randomized human efficacy evidence** actually is.

For clinical questions, the randomized human layer deserves the most weight—and that layer remains sparse.

## A 2026 safety question: does ergothioneine raise TMAO?

One theoretical concern has been whether microbial degradation of ergothioneine could contribute to trimethylamine and ultimately trimethylamine N-oxide, or TMAO.

A 2026 study combined bioinformatic work with a randomized placebo-controlled human experiment. In that short trial, ergothioneine increased plasma ergothioneine but did **not** increase plasma TMAO. [5]

That is reassuring for this specific short-term question. It should not be generalized into a blanket statement that every possible long-term safety issue is settled.

## Does ergothioneine improve cognition?

The best current answer is **possibly, but not established**.

Evidence supporting the hypothesis includes:

- a small one-year randomized pilot with favorable learning and biomarker signals;
- prospective observational associations between lower ergothioneine and cognitive decline;
- mechanistic and preclinical evidence consistent with neuroprotection.

Evidence limiting confidence includes:

- very small randomized samples;
- few independent intervention trials;
- lack of replicated disease-progression outcomes;
- heavy reliance on biomarkers and observational associations;
- uncertainty about which populations, if any, would benefit most.

Until larger independent trials reproduce a meaningful cognitive effect, statements such as “ergothioneine prevents dementia” or “slows brain aging” go beyond the evidence.

## Is mushroom intake equivalent to isolated ergothioneine?

No.

Mushrooms contain multiple nutrients and bioactive compounds. A mushroom intervention cannot isolate ergothioneine unless the study design specifically does so, and a trial of pure ergothioneine cannot be treated as evidence that every mushroom product delivers the same exposure.

Product identity is especially important when comparing:

- whole foods;
- mushroom powders;
- extracts;
- ergothioneine-standardized products;
- isolated ergothioneine.

The evidence should follow the preparation that was actually tested.

## Safety: what is known

Published human studies have not produced a prominent short-term safety signal at the studied exposures. The one-year MCI pilot also reported no concerning changes in routine hematologic, kidney, or liver markers. [2]

But the total exposure database remains small. Existing evidence cannot confidently define:

- rare adverse effects;
- pregnancy or lactation safety;
- long-term effects across many years;
- safety in severe kidney or liver disease;
- every medication interaction;
- safety of poorly characterized multi-ingredient products.

Absence of a signal in a small trial is not proof that a rare risk does not exist.

## Evidence verdict

| Question | Current evidence |
|---|---|
| Is ergothioneine absorbed orally? | **Yes.** Human pharmacokinetic evidence is direct. |
| Are blood levels associated with cognitive outcomes? | **Yes, observationally.** Causality is not established. |
| Does supplementation improve cognition? | **Preliminary.** One very small randomized pilot is encouraging. |
| Does it prevent dementia? | **Not established.** |
| Does it slow biological aging generally? | **Not established.** |
| Does short-term supplementation raise TMAO? | A 2026 randomized study found **no increase** over the studied period. |
| Is long-term safety fully characterized? | **No.** |
