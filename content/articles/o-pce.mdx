# O-PCE (N-Ethyldeschloroketamine): Dissociative Toxicity, Deaths & 2026 Addictovigilance Data

## Quick answer

**O-PCE is an arylcyclohexylamine dissociative related to ketamine and PCE.**

A 2026 French surveillance study identified 56 ketamine-analogue cases reported from 2017–2024. O-PCE was involved in **38%** of the cases, second only to 2F-DCK.

Across the analogue series, 61% of cases were serious and deaths represented 11%.

Those numbers describe the whole ketamine-analogue case series, not O-PCE alone.

## What symptoms were seen?

Neurologic and psychiatric effects dominated the series, especially:

- dissociation;
- impaired consciousness;
- confusion;
- distressing behavioral effects.

The study also identified different user profiles, including people who were **unintentionally exposed** when ketamine analogues were sold as other drugs.

## Why “longer ketamine” is an unsafe simplification

O-PCE shares a broad NMDA-antagonist family with ketamine.

That does not establish equal:

- potency;
- duration;
- metabolism;
- cardiovascular effects;
- behavioral toxicity;
- safe exposure.

A familiar mechanism is not the same thing as a clinically interchangeable drug.

## Product substitution matters

French cases included analogues sold or substituted as synthetic cathinones, cocaine, or ketamine.

That means the user may begin with the wrong assumptions about onset, duration, and expected effects.


## Why O-PCE should not be reduced to “long ketamine”

O-PCE belongs to the arylcyclohexylamine dissociative family, but describing it as merely a longer or stronger version of ketamine can hide meaningful uncertainty.

Changes in chemical structure can alter NMDA-receptor activity, secondary receptor effects, metabolism, duration, and behavioral toxicity. Human evidence for O-PCE is much thinner than for ketamine, so those differences are not mapped well enough to create dependable equivalence claims.

## Behavioral toxicity can dominate the emergency

Dissociative intoxication can produce severe confusion, disorientation, agitation, bizarre behavior, impaired pain perception, and inability to recognize danger.

That creates risks from falls, traffic, exposure, self-injury, or confrontation even when the person is not experiencing classic respiratory depression.

A prolonged dissociative state can also become difficult for friends or bystanders to manage safely.

## Product identity is an additional uncertainty

Powders marketed as O-PCE can be difficult to distinguish visually from DCK, 2F-DCK, DMXE, other PCE/PCP analogues, or unrelated white powders.

A subjective “O-PCE-like” experience is not proof of identity. Analytical confirmation is needed when the exact compound matters.

## Co-exposures change the clinical picture

Opioids, benzodiazepines, alcohol, stimulants, and other dissociatives can all change risk in different directions. Profound sedation or slow breathing should raise concern for an opioid or depressant component rather than being dismissed as ordinary dissociation.

Seizure, collapse, prolonged unresponsiveness, severe chest pain, dangerous agitation, or inability to maintain an airway warrants urgent medical care.

The most defensible safety framing is that **O-PCE has a recognizable dissociative pharmacology but an incompletely characterized human risk profile, and gray-market identity adds another layer of uncertainty**.

## Bottom line

O-PCE is no longer supported only by internet anecdotes. Real-world addictovigilance now places it among the more common ketamine analogues in serious French cases.

The evidence supports caution about both the compound itself and the possibility of unintentional substitution.
