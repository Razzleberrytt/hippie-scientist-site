> **Bottom line:** Ergothioneine has moved from an interesting dietary-antioxidant story into early human intervention research. The signal is intriguing, but the larger randomized trial did **not** show a sustained benefit on its primary composite-memory outcome. The evidence therefore stays preliminary.

## Why ergothioneine gets attention

Ergothioneine is a sulfur-containing diet-derived compound that humans actively transport through SLC22A4/OCTN1. Mushrooms are among the richest commonly discussed food sources.

Its chemistry and tissue transport have generated interest in oxidative stress, neuroprotection, and healthy aging.

That biology is interesting, but the key clinical question is whether supplementation actually improves cognition.

## The one-year MCI pilot was positive—but tiny

A 2024 double-blind randomized placebo-controlled pilot enrolled only **19 adults aged 60 or older with mild cognitive impairment**.[1]

Participants assigned to ergothioneine took 25 mg three times per week for one year.

The study reported favorable changes in learning performance and stabilization of neurofilament light chain, a biomarker related to neuronal injury, without concerning changes in routine kidney, liver, or blood safety markers.[1]

That is worth following. It is not enough for a broad cognitive claim because a 19-person pilot is highly vulnerable to chance and unstable effect estimates.

## The larger 2025 trial is more sobering

A 2025 randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial enrolled **147 adults aged 55 to 79 with subjective memory complaints**.[2]

Participants received placebo, 10 mg/day, or 25 mg/day for 16 weeks.

The primary outcome was change in a computerized composite-memory score. The 25 mg group improved within-group at week four, but that signal was **not sustained at week 16**, and the study did not show a significant treatment effect on the primary composite-memory outcome.[2]

Some secondary outcomes were more encouraging, including subjective prospective-memory and sleep-initiation signals.

Those findings are useful for designing future trials. They should not be promoted as though the primary outcome were positive.

## Why the two studies are not directly contradictory

The pilot enrolled people with diagnosed mild cognitive impairment and ran for a year.

The larger trial enrolled older adults with subjective memory concerns whose baseline cognitive performance was generally in the normal range, and it ran for 16 weeks.

Different populations, durations, and endpoints can produce different results.

The correct conclusion is not to pick the more exciting study. It is that the clinical evidence is still too small and heterogeneous to define who benefits, if anyone.

## What the 2025 systematic review changes

A 2025 systematic review found growing experimental and observational interest in ergothioneine and cognition.[3]

But a review cannot create strong clinical evidence when the underlying intervention trials are still sparse.

The review is useful for mapping mechanisms and research gaps, not for upgrading a small human literature into a proven treatment.

## Does ergothioneine prevent dementia?

There is no evidence that ergothioneine supplementation prevents Alzheimer's disease or other dementias.

Biomarker changes, observational associations, and small cognition trials are far short of demonstrating disease prevention.

## Safety

The one-year pilot did not show concerning routine laboratory changes, and the larger 16-week trial provides additional short-term exposure data.[1][2]

That is helpful but still too limited to define uncommon adverse effects, complex medication interactions, or long-term use across broader populations.

## Bottom line

Ergothioneine is a **research-worthy compound with mixed early human evidence**.

The tiny MCI pilot is encouraging. The larger 2025 trial is an important reality check because the primary memory outcome was not convincingly positive.

That combination makes the right verdict straightforward: preliminary evidence, real uncertainty, and a strong need for larger independent trials.
