# Metonitazene: Complete Toxicology, Overdose, Dependence, Withdrawal & Safety Monograph

> **Emergency safety:** Metonitazene is a potent opioid. If someone cannot be awakened, has slow or stopped breathing, blue/gray lips, choking/gurgling while unresponsive, or collapses after an unknown drug, call emergency services and administer **naloxone** if available.

## Quick answer

**Metonitazene is a potent synthetic opioid in the nitazene family.** It entered the modern recreational drug supply in **mid-2020**, shortly after isotonitazene became a major forensic signal.

It quickly became important because it was detected in fatal cases at very low blood concentrations and was frequently found alongside fentanyl and other drugs.

The strongest metonitazene evidence comes from:

- U.S. postmortem case series;
- international fatal cases;
- human metabolism studies;
- mass-spectrometry method validation;
- recent national autopsy surveillance;
- nitazene-class clinical overdose/withdrawal cohorts.

Direct controlled human pharmacokinetics and long-term safety trials do not exist.

## Identity

**Name:** Metonitazene  
**Class:** nitazene / 2-benzylbenzimidazole synthetic opioid  
**Primary target:** mu-opioid receptor  
**Medical approval:** none

Metonitazene is closely related to but distinct from:

- isotonitazene;
- protonitazene;
- etonitazene;
- N-pyrrolidino metonitazene / metonitazepyne.

The similar names matter because each compound has different potency, metabolism, prevalence and legal history.

---

## History: the post-isotonitazene replacement wave

Modern nitazene availability accelerated after 2019.

Metonitazene appeared in the recreational supply around **mid-2020** and began showing up in death investigations by the end of that year. [PMID 34137194](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34137194/)

The sequence illustrates a core NPS-market pattern:

1. one potent synthetic opioid emerges;
2. regulators and laboratories adapt;
3. related analogues replace or supplement it;
4. toxicology again has to catch up.

Metonitazene became one of the clearest examples.

---

## Pharmacology

Metonitazene is a potent **mu-opioid receptor agonist**.

MOR activation can produce:

- analgesia;
- euphoria;
- sedation;
- miosis;
- respiratory depression;
- physical dependence;
- tolerance.

A 2026 Swedish study measured six nitazenes and found MOR potencies in the sub- to low-nanomolar range; metonitazene was among compounds with potency/efficacy comparable to or exceeding fentanyl in that assay. [PMID 42034717](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42034717/)

That is a laboratory finding.

It is **not** a human dose conversion.

---

## U.S. postmortem evidence

A landmark 2021 U.S. forensic study confirmed metonitazene in **20 authentic postmortem cases**.

Key findings:

- median blood concentration: 3.8 ng/mL;
- range: 0.5–33 ng/mL;
- metonitazene was the **only opioid in 30%**;
- fentanyl was present in **55%**;
- other NPS benzodiazepines, opioids or hallucinogens also appeared.

Medical examiners included metonitazene among drugs responsible for death. [PMID 34137194](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34137194/)

The “only opioid” finding is important because it supports intrinsic capacity for fatal opioid poisoning rather than merely passive co-detection with fentanyl.

---

## DEA fatality data

When DEA permanently scheduled metonitazene, it reported **at least 51 U.S. fatalities between July 2020 and August 2021** associated with the compound.

Again, association does not mean metonitazene was the sole drug in every death.

It demonstrates rapid and substantial public-health impact within roughly a year of emergence. [Federal Register](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-08-18/pdf/2023-17778.pdf)

---

## International fatal evidence

### Japan

A 2023 case report documented fatal acute metonitazene intoxication. [PMID 36801590](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36801590/)

### Norway

National Norwegian forensic surveillance identified:

- 11 metonitazene-related deaths in 2023;
- 18 metonitazene-related deaths in 2024.

Metonitazene became the dominant nitazene in that dataset. [PMID 41477999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41477999/)

### Sweden

A 2026 Swedish study of **57 nitazene-positive autopsy cases** from 2020–2024 found metonitazene in **38**, making it the most frequently encountered nitazene in the series. [PMID 42034717](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42034717/)

This shows that metonitazene's significance persisted well beyond its early U.S. emergence.

---

## What overdose looks like

Metonitazene poisoning produces a classic opioid toxidrome:

- profound sedation;
- inability to wake;
- slow/shallow breathing;
- apnea;
- cyanosis;
- pinpoint pupils;
- aspiration;
- cardiac arrest in severe cases.

Respiratory depression is the life-threatening mechanism.

---

## Naloxone

Naloxone is appropriate for suspected metonitazene overdose.

A 2025 scoping review of nitazene overdoses included metonitazene cases treated with naloxone and highlighted the need for hospitalization/observation in many cases. [PMID 39904921](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39904921/)

There is no evidence-based reason to invent a special consumer “metonitazene naloxone protocol.”

The practical response remains:

- call emergency services;
- give naloxone according to the available product/emergency instructions;
- support breathing if trained;
- continue observation because toxicity can recur.

---

## Fentanyl and polysubstance overlap

In the original U.S. case series, **55%** of metonitazene-positive deaths also involved fentanyl.

New York City casework likewise found nitazenes commonly alongside fentanyl/fentanyl analogues and xylazine. [PMID 37638699](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37638699/)

This matters because multiple respiratory/CNS depressants can:

- increase overdose severity;
- prolong sedation;
- complicate naloxone response;
- obscure which substance caused which effect.

---

## Metabolism

Human-hepatocyte research shows metonitazene undergoes:

- N-deethylation;
- O-dealkylation;
- glucuronidation;
- additional secondary transformations. [PMID 38582802](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38582802/)

Metabolite-aware testing is important because parent concentrations can be extremely low.

### Metabolites vs degradation products

The 2026 Swedish study identified possible:

- N-desethyl metonitazene;
- acetamido metonitazene;
- 5-amino metonitazene;
- other amino-containing products.

The authors cautioned that some signals may reflect **postmortem or storage-related degradation** rather than simple in-vivo metabolism. [PMID 42034717](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42034717/)

That is exactly the kind of forensic nuance simple internet drug charts miss.

---

## Drug testing and detection

### Routine screens

Many routine hospital opioid immunoassays do not specifically target metonitazene.

A negative ordinary opioid test does not reliably exclude it.

### Definitive testing

Forensic methods use:

- LC-QTOF-MS for screening/metabolite discovery;
- LC-MS/MS for quantitative confirmation;
- high-resolution mass spectrometry.

The 2021 U.S. study combined QTOF screening with tandem-MS confirmation. [PMID 34137194](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34137194/)

NYC subsequently validated metonitazene measurement in:

- plasma;
- blood;
- urine;
- liver;
- brain. [PMID 37638699](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37638699/)

---

## Toxic concentration: why one number does not work

Fatal metonitazene cases span a range of blood concentrations.

Severity depends on:

- tolerance;
- other opioids;
- benzodiazepines;
- xylazine/alcohol;
- route;
- timing;
- specimen site;
- postmortem redistribution;
- metabolism.

Therefore:

> A published fatal concentration is not a consumer lethal-dose calculator.

The same concentration can carry different meaning in different circumstances.

---

## Tolerance

Repeated MOR agonism causes neuroadaptation.

Direct controlled metonitazene tolerance trials are absent, but opioid-class evidence makes tolerance strongly plausible.

Tolerance can promote:

- escalation;
- dependence;
- reduced perceived sedation.

### Loss of tolerance

Tolerance falls during abstinence or substantial reduction.

Returning to previous exposure patterns can then sharply increase overdose risk.

Unknown potency makes this especially hazardous with unregulated nitazenes.

---

## Physical dependence and withdrawal

There is no large metonitazene-only prospective withdrawal cohort.

But human nitazene clinical surveillance now documents **acute opioid withdrawal after repeated nitazene exposure**, and metonitazene belongs to the same potent MOR-agonist class. [PMID 40810707](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40810707/)

The evidence therefore supports:

- serious physical-dependence risk;
- opioid-like withdrawal;
- substantial uncertainty about exact metonitazene timing.

Possible opioid-withdrawal symptoms include:

- restlessness;
- anxiety;
- insomnia;
- sweating/chills;
- nausea/vomiting;
- diarrhea;
- abdominal cramps;
- muscle aches;
- craving.

There is no validated metonitazene home-taper schedule.

---

## Dependence vs opioid use disorder

Physical dependence means withdrawal follows reduction/cessation.

OUD is a broader pattern involving impaired control and continued use despite harm.

Warning signs include:

- craving;
- inability to reduce use;
- escalating use;
- using despite overdose;
- hazardous use;
- work/family disruption;
- tolerance;
- withdrawal.

Both deserve clinical attention, but they are not synonymous.

---

## Treatment and support

No medication is specifically approved for “metonitazene addiction.”

Established opioid-use-disorder care applies.

Evidence-based OUD medications include:

- buprenorphine;
- methadone;
- extended-release naltrexone in appropriate patients.

This article does not provide self-induction doses or DIY detox schedules.

For U.S. treatment resources:

- **FindTreatment.gov**
- SAMHSA National Helpline: **1-800-662-HELP (4357)**

## Legal and regulatory history

### International

In March 2022, the UN Commission on Narcotic Drugs voted to place **metonitazene in Schedule I of the 1961 Single Convention**.

The international-control decision entered into force after notification in May 2022. [UNODC](https://www.unodc.org/LSS/Announcement/Details/a56e0bd9-0da5-4152-a34d-7cff7746bf50)

### United States

Metonitazene was initially included in DEA's April 2022 temporary nitazene Schedule I order.

DEA later **permanently placed metonitazene in Schedule I**, with the final rule published August 18, 2023 to meet U.S. treaty obligations. [Federal Register](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-08-18/pdf/2023-17778.pdf)

## Special populations

Controlled metonitazene studies are absent for:

- pregnancy;
- breastfeeding;
- adolescents;
- older adults;
- respiratory disease;
- liver/kidney disease.

Particular concern applies to:

- opioid-naive users;
- people mixing sedatives;
- people recently abstinent;
- people using alone;
- people relying on counterfeit pills/powders.

No direct evidence should be mistaken for no risk.

## Myths and misconceptions

### “Metonitazene is basically fentanyl.”

No. It is chemically distinct, even though both act strongly at MOR.

### “It only matters when fentanyl is present.”

False. Metonitazene was the only identified opioid in 30% of the original U.S. case series.

### “A normal opioid screen rules it out.”

False.

### “A low ng/mL blood level means it was a small harmless exposure.”

False. Very potent opioids can be toxic at low measured concentrations.

### “Withdrawal is not proven, so dependence is unlikely.”

No. Nitazene withdrawal is directly documented as a class phenomenon and potent MOR agonism strongly supports dependence risk.

### “The market moved on, so metonitazene is historical.”

False. It remained prominent in Norwegian and Swedish fatality data through 2024.

---

## What we know vs what remains unknown

### Established

- potent MOR agonism;
- fatal overdoses;
- frequent fentanyl co-detection;
- low-concentration forensic detection;
- defined human metabolism;
- international spread;
- U.S. and international Schedule I control.

### Strongly plausible / class-supported

- tolerance;
- physical dependence;
- opioid withdrawal;
- increased danger with other depressants;
- loss-of-tolerance overdose risk.

### Not established

- safe recreational dose;
- universal lethal blood concentration;
- human fentanyl-equivalence ratio;
- metonitazene-specific withdrawal timeline;
- DIY taper;
- long-term human safety;
- pregnancy safety.

---

## Bottom line

Metonitazene became one of the most important post-isotonitazene synthetic opioids because it combined:

- potent MOR activity;
- rapid international spread;
- fatal exposure at very low concentrations;
- frequent fentanyl overlap;
- difficult analytical detection;
- evolving metabolites/degradation interpretation;
- persistence across later European death datasets.

Its strongest harm-reduction lesson is the same one the broader nitazene family keeps teaching:

> **An unfamiliar opioid can be pharmacologically powerful, analytically easy to miss, and embedded in a polysubstance supply long before users or routine clinical testing catch up.**
