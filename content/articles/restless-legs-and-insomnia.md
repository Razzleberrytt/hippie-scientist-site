> **Bottom line:** Restless legs syndrome (RLS) can look like insomnia because it makes settling down at night difficult, but the mechanism is different. The classic pattern is an **urge to move the legs that worsens during rest, is strongest in the evening or night, and improves temporarily with movement**. That pattern deserves a different evaluation than ordinary sleep-onset insomnia.

## The four-part pattern matters more than the word “restless”

Lots of things can make legs feel uncomfortable at night: muscle cramps, neuropathy, arthritis, anxiety, positional discomfort, medication effects, or simply overworked muscles.

RLS is more specific. The hallmark pattern includes:

- an urge to move the legs, often with unpleasant sensations;
- symptoms that begin or worsen during rest;
- temporary improvement with movement such as walking or stretching; and
- a strong evening or nighttime predominance.

The more closely symptoms match that pattern, the more useful an RLS-focused evaluation becomes.

## Why RLS gets mislabeled as insomnia

Someone with RLS may say, “I can’t fall asleep,” which sounds identical to sleep-onset insomnia.

But ask what happens when they lie still. If the answer is “my legs start crawling, buzzing, pulling, or feeling impossible to keep still, and walking helps,” the problem is no longer generic insomnia.

The distinction matters because escalating sedating supplements does not address the same mechanism.

## Periodic limb movements are related but not identical

**Periodic limb movements during sleep** are repetitive movements that occur after sleep begins. They can occur with RLS but are not the same diagnosis.

A person can have limb movements during sleep without the conscious evening urge-to-move pattern of RLS. Likewise, someone can have RLS symptoms while awake before sleep without knowing whether periodic movements occur after they fall asleep.

This is one reason a symptom history is more useful than assuming every nighttime leg movement has one cause.

## The 2025 AASM guideline changed the treatment conversation

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine published an updated clinical practice guideline for RLS and periodic limb movement disorder in 2025.[1]

One important theme is that treatment decisions should not begin with a generic sedative. The guideline emphasizes **iron status**, aggravating factors, and evidence-based RLS therapies.

It also reflects increased concern about **augmentation** with long-term dopaminergic treatment — a phenomenon in which symptoms can start earlier, become more intense, or spread after chronic exposure to certain dopamine-targeting medications.

That makes old “just give a dopamine agonist” summaries increasingly outdated.

## Iron is important — but more is not automatically better

Iron biology is closely tied to RLS, and the AASM guideline includes a good-practice statement around regular iron studies in clinically significant RLS.[1]

That does **not** mean everyone with restless legs should buy high-dose iron.

Iron can accumulate to harmful levels, causes gastrointestinal side effects, and may interact with medications. The useful approach is to assess iron status — commonly including ferritin and transferrin saturation — and interpret it in the clinical context.

In other words: **test the hypothesis before treating the deficiency.**

## Check what may be aggravating the pattern

RLS symptoms can be influenced by medications, substances and other health conditions.

A review may include:

- some antihistamines;
- some serotonergic or antidopaminergic medications;
- caffeine and other stimulants in susceptible people;
- alcohol;
- pregnancy;
- kidney disease;
- and iron deficiency or low iron stores.

This does not mean any one item is automatically the cause. It means timing matters: if symptoms abruptly appeared after a new medication or major health change, that information belongs in the evaluation.

Prescribed medication should not be stopped abruptly just to test a theory.

## RLS versus anxiety-driven restlessness

Anxiety can produce generalized physical agitation and a feeling of being unable to relax. RLS has a more stereotyped **rest–movement–evening** pattern.

Ask three questions:

1. Does being still make it worse?
2. Does moving the legs reliably relieve it, at least temporarily?
3. Is evening or nighttime clearly worse than daytime?

A strong yes to all three is much more suggestive of RLS than simply feeling keyed up before bed.

## RLS versus nocturnal leg cramps

A cramp is usually a sudden, painful tightening of a muscle that may leave soreness afterward.

RLS is usually described as an urge to move plus sensations such as crawling, pulling, buzzing, aching or internal restlessness. Movement helps because the person is moving, not because a contracted muscle is being stretched back to normal length.

The two can coexist, but they should not be treated as interchangeable.

## Where CBT-I fits when RLS and insomnia coexist

A person can have RLS **and** develop chronic insomnia around it.

For example, months of unpredictable symptoms can create fear of bedtime, extended time awake in bed, irregular schedules and conditioned arousal. Treating RLS may remove the original trigger while an insomnia loop remains.

In that situation, RLS-directed management and CBT-I can address different layers of the problem. Major insomnia guidelines support CBT-I for chronic insomnia.[2]

## When to get evaluated

Evaluation is particularly useful when:

- the urge-to-move pattern is frequent or worsening;
- sleep is repeatedly disrupted;
- symptoms appeared during pregnancy or with a major health change;
- there is known kidney disease or possible iron deficiency;
- symptoms began after a medication change; or
- daytime function is being affected.

## Bottom line

Restless legs syndrome is not simply “being restless in bed.” Its signature is an evening-predominant urge to move that worsens at rest and improves with movement.

That pattern changes the sleep decision tree. Rather than treating the complaint as generic insomnia, the higher-value move is to evaluate RLS-specific contributors — especially iron status and aggravating factors — while addressing chronic insomnia separately if it has developed alongside the leg symptoms.
