## Anger is real; aggression is a behavior

Feeling angry after injustice, rejection, humiliation or frustration is a common human experience. Sometimes anger signals a violated boundary or a need to act. Other times it becomes prolonged, disproportionate, exhausting or difficult to manage. **Anger dysregulation** refers to difficulty modulating anger's intensity, duration or behavioral consequences, rather than a stand-alone diagnosis inferred from one argument. [1,3]

**Aggression is different.** It concerns behavior aimed at harming or threatening others; it can be verbal, physical or indirect depending on the research instrument. A person can feel furious and refrain from harming anyone. Another can act coercively without displaying visible anger. The distinction matters for evidence and for ethics: an improvement on a self-rated anger scale does not necessarily mean that intimidation or violence has stopped. [2,4]

This article is a **critical narrative synthesis**, not a new quantitative meta-analysis. It compares existing studies of arousal regulation, anger beliefs, social cognition and psychotherapy. Their outcomes differ, so it would be scientifically incorrect to combine all reported effects into a single cure rate.

## What the 154-study arousal review found

Kjaervik and Bushman analyzed **154 studies and 184 samples involving 10,189 participants**. They compared activities intended to increase or decrease physiological arousal and their effects on anger or aggression. Activities that **decreased arousal** showed a moderate-to-large average reduction, **g = -0.63**, while increasing-arousal activities produced a negligible mean estimate, **g = -0.02**. [1]

The findings challenge a familiar intuition: "I need to get the anger out by doing something intense." Venting and arousal-increasing behavior may feel satisfying in the moment without decreasing anger as a measured outcome. Activities such as breathing and mindfulness practices often aim to reduce activation instead. [1]

This does **not** mean all vigorous physical exercise is harmful, that no one benefits from movement, or that a brief grounding action is guaranteed to work. The experiments varied in population, setting, comparator, activity, and measurement duration. A laboratory induction is not equivalent to a home with long-standing coercion or to clinical treatment for recurrent explosive behavior.

## The temptation to vent

Anger can feel physically urgent, making intense confrontation seem like immediate relief. Some people interpret that relief as proof a confrontation was necessary. But subjective short-term release and a durable reduction of anger are different outcomes. In the large arousal review, average effects did not support the idea that **increasing arousal is a consistently effective anger-management strategy**. [1]

A safer practice during escalating conflict is to separate action from feeling. One can acknowledge, "I feel very angry," delay a consequential message, leave a heated interaction when safe, or practice a calming exercise. These are options for self-regulation, not proof that a person should suppress valid grievances indefinitely.

If someone uses threats, violence or coercion, telling the harmed person to practice calm communication misses the problem. Safety may require outside help and firm boundaries; it is not a two-person emotion-management assignment.

## Rumination, suppression and reappraisal

The 2025 meta-analysis by Pop and colleagues aggregated **81 studies and 115 effects** examining links between anger and regulation strategies. Anger was positively associated with rumination, avoidance and suppression and negatively associated with reappraisal and acceptance. [3]

Another review of **137 articles, 171 studies and more than 250,000 participants** found associations between maladaptive regulation patterns and aggression; difficulties in regulating emotion showed an estimated pooled correlation around **r = 0.25**, whereas so-called adaptive strategies had smaller, less consistent inverse associations. [4]

Those studies inform plausible habits worth evaluating. **Rumination** means repeatedly replaying an offense or imagined retaliation; **suppression** means attempting to hide or push down emotional expression; **reappraisal** means reconsidering interpretations without denying reality. But correlations may reflect reciprocal effects: angry people might ruminate more, and rumination might also sustain anger. Observational designs rarely isolate both directions cleanly. [3,4]

Reappraisal also has limits. Reinterpreting an ambiguous slight may be useful. Reinterpreting repeated intimidation as harmless can be dangerous. Emotion regulation should not require a person to ignore reliable evidence of harm.

## What DBT research actually tested

Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) often includes skills for identifying feelings, tolerating distress, reducing impulsive reactions and maintaining boundaries. A 2022 systematic review combined **34 DBT studies with 2,536 participants**. It found a **small anger reduction (Hedges' g = -0.21)**. The mean aggression estimate was around **g = -0.10**, not statistically significant in the reported pooled analysis. [2]

That difference is important. Someone may become less subjectively angry without necessarily changing all of their harmful conduct. Studies also span different populations and treatment implementations, and the measured aggression outcomes are not interchangeable with criminal violence, coercive control or risk to an individual partner. [2]

A 2026 seven-study review of DBT in BPD examined depression and anger symptoms and described substantial heterogeneity; after specific statistical adjustments, the mean anger effect was no longer clearly significant. It would be wrong to use that uncertainty to dismiss treatment entirely, but equally wrong to promise that a course of DBT reliably eliminates every outburst or relationship problem. [7]

## Mindfulness and attention practices

A 2025 meta-analysis of mindfulness-based interventions reported average benefits for **anger (d approximately -0.48)** and **aggression (d approximately -0.61)**. It drew attention to differences in controls, populations, duration and measurements; stronger claims require larger and better-controlled comparisons, including follow-up on meaningful real-world behavior. [5]

Mindfulness is sometimes marketed as never judging anything. In clinical practice, a useful distinction is learning to notice the bodily and cognitive signs of an emotion **without immediately acting on them**. That does not require approving the event that caused the anger or tolerating abuse.

As with other interventions, a positive pooled result is an average. Some people may dislike inward attention during trauma-related distress, and one specific exercise may be unhelpful. The evidence cannot identify a universal best method for every situation.

## Can cognitive bias work change anger?

Cognitive bias modification (CBM) research attempts to alter automatic interpretations or attention patterns, often through repeated computerized tasks. A 2023 meta-analysis included **29 randomized controlled trials involving 2,334 participants**, with **small average effects** on anger (**g around -0.18**) and aggression (**g around -0.23**). [6]

These estimates suggest mechanisms worth researching, but task performance and changes in questionnaire scores do not guarantee safe behavior outside a study. Protocols vary, and longer-term transfer to everyday relationships remains uncertain. It would be misleading to imply that a brief phone game can cure serious violence or emotional regulation problems.

## Anger across diagnoses—and without one

Intense or persistent anger can occur alongside depression, anxiety, trauma-related disorders, neurodevelopmental differences, sleep problems, substance effects, and various personality patterns. It can also occur without any mental disorder. **Anger alone does not diagnose BPD, PTSD, ADHD, intermittent explosive disorder or bipolar disorder.** [7,8]

An appropriate assessment explores how long a pattern has been present, what emotions and actions occur, triggers, safety, functioning, other symptoms, developmental history, medications and contextual stressors. A diagnosis is not a substitute for examining whether someone is threatened or controlled.

It is also a mistake to claim every angry reaction is "trauma response" or an involuntary brain event. Biology, learning and stress can influence behavior while personal accountability and practical safety remain important.

## How to recognize escalation before behavior becomes harmful

Noticing early signals can create choices. A person might observe a racing heart, clenched jaw, a strong desire to send repeated messages, narrowing attention, absolute language or an urge to confront someone immediately. The observation is personal rather than a validated universal warning score.

During escalation, calming the body, postponing irreversible communication, changing environments when safe, and seeking support may reduce the chance of acting impulsively. The arousal meta-analysis makes the rationale for decreasing activation plausible, without proving every specific routine works for everyone. [1]

A boundary can be both firm and calm: "I won't keep talking while I'm being insulted" identifies behavior and an action. It does not require guessing a partner's motive or diagnosis. When violence or threats are present, personal safety planning takes precedence over relationship negotiation.

## When help is needed

Repeated anger that causes distress, disrupts work or relationships, or leads to unsafe behavior merits assessment. The clinical goal should include **reduced harmful behavior and improved functioning**, not only a score on an anger questionnaire. Evidence from broad treatment meta-analyses can inform choices, but a clinician should match care to the actual pattern. [2,5,8]

Immediate danger, threats, serious aggression or violence require a safety-centered response. Anyone in immediate danger in the United States can call 911. People affected by intimate-partner violence may seek confidential support from the National Domestic Violence Hotline at **800-799-SAFE (7233)** or via [thehotline.org](https://www.thehotline.org/), including people unsure whether their situation qualifies.

## What this literature cannot determine

No review cited here can tell us why a particular individual became angry yesterday, whether an apology is sincere, whether a specific partner will become violent, or whether a mental-health diagnosis explains abusive behavior. Self-report anger, lab-based provocation and real-world aggression are distinct endpoints. [1–7]

A pooled intervention effect does not specify the safest interpersonal response. In particular, a victim or partner should not be made responsible for treating someone else's aggression by changing how they speak, providing more reassurance or allowing threats.

## Study limitations and future directions

Even large meta-analyses inherit measurement and sampling problems from their constituent studies. Self-reported emotion, behavioral lab tasks and actual long-term safety outcomes are not equivalent. The anger-regulation literature mixes interventions designed to reduce immediate arousal with therapies intended to develop skills over months. [1–6]

There is substantial heterogeneity in intervention comparisons, study quality, adverse-event reporting and follow-up. Future studies should pre-register clinically important behaviors, track both harm and improvement, use active comparators, assess mechanisms carefully and include groups underserved by typical university studies.

## Bottom line

**Anger is a feeling, not permission to harm someone.** A large meta-analysis supports calming arousal over arousal-increasing venting for average anger reduction. Other studies find modest benefits for DBT and selected mindfulness or cognitive-bias approaches, with important limitations, especially when outcomes involve real-world aggression. [1,2,5,6]

The most responsible approach combines emotional awareness with accountability, behavioral change, attention to safety, and treatment where needed. Neither emotion nor diagnosis predicts a particular person's character or future. [3,4,8]
