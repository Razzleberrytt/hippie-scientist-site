# Rilmazafone: Complete Prodrug, Metabolism, Fatal Toxicology & Safety Monograph

> **Emergency safety:** Inability to wake, slow or irregular breathing, blue/gray lips, choking or gurgling while unresponsive, seizure, collapse, or severe confusion after a sedative or mislabeled product requires emergency care. Rilmazafone produces active benzodiazepine metabolites and can interact dangerously with opioids, alcohol and other sedatives. Naloxone does not reverse benzodiazepines but should be used when opioid exposure is possible.

## Quick answer

**Rilmazafone is a prescription hypnotic used in Japan and a benzodiazepine prodrug.** The administered parent has an open-ring structure; metabolism converts it into active compounds that adopt a triazolobenzodiazepine structure and produce benzodiazepine-type effects.

That makes rilmazafone different from most RC benzos chemically, but not a loophole in benzodiazepine biology.

It also has a split evidence base:
- substantial older therapeutic/metabolism literature from regulated medical use;
- newer forensic concern when rilmazafone appears in online or mislabeled products outside that regulated context.

## Identity

**Canonical name:** Rilmazafone  
**CAS:** 99593-25-6  
**Class:** benzodiazepine-type hypnotic prodrug  
**Medical use:** prescription hypnotic in Japan  
**Key active metabolites:** rilmazolam, N-desmethyl rilmazolam, di-desmethyl rilmazolam and related products

FDA's substance registry identifies rilmazafone as a distinct ingredient substance; registration does not imply U.S. approval.

## History and medical use

Rilmazafone was developed as a sleep-inducing medicine and has been prescribed in Japan.

Japanese medical-use data distinguish it from most designer benzodiazepines, which never reached approved therapeutic use.

That history matters because it provides direct controlled-human information. It does **not** make nonmedical online products equivalent to Japanese pharmaceutical formulations.

## Why rilmazafone is a prodrug

Rilmazafone is initially a **ring-opened** molecule.

After administration it undergoes enzymatic transformations that include:
- removal of the glycyl group;
- ring closure;
- sequential demethylation;
- additional downstream metabolism.

The ring closure creates active compounds with a triazolobenzodiazepine structure.

In practical terms, the parent name “rilmazafone” describes only the starting molecule; the CNS pharmacology is driven by the active metabolites produced after administration.

## Human pharmacokinetics

Historical human work measured active metabolites in plasma.

The 2023 forensic paper summarizes an earlier small human study in which active metabolites peaked on different timelines:
- rilmazolam relatively early;
- N-desmethyl rilmazolam later;
- di-desmethyl rilmazolam much later and with a longer half-life.

That staggered metabolite pattern matters because subjective onset/offset does not map to one parent-drug concentration.

The evidence should not be converted into recreational timing advice.

## Controlled therapeutic evidence

A randomized double-blind crossover study in healthy adults aged 60-70 compared rilmazafone with zolpidem, triazolam and placebo for next-day residual effects.

That kind of controlled therapeutic evidence is unusual in the RC/NPS section and raises confidence about **pharmaceutical rilmazafone used as studied**.

It does not validate:
- unknown powder/tablet strength;
- online formulations;
- combinations with alcohol or opioids;
- products mislabeled as other drugs.

## Human effects and acute toxicity

Expected benzodiazepine-metabolite effects include:
- sedation;
- hypnotic effects;
- psychomotor slowing;
- impaired balance and coordination;
- memory impairment;
- reduced consciousness at high exposure.

Severe toxicity becomes more likely when other CNS depressants are present.

## 2023 fatal intoxication evidence

A 2023 Swedish forensic report described two deaths linked to rilmazafone exposure.

Packages at the scenes were labeled **“Pagoclone”**, but toxicology identified active rilmazafone metabolites. Because commercial reference standards were lacking, investigators synthesized metabolite standards for quantification.

The forensic conclusions were:
- rilmazafone exposure was considered the **cause of death** in one case;
- rilmazafone **contributed** to the second.

Both cases also contained other drugs, so interpretation required full case context.

## Product mislabeling is central to the risk

This is one of the strongest lessons from rilmazafone casework.

A package label can be wrong.

That creates two separate uncertainties:
1. **pharmacologic uncertainty** — the active metabolite profile after rilmazafone;
2. **product uncertainty** — whether the material is rilmazafone at all.

The first can be studied scientifically. The second requires analytical testing of the actual product.

## Interactions

### Opioids
Benzodiazepine-active metabolites plus opioids can cause additive respiratory and CNS depression.

### Alcohol
Alcohol can amplify sedation, amnesia and impaired coordination.

### Other sedatives
Other benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, pregabalin/gabapentinoids and sedating medications can compound effects.

Both 2023 fatal cases involved additional medications, illustrating why mixed-drug interpretation is essential.

## Tolerance and physical dependence

Repeated exposure to active benzodiazepine-site modulators can produce tolerance and physical dependence.

Direct rilmazafone-specific dependence incidence is not well characterized in modern literature.

Medical approval in one jurisdiction does not eliminate benzodiazepine dependence biology.

## Withdrawal

If physical dependence develops, benzodiazepine-type withdrawal can include:
- rebound insomnia/anxiety;
- tremor;
- autonomic symptoms;
- perceptual disturbance;
- confusion;
- seizures;
- delirium.

The 2025 multisociety benzodiazepine guideline recommends against abrupt discontinuation when dependence is likely.

There is no validated rilmazafone-specific DIY taper schedule, especially for unregulated products of uncertain strength.

## Addiction / use-disorder education

Physical dependence is not identical to a benzodiazepine use disorder.

A broader use disorder may involve:
- impaired control;
- repeated hazardous use;
- craving;
- blackouts;
- continued use despite health or relationship harm;
- excessive time spent obtaining or recovering.

## Treatment and support

Acute intoxication care focuses on:
- airway and breathing;
- mental status;
- trauma;
- co-exposures.

Flumazenil is used selectively in medical settings and is not a universal antidote because it may precipitate withdrawal or seizures in susceptible patients.

For regular nonmedical exposure, dependence should be clinically assessed before abrupt cessation.

## Drug testing

Rilmazafone testing is complicated by prodrug metabolism.

Important analytes include active downstream compounds rather than the parent alone.

Definitive forensic testing may require:
- LC-MS/MS;
- LC-HRMS;
- authentic/synthesized metabolite standards;
- metabolite-aware interpretation.

Routine benzodiazepine screens may not identify the exposure reliably or specifically.

## Forensic interpretation

A rilmazafone case should distinguish:
- parent drug/material evidence;
- active metabolites in blood;
- co-detected drugs;
- cause vs contribution;
- product label vs confirmed identity.

The 2023 cases are especially valuable because they show how reference-material gaps can hinder interpretation and how laboratories may need to validate unusual metabolites before drawing conclusions.

## Epidemiology and U.S. market signal

CFSRE's NPS Discovery program reported rilmazafone in drug materials from Pennsylvania and noted emergence in the recreational supply.

That is a forensic surveillance signal, not a prevalence estimate for the United States.

The compound's market relevance may change rapidly because online NPS markets substitute products when laws and availability change.

## Legal and regulatory status

### United States
CFSRE's 2023 monograph reported that rilmazafone was **not explicitly scheduled in the United States** at that time. This October 2026 article does not treat that statement as a guarantee of current legality in every jurisdiction.

State laws and federal analogue questions can differ, and rules can change.

### Japan
Rilmazafone has recognized prescription hypnotic use in Japan.

Medical approval there does not imply U.S. FDA approval.

## Special populations

Rilmazafone has some controlled data in older adults, unlike many RC benzos.

However, unregulated-product evidence remains sparse for:
- pregnancy/breastfeeding;
- adolescents;
- severe liver/kidney disease;
- respiratory disease;
- polysubstance-use populations.

Older adults remain vulnerable to falls, cognitive effects and prolonged sedation from benzodiazepine-active drugs.

## Myths versus evidence

### “Rilmazafone is not a benzodiazepine, so benzo risks do not apply.”
**Misleading.** It is a prodrug converted into active benzodiazepine structures.

### “It is a medicine in Japan, so online rilmazafone is safe.”
**False.** Pharmaceutical formulation and product identity are part of safety.

### “A package labeled pagoclone contains pagoclone.”
**False.** The 2023 fatal cases directly demonstrate the danger of label trust.

### “The parent drug must be found to prove exposure.”
**False.** Active metabolites can be the key forensic evidence.

### “Medical-use history means dependence is impossible.”
**False.** Benzodiazepine-active drugs can produce physical dependence.

## Major evidence gaps

- modern population pharmacokinetics;
- contemporary poison-center/ED cohorts;
- incidence of dependence in nonmedical use;
- prospective withdrawal course;
- counterfeit/mislabeled product prevalence;
- current U.S. market prevalence;
- pregnancy and severe organ-dysfunction data.

## Bottom line

Rilmazafone is one of the most instructive drugs in the RC/NPS library because it shows why **molecule, metabolite and product are three different questions**.

The molecule has legitimate medical history. The metabolites are active benzodiazepines. The online product may not be what the label says.

The 2023 fatal cases make the harm-reduction conclusion straightforward: **pharmaceutical history does not protect an unregulated supply from mislabeling, polysubstance toxicity, dependence or fatal outcomes**.
