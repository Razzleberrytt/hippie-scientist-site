# Psychedelics and the brain: what can scientists actually prove?

**Quick answer:** Yes, scientists can measure changes in brain activity when people take classic psychedelics such as psilocybin or LSD. They have even detected some changes weeks later in small studies. But **a change on a brain scan does not prove that new brain cells grew, that depression was cured, or that someone's brain was permanently rewired**. [1–5]

## Start with the basics: what is a psychedelic?

**Psychedelics** are substances that can change perception—the way your brain makes sense of sights, sounds, time, thoughts, and feelings. Different substances grouped under this label can work differently, so this article focuses mainly on **classic psychedelics**, including psilocybin, LSD, and DMT.

These substances influence the **serotonin system**. Serotonin is one of the chemical messengers used by the brain. Cells have **receptors**, which are receiving spots that respond to chemical messages.

One important receptor has the technical name **5-HT2A**. Think of that name as a label for one specific kind of serotonin signal receiver. It is involved in many of the characteristic effects of classic psychedelics. That does not mean it explains everything that happens in a person's experience. [1]

## What do brain scans actually show?

The brain has many regions that work together. Researchers call groups of brain areas that tend to work together **brain networks**.

A type of scan called **functional MRI**, or **fMRI**, estimates changes in brain activity by measuring changes related to blood flow. It does not film individual neurons firing. A **neuron** is a nerve cell that sends and receives signals.

Scientists compare patterns from different brain regions. When two areas change activity in a related way, researchers call that **functional connectivity**. In everyday language, their activity appears more coordinated.

A major study published in April 2026 combined **11 brain-scan datasets** involving classic psychedelic substances. The researchers found a pattern of stronger communication between some large brain networks during psychedelic effects. The pattern differed across drugs and networks. [1]

Imagine a workplace where certain teams usually communicate mostly within their own group. During a psychedelic experience, some teams' activity may become more connected with other teams. That is a **metaphor**, not evidence of new wires being built between parts of the brain.

## Do psychedelics make the brain more connected?

In one sense, sometimes yes. The 2026 analysis found increased **between-network functional connectivity** for certain networks—that means patterns of brain activity in separate groups became more linked.

But that does not mean every part of the brain became more connected to every other part. The researchers also found differences across drugs, regions, and analysis methods. [1]

It is also important to distinguish activity from anatomy. **Anatomy** means physical structure.

| What a scan can suggest | What it cannot prove on its own |
| --- | --- |
| Two brain areas show related activity | A new physical connection grew between them |
| A drug changes patterns during an experience | The changes are permanent |
| A group shows average differences | Every participant experienced exactly the same effect |
| An effect lasts beyond the acute experience | That effect caused a clinical recovery |

The word **acute** means happening during or soon after the substance's effects.

## What about claims that psychedelics "rewire" your brain?

The word **rewire** sounds as though a technician opened the skull and replaced old brain cables with new ones.

Real brains do change over time, of course. Scientists call this capacity **neuroplasticity**: the brain's ability to change how its cells and networks work, and sometimes how they connect.

Laboratory and animal studies suggest that certain psychedelic compounds can change nerve-cell structures and signals. Those findings matter, but they are not proof that an adult human grows a known number of new synapses after a psychedelic session.

A **synapse** is a tiny junction where nerve cells pass signals to one another. "Growing more synapses" is a different claim from "brain scans show changing activity."

In July 2026, a small study of **15 healthy volunteers** used a different scanning method called **PET**, which tracks a labeled marker in the body. Researchers examined a protein linked with synapses before and after psilocybin. They did **not** find a statistically significant overall increase in the marker one week later. [3]

**Statistically significant** means a result met a planned mathematical threshold used to judge whether the data support a difference. Not reaching that threshold does not prove nothing happened anywhere in the brain. It means the study could not establish the overall increase it was testing.

The research team also explored whether the experience's setting made a difference. Interesting differences emerged, but this small, non-randomized comparison cannot establish a universal rule that one kind of room creates new synapses.

## Can effects really last for weeks?

A 2024 study led by Joshua Siegel repeatedly scanned a **small group of healthy adults** before, during, and after a psilocybin session. The researchers found major temporary changes in brain-network activity. Some differences involving particular networks remained measurable after the immediate effects had passed. [2]

The careful wording is **some measured brain-network changes persisted for weeks in that study**. That is not the same as saying every person permanently changes after one experience.

Different experiments measure different things. A brain scan, a questionnaire about someone's experience, and a mental-health symptom scale do not all answer the same question.

## Can psychedelics treat depression?

Research is investigating whether **psilocybin-assisted treatment** can help some people with depression. In these studies, participants generally undergo screening and structured professional support. An experimental clinical program is not equivalent to unsupervised use.

A **randomized trial** is a study in which participants are assigned by chance to different study groups, reducing some kinds of bias.

A 2022 trial reported a meaningful improvement in depression scores for one psilocybin study group at a measured time point, along with adverse effects. [5]

But a 2026 trial, called **EPISODE**, examined people with treatment-resistant depression and did **not** find a statistically significant difference on its main planned response measure at six weeks. Other measures suggested potential improvements, and the trial also reported safety concerns, including a case of lasting visual-perception disturbance. [4]

That is why "shows promise" is scientifically different from "proven cure."

## What are the main unanswered questions?

Researchers are still investigating how these pieces fit together:

- Do changes in brain-network activity help explain later psychological improvements, or do both reflect other processes?
- Do shifts in synapse-related measurements differ by setting, person, or drug?
- Which people could benefit in controlled treatment programs, and who may be more likely to experience harm?
- How durable are any benefits or negative effects over months and years?

Some people experience intense distress during psychedelic effects. There are also concerns about serious psychiatric complications for vulnerable individuals and persistent perceptual symptoms in a smaller group. The evidence cannot support treating these substances as universally safe. [4]

## Popular claims versus what the evidence says

**"You unlock 90 percent of your brain."** No. There is no credible scientific basis for the idea that most of the brain is normally unused.

**"Brain scans prove you grow new neurons."** No. A functional brain scan measures changes linked to activity, not the birth of individual neurons.

**"One experience permanently rewires your brain."** Not established. Some changes have been measured weeks later in small studies, but permanence across individuals has not been proven.

**"Psychedelics cure depression."** Not established. Results vary across studies, and risk-benefit questions remain important.

**"Nothing real happens in the brain."** Also false. Brain-imaging research has repeatedly measured drug-related changes in network activity. [1,2]

## Common questions

**What is neuroplasticity in simple terms?** The brain's ability to adjust with experience, learning, and other influences. It doesn't mean new brain cells grow every time something feels meaningful.

**What is the default mode network?** A group of brain areas often active when people think about themselves, remember the past, or imagine the future. It is one network studied in psychedelic research—not a mysterious "ego switch."

**Does a brain scan show what someone is seeing during a trip?** Not directly. Researchers can measure patterns associated with brain activity, but a scan is not a recording of someone's subjective experience.

**Are all psychedelics alike?** No. They differ in chemistry, how they act, how long their effects last, and how much evidence exists. See [the different types of DMT](/articles/dmt-types-explained/).

## Bottom line

The scientifically interesting answer is neither "psychedelics magically rebuild the brain" nor "nothing changes." **Classic psychedelics can measurably alter human brain-network activity.** Some changes may outlast the immediate experience. The evidence for physical rewiring, lasting clinical benefits, and safety in different people is still developing.
