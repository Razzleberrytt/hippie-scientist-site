> **Bottom line:** Sleep really does change with age, but “older people just sleep badly” is not an evidence-based conclusion. Normal aging tends to produce lighter, more fragmented sleep and earlier circadian timing. Chronic insomnia, obstructive sleep apnea, restless legs syndrome, REM sleep behavior disorder and severe daytime sleepiness remain **clinical problems**, not inevitable features of getting older.[1-5]

## Normal aging changes sleep architecture

A 2026 review of sleep health in adults age 65 and older summarizes several common age-related changes:[1]

- reduced slow-wave sleep;
- reduced REM sleep;
- lower sleep efficiency;
- more fragmented sleep; and
- dampened circadian amplitude.

Those changes can make sleep feel lighter and easier to interrupt.

They do not mean the sleep system has become irrelevant or that restorative sleep is no longer needed.

## “Older adults need less sleep” is too simplistic

People often notice that older relatives go to bed earlier, wake earlier, nap more, or spend less time in deep sleep. From that, it is easy to conclude that aging sharply reduces biological sleep need.

The evidence is more complicated.

Age changes **when and how sleep is expressed**. It also changes health conditions, medications, activity levels, light exposure and social schedules. Those factors can alter total sleep time and sleep continuity without proving that a person simply no longer needs adequate sleep.

The more useful question is whether sleep is sufficient for daytime alertness, mood, cognition and function.

## Circadian timing often shifts earlier

Older adults commonly show earlier sleep and wake tendencies compared with younger adults.[1]

Circadian rhythms can also become less robust, with reduced amplitude of biological signals across the day.

That can contribute to:

- earlier evening sleepiness;
- earlier morning waking;
- more daytime napping; and
- weaker separation between daytime alertness and nighttime sleepiness.

This does not mean every early wake-up is a disorder. But a major unwanted schedule shift that causes impairment still deserves context rather than being dismissed as age.

## Insomnia is common—but common is not the same as normal

A 2023 systematic review/meta-analysis included **18,270 older participants across 16 studies** using DSM-based insomnia definitions.[2]

The pooled prevalence of insomnia disorder was about **19.6%**, although heterogeneity was substantial and the certainty of the prevalence estimate was very low.[2]

That number is useful for scale, not precision.

It says insomnia is common in later life. It does **not** say insomnia is an unavoidable biological consequence of aging.

## Sleep apnea becomes especially important

The 2026 review notes obstructive sleep apnea affects a substantial proportion of older adults.[1]

OSA can present with more than loud snoring. Possible consequences include:

- fragmented sleep;
- morning headache;
- daytime sleepiness;
- impaired concentration;
- nocturia; and
- cardiovascular stress.

A person can therefore describe “light, broken sleep from aging” when airway obstruction is contributing.

Adding a sedating sleep aid does not correct airway collapse.

See [Sleep Apnea vs Insomnia](/articles/sleep-apnea-vs-insomnia/) for the distinction.

## Restless legs can masquerade as generic poor sleep

Restless legs syndrome can make it difficult to sit or lie still in the evening and can repeatedly delay sleep.[4]

The classic pattern involves an urge to move with unpleasant sensations that worsen at rest and improve with movement.

Older adults may also have iron deficiency, kidney disease, neuropathy or medication exposures that complicate the picture.

This is why “try more magnesium” is a weak response to every complaint of nighttime leg discomfort.

The disorder-specific pathway is covered in [Restless Legs, Iron and Sleep](/articles/restless-legs-iron-and-sleep/).

## REM sleep behavior disorder is not normal dream activity

Another age-relevant disorder is REM sleep behavior disorder, in which normal REM-related muscle paralysis is impaired and people may physically enact dreams.

That is different from vivid dreams, ordinary movement during sleep or occasional talking.

The 2026 aging review highlights REM sleep behavior disorder among clinically important sleep conditions in older populations.[1]

Violent or dangerous dream-enactment behavior deserves assessment rather than a supplement experiment.

## CBT-I still works in older adults

Chronic insomnia in later life should not default automatically to sedative medication.

AASM recommends cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia as a first-line behavioral treatment for chronic insomnia in adults.[3]

CBT-I addresses the processes that maintain insomnia, such as:

- conditioned wakefulness in bed;
- unstable sleep scheduling;
- excessive time in bed;
- fear and monitoring around sleep; and
- maladaptive beliefs about the consequences of a bad night.

That is a different intervention from simply giving a list of “sleep hygiene” tips.

## Medication risk changes with age

Older adults are more likely to use multiple medications and may be more vulnerable to:

- next-day sedation;
- dizziness;
- falls;
- confusion;
- anticholinergic effects; and
- drug-drug interactions.

That matters especially for OTC antihistamines such as diphenhydramine and doxylamine.

Feeling sedated is not the same as having a good long-term insomnia treatment.

See [OTC Antihistamines for Sleep](/articles/otc-antihistamines-for-sleep/).

## Pain, nocturia and medical illness can fragment sleep

Older adulthood also brings a higher prevalence of conditions that can repeatedly interrupt sleep:

- chronic pain;
- reflux;
- urinary symptoms;
- heart or lung disease;
- medication effects;
- depression or anxiety; and
- caregiving stress.

If a physical symptom wakes someone ten times a night, a sedative may obscure rather than solve the bottleneck.

[Chronic Pain and Sleep](/articles/chronic-pain-and-sleep/) explains one example of that bidirectional relationship.

## Napping is not automatically bad

Older adults often nap more frequently.

A nap can be useful when nighttime sleep was inadequate or when illness, caregiving or schedule disruption creates daytime sleepiness.

But long or late naps can also reduce nighttime sleep pressure and make insomnia harder for some people.

The correct question is not “Are naps bad?” It is how nap timing and duration interact with the person's nighttime sleep.

See [Naps and Nighttime Sleep](/articles/naps-and-nighttime-sleep/).

## Sleep trackers can create unnecessary alarm

Wearables frequently report lower “deep sleep” in older adults.

Some reduction in slow-wave sleep is expected with aging.[1]

But consumer devices are also imperfect at sleep-stage classification. A low deep-sleep number on a watch should not be interpreted as proof of a disease or as a reason to chase supplements advertised to “boost deep sleep.”

See [How Accurate Are Sleep Trackers?](/articles/sleep-trackers-accuracy/).

## The environment still matters

Aging does not make light, noise, heat and air quality irrelevant.

Older adults may actually be more vulnerable to environmental sleep disruption because sleep is already more fragmented.

A 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis in middle-aged and older adults found associations between long-term exposure to particulate air pollution and worse sleep-health outcomes.[5]

Most of those environmental data are observational, so they do not prove that air pollution directly caused every sleep problem. Still, bedroom and neighborhood conditions belong in a serious sleep assessment.

## What should not be dismissed as “just aging”

Examples include:

- persistent insomnia with meaningful distress;
- loud snoring, gasping or witnessed breathing pauses;
- dangerous daytime sleepiness;
- repeated falls or confusion after sleep medications;
- urge-to-move leg symptoms;
- violent dream enactment;
- major unwanted circadian change; or
- sleep disturbance driven by pain or another medical condition.

## Bottom line

Healthy aging changes sleep, but it does not make poor sleep untreatable.

The most defensible interpretation is:

- sleep becomes lighter and more fragmented on average;
- slow-wave and REM sleep tend to decline;
- circadian rhythms often shift earlier and become less robust;
- insomnia and sleep apnea are common, but they remain disorders rather than inevitable aging; and
- CBT-I, apnea treatment, restless-legs evaluation and medication review can still be highly relevant in later life.

The key distinction is **normal age-related change versus a treatable sleep disorder layered on top of aging**.
