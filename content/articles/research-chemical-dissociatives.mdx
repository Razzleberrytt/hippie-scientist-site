# Research-Chemical Dissociatives: 2F-DCK, FXE, DCK, O-PCE, DMXE and the Identity Problem

## Quick answer

RC dissociatives include a large family of compounds related chemically or pharmacologically to ketamine, PCP, PCE, or methoxetamine.

Names commonly discussed include **2F-DCK, DCK, O-PCE, DMXE, 3-MeO-PCE, 3-HO-PCP, 3-HO-PCE, 3-Me-PCPy, HXE, MXPr, MXiPr, fluorexetamine and 2F-2oxo-PCE.**

The danger in reducing these to “ketamine analogues” is that they can differ substantially in duration, potency, stimulation, behavioral toxicity, and product identity.

## Arylcyclohexylamine does not mean interchangeable

Many RC dissociatives belong to the arylcyclohexylamine family and are thought to act substantially through NMDA-receptor antagonism.

That common mechanism does not make them clinically identical.

Small structural changes can alter:

- receptor selectivity;
- metabolic pathways;
- duration;
- active metabolites;
- stimulant or dopaminergic effects;
- behavioral disinhibition;
- toxicity.

## The FXE / CanKet identity problem

One of the clearest examples comes from **2F-2oxo-PCE**.

CFSRE described it as a positional isomer of fluorexetamine. Drug-checking programs initially encountered an unknown ketamine-like drug that became nicknamed “CanKet.” Later structural work distinguished 2F-2oxo-PCE from the compound originally meant by fluorexetamine.

This matters because a street or vendor label can survive even after the underlying chemistry changes.

So the question “what does FXE feel like?” can be chemically ambiguous unless the material was actually identified.

## Co-occurring opioids change the risk

In CFSRE's 2024 alert, more than half of drug materials containing 2F-2oxo-PCE also contained opioids, primarily fentanyl.

That means the danger around a dissociative sample may not be limited to dissociation.

Profound sedation or slowed breathing should raise concern for opioid co-exposure even when the expected drug was a dissociative.

## Acute toxicity can be behavioral as well as physiological

Dissociative intoxication can involve:

- severe impairment and falls;
- confusion or delirium;
- agitation;
- abnormal perception;
- inability to communicate reliably;
- loss of awareness of injury;
- risky behavior;
- unconsciousness in severe cases.

PCP-like compounds can be particularly difficult to predict from the name alone because stimulant, psychotic, and dissociative features can overlap.

## Duration uncertainty matters

Some users assume all ketamine-like RCs will wear off on roughly a ketamine-like timeline.

That is unsafe.

Different analogues can last much longer, and redosing during a delayed peak can produce unexpectedly prolonged impairment.

This site intentionally does not convert community duration reports into dosing schedules because the identity and concentration of gray-market material can be uncertain.

## Urinary and bladder risk

Chronic heavy ketamine exposure is associated with urinary tract and bladder injury.

Whether every newer analogue carries the same long-term risk at comparable exposures is not established. Structural similarity is a reason for caution, not proof of an identical toxicity rate.

## Bottom line

RC dissociatives are a perfect example of why **drug-family familiarity is not enough**.

A product can be ketamine-like in broad mechanism while differing markedly in duration and behavioral toxicity—and the vendor name may not even identify the correct isomer.

When the chemistry is uncertain, confidence should go down, not up.
