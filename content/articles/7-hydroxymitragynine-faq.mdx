**Identity: what 7-OH is and isn't**

## What is 7-hydroxymitragynine?

**7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) is an opioid-active alkaloid (CAS 174418-82-7) that exists in two completely different contexts: as a minor metabolite your body makes from mitragynine, and as the active ingredient in concentrated commercial products.**

Those two contexts have almost nothing in common in terms of dose, and dose is what the pharmacology, the toxicology, and now the federal law all turn on.

## Is 7-OH the same as kratom?

**No. Kratom is a botanical mixture dominated by mitragynine; 7-OH products are concentrated preparations built around 7-hydroxymitragynine.** Treating them as the same thing is the single most common error in news coverage and online discussion — and it runs in both directions (alarmists citing 7-OH harms against leaf; sellers implying 7-OH tablets are "just kratom").

## Is 7-OH the same as mitragynine?

**No. Mitragynine is the parent alkaloid; 7-OH is its more potent opioid-active metabolite.** They differ in receptor potency, in how the body handles them, and — as of October 2026 — in how federal law treats them.

## Is 7-OH natural?

**It occurs naturally in kratom at trace levels and forms in the human body from mitragynine — but concentrated consumer tablets may contain enriched or manufactured 7-OH unlike ordinary botanical leaf. A label cannot confirm manufacturing method or composition.** "Natural" describes origin, not dose. The HHS October 2026 framework explicitly distinguishes natural botanical kratom (trace 7-OH) from enhanced products.

## Is 7-OH the same as mitragynine pseudoindoxyl (MGPI)?

**No. 7-OH and MGPI are distinct compounds and have different federal scheduling status as of October 12, 2026.** MGPI is subject to the August 26 temporary Schedule I order; 7-OH is the subject of a pending new notice process (DOJ, August 25 and October 9, 2026).

## How is 7-OH different from mitragynine?

| Feature | Mitragynine | 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) |
|---|---|---|
| Role | Parent alkaloid, most abundant in leaf | Metabolite of mitragynine; also sold directly in concentrates |
| Mu-opioid activity | Partial/weak agonist in most assays | More potent MOR agonist (Kruegel et al., 2019; Obeng et al., 2021) |
| Natural leaf levels | Dominant alkaloid | Trace |
| Commercial products | Leaf powder, teas, extracts | Tablets, shots, and concentrates built around 7-OH |
| Federal status (Oct 2026) | Not scheduled | Threshold-based temporary scheduling proceeding (see legal section) |

## How does the body make 7-OH from mitragynine?

**The liver converts part of each mitragynine dose into 7-OH, primarily via the CYP3A4 enzyme.** Human proof: when volunteers took the CYP3A4 inhibitor itraconazole, 7-OH exposure fell while mitragynine exposure rose (PMID 38481700). Human liver-preparation work identified CYP3A isoforms in the conversion pathway (PMID 31263758). This means CYP3A4 inhibitors, inducers, liver function, and genetics can all shift how much 7-OH a given mitragynine dose produces.

**Potency and pharmacology**

## Is 7-OH an opioid?

**Yes, by the definition that matters: it activates the mu-opioid receptor, the same receptor family as morphine and oxycodone, and produces opioid-like effects including analgesia, respiratory depression (in animals), dependence, and withdrawal.** Its full pharmacology differs from classical opioids in the details, but "not a classical opioid" is not "not an opioid."

## How potent is 7-hydroxymitragynine?

**7-OH is substantially more potent at the mu-opioid receptor than its parent mitragynine — potent enough that Kruegel et al. (2019) identified it as the key mediator of mitragynine's analgesic effects in mice, and Kamble et al. (2020) showed human plasma metabolism increases its opioid potency and efficacy.** Precise cross-drug potency ratios (e.g., "X times morphine") depend on the assay, species, and endpoint — be skeptical of any single number quoted without those qualifiers.

## Is 7-OH stronger than morphine?

**No single human potency multiplier is established.** Potency depends on species, exposure route and measured endpoint. A rat breathing study cannot set a human dose comparison (PMID 41106041).

## Why does potency matter more for 7-OH products than for leaf?

**Because concentration, co-ingredients and exposure vary substantially between botanical preparations and enhanced products.** Leaf contains 7-OH at trace levels; the body makes modest additional amounts from mitragynine. Concentrated 7-OH tablets deliver the potent metabolite directly, at levels leaf never approaches. Same molecule, radically different exposure — which is exactly why the October 2026 federal framework regulates by concentration, not by the mere presence of the compound.

**Overdose and breathing risk**

## Can 7-OH slow or stop breathing?

**Yes. In a controlled 2025 rat study, 7-OH produced dose-related respiratory depression that was reversed by naloxone (Zuarth Gonzalez et al., PMID 41106041).** Opioid-like respiratory depression is the mechanism behind fatal opioid overdose generally, and 7-OH has now demonstrated it experimentally.

## Is a lethal human dose of 7-OH known?

**No validated lethal human threshold has been established.** A precise figure cannot be inferred from laboratory assays, rat experiments or mixed-exposure reports (PMID 41106041).

## Can 7-OH cause a fatal overdose?

**No human lethal dose is established, but the mechanism for fatal overdose — opioid-like respiratory depression — is demonstrated, and the risk climbs with dose and with co-use of other depressants (alcohol, benzodiazepines, opioids).** Absence of a published lethal dose is not evidence of safety; it reflects how new concentrated 7-OH products are.

## Does naloxone (Narcan) reverse 7-OH overdose?

**The animal evidence says yes: 7-OH-induced respiratory depression in rats was naloxone-reversible (PMID 41106041).** That makes naloxone the rational field response to suspected 7-OH overdose with depressed breathing — the same as for any opioid-like toxicity. Human reversal data specific to 7-OH are limited, which is expected for an emerging product category, not a reason to withhold naloxone.

## What should you do if someone appears to have overdosed on 7-OH?

**Call emergency services immediately, administer naloxone if available, and follow dispatcher guidance for breathing support.** Do not leave an unresponsive person alone. Rat evidence supports naloxone reversal of opioid-like breathing effects, while human-specific evidence is limited (PMID 41106041).

## What happens if you mix 7-OH with alcohol or benzodiazepines?

**You stack depressants on depressants.** Opioids, alcohol, and benzodiazepines all suppress breathing through overlapping and additive pathways. Polydrug exposure is the most important real-world multiplier of overdose risk for every opioid-like substance, and 7-OH is no exception.

**Dependence, withdrawal, and treatment**

## Can 7-OH cause dependence?

**Yes. Repeated concentrated 7-OH use can produce physical dependence, craving, loss of control, and clinically diagnosed substance use disorder — documented in 2026 addiction-medicine case literature.**

## What does 7-OH withdrawal feel like?

**Published case reports describe an opioid-like withdrawal syndrome.** A 2026 case report detailed management of acute withdrawal after high-dose chronic 7-OH use (Lybik et al., PMID 41690384), and a separate 2026 case required inpatient medically managed withdrawal (Wightman & Hu, PMID 40758956). As with opioids generally, severity varies with dose, duration, and individual factors — and no universal timeline is established.

## How long does 7-OH withdrawal last?

**No reliable universal 7-OH withdrawal timeline is established.** Clinical cases describe opioid-like symptoms but cannot establish a typical duration (PMIDs 41690384, 41875249).

## Can 7-OH withdrawal begin within hours?

**Yes, some patients have reported symptoms within hours, but timing varies.** A single published case described withdrawal beginning within eight hours; this is not a population estimate (PMID 41875249).

## Is there treatment for 7-OH use disorder?

**Yes — the same medications used for opioid use disorder are being applied.** A 2026 case report documented 7-OH use disorder treated with buprenorphine (Hendler et al., PMID 41875249), and a 2026 retrospective case series examined buprenorphine for 7-OH use (Fenske et al., PMID 42225057). These are early clinical reports, not trial evidence — but they establish that addiction medicine is treating 7-OH dependence within the existing OUD framework.

## Is buprenorphine used for 7-OH use disorder?

**Yes, in reported clinical care, though randomized trials are lacking.** The evidence includes a case report (PMID 41875249) and a nine-patient retrospective series (PMID 42225057). This is not a self-treatment guide.

## Where can someone get help for 7-OH dependence?

**A primary-care clinician, an addiction-medicine service, or — in the US — the SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357.** 7-OH dependence is being managed as an opioid-related disorder; you don't need a 7-OH-specific program to get evidence-based help.

**Products: what's actually being sold**

## What is in 7-OH products sold in gas stations and smoke shops?

**Tablets, shots, and concentrates marketed around 7-hydroxymitragynine content — frequently with labeling that doesn't match the contents.** A 2026 peer-reviewed study found elevated 7-OH levels in products misbranded as kratom (Brown et al., PMID 41065466), and a separate 2026 quantitative analysis documented 7-OH levels and stability across commercial kratom products (Avula et al., PMID 41825819).

## Can kratom extracts contain enhanced amounts of 7-OH?

**Yes; a product marketed as kratom is not necessarily equivalent to ordinary botanical leaf.** Product analyses found measured 7-OH quantities that differed from some product labels (PMIDs 41825819 and 41065466).

## Are 7-OH products accurately labeled?

**Often not.** Beyond the misbranding findings above, the broader kratom-product literature has repeatedly documented label-vs-content mismatches and unreported alkaloids. A brand name, a COA, or a milligram number on the front of the package is not proof of what's inside — particularly in a product category that has existed largely outside consistent regulatory oversight.

## How do you read a 7-OH product label?

**Read the alkaloid name, not the marketing words.** The single most important line is which compound the milligrams refer to: *mitragynine* and *7-hydroxymitragynine* are different drugs at different potencies, and federal law now treats them differently. Terms like "nano," "enhanced," or "extract" describe processing — they don't change which alkaloid you're buying, and absorption claims attached to them are the manufacturer's own, not FDA-evaluated. If the label doesn't clearly name the alkaloid and amount, that's information in itself.

## How much 7-OH is in kratom leaf naturally?

**Trace levels — a small fraction of total alkaloid content, with mitragynine dominant.** This is the factual basis for the entire leaf-vs-concentrate distinction: the HHS October 2026 threshold framework (1 mg/g) was explicitly designed around the gap between natural trace occurrence and manufactured concentration.

**Drug testing**

## Do routine drug tests detect 7-OH?

**Standard opiate immunoassays are not designed for 7-OH and can miss it entirely.** Targeted liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) or high-resolution mass spectrometry methods can identify 7-OH and distinguish it from related alkaloids — but only if the lab is specifically looking for it with a validated method (Papsun et al., 2026, PMID 42135874).

## Can 7-OH be confused with other compounds in testing?

**Yes — related kratom alkaloids, including mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, require methods that distinguish them.** This is a known forensic pitfall: without alkaloid-specific methods, related compounds can be misidentified or missed (Papsun et al., 2026).

## Legal status — current order versus proposals (checked October 12, 2026)

> **Regulatory status:** The October 9 HHS/DOJ announcements describe proposals, not an already-effective legal threshold. The new notices are reportedly scheduled for Federal Register publication October 14; inspect the actual publication and any later final temporary order.

## Is 7-OH legal?

**As of October 12, 2026, 7-OH is not temporarily federally scheduled, but DEA has announced revised proposed controls.** State and local laws may separately restrict products; review official rules for the jurisdiction.

## Is 7-OH legal in every U.S. state?

**No single federal answer establishes legality in every state.** State and local laws can impose separate restrictions. Check the actual law and current enforcement guidance where relevant; this is not legal advice.

## Is 7-OH federally Schedule I?

**Not as of October 12, 2026.** The July notice did not lead to a 7-OH temporary order. DOJ announced new notices October 9, reportedly scheduled for **October 14, 2026** Federal Register publication. A future temporary order would require the notice period and publication of a separate order; **November 13 is only a possible earliest date if the expected publication occurs**, not a confirmed effective date.

## What are the federal 7-OH thresholds?

**Under HHS recommendations announced October 9, proposed future Schedule I control for 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl (MGPI), either alone or their combined amount when both are present, would apply if:**

- **concentration exceeds 1 mg per gram** in solids or **1 mg per mL** in liquids, **or**
- **the *mass ratio* of mitragynine to 7-OH, MGPI, or their combined amount is less than 100:1.**

Under the **proposed future rule**, meeting either test would be sufficient. These thresholds are **not yet operative exemptions** to the existing August 26 temporary Schedule I order for MGPI, MGM-15 and MGM-16. Below-threshold does not mean safe.

## When could temporary federal 7-OH scheduling take effect?

**Not automatically on October 14, 2026.** That is a scheduled notice date; any actual temporary control requires a later order after the applicable notice period. No final effective date is confirmed as of October 12 (DOJ, October 9, 2026).

## Are products below the proposed federal thresholds proven safe?

**No. Proposed regulatory concentration and ratio thresholds are not clinical safety thresholds.** HHS described them as a way to distinguish product compositions, not as evidence of safety (HHS, October 9, 2026).

## What is the 100-to-1 ratio rule?

**It is a proposed composition-based legal trigger, not a toxicological safety marker.** The ratio measures mitragynine mass relative to 7-OH, MGPI or their combined mass; a low ratio alone does not prove how a product was manufactured.

## What's the difference between 7-OH, MGPI, MGM-15, and MGM-16?

| Compound | What it is | Federal status (Oct 12, 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| **7-OH** | Potent metabolite; also sold concentrated | Pending temporary scheduling (notice scheduled Oct 14) |
| **Mitragynine pseudoindoxyl (MGPI)** | Lab-derived related compound | Temporary Schedule I since **Aug 26, 2026**; future threshold treatment depends on issuance of a new order |
| **MGM-15 / MGM-16** | Semi-synthetic derivatives | Temporary Schedule I since **Aug 26, 2026** |

## Does 7-OH have an FDA-approved medical use?

**FDA has not approved concentrated 7-OH as a medication for pain, addiction or any other indication.** A product being commercially available does not establish approval (FDA, *FDA and Kratom*).

## Do randomized human trials prove that 7-OH treats pain?

**No published randomized human efficacy trial establishing isolated 7-OH as a pain treatment was identified among these cited studies.** Receptor experiments and animal antinociception do not establish patient benefit (Kruegel 2019; Obeng 2021).

## What does the FDA say about 7-OH?

**The FDA has positioned concentrated 7-OH products as a public-health concern**: it issued seven warning letters to 7-OH marketers and distributors in June–July 2025, published a "Hiding in Plain Sight" consumer warning about 7-OH products, and in July 2025 recommended scheduling concentrated 7-OH under the Controlled Substances Act. None of this is an approval or a safety finding — it's enforcement and warning activity.

## What do poison centers and medical toxicologists say?

**The American College of Medical Toxicology issued a 2026 position statement on public-health concerns around 7-OH and related kratom compounds**, and the 2026 clinical case literature (dependence, withdrawal, buprenorphine treatment) is accumulating in addiction-medicine and emergency-medicine journals. The signal direction is consistent: concentrated 7-OH behaves like the opioid it pharmacologically is.

**The bigger picture**

## Does concentrated 7-OH treat opioid withdrawal?

**There are no controlled human efficacy data establishing concentrated 7-OH as an opioid-withdrawal treatment.** Clinical reports describing treatment of *7-OH dependence* with prescribed medications are a different question (PMIDs 41875249, 42225057).

## Why is 7-OH controversial?

**Because it sits at the collision of three unresolved fights:** (1) whether it should be treated as a dangerous unregulated opioid or a potential harm-reduction alternative; (2) whether regulation can surgically target concentrates without sweeping up botanical kratom; and (3) how much of the current evidence — mostly case reports, product analyses, and animal work — justifies emergency scheduling. The October 2026 **proposed** threshold framework is an attempt to distinguish product types. Whether it works is now an empirical question.

### 7-OH vs. mitragynine vs. kratom leaf: the one-paragraph version

**Kratom leaf is a mitragynine-dominant botanical with the largest (still limited) human evidence base. Mitragynine is its major alkaloid, a partial opioid agonist your liver partly converts to 7-OH. 7-OH is the potent metabolite — trace in leaf, manufactured at high dose in tablets — with demonstrated respiratory depression in animals, documented dependence and withdrawal in humans, and a pending federal notice process proposing concentration and ratio criteria.** Anyone who uses one of these words to mean the others is selling something — a product, a panic, or a policy.
