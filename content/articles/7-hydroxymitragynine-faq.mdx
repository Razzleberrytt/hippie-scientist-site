## Identity: what 7-OH is and isn't

## What is 7-hydroxymitragynine?

**7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) is an opioid-active alkaloid (CAS 174418-82-7) that exists in two completely different contexts: as a minor metabolite your body makes from mitragynine, and as the active ingredient in concentrated commercial products.**

Those two product contexts involve very different exposures. The pharmacology and toxicology must be considered separately, and the October federal proposals specifically seek to distinguish trace from enhanced 7-OH and MGPI content.

## Is 7-OH the same as kratom?

**No. Kratom is a botanical mixture dominated by mitragynine; 7-OH products are concentrated preparations built around 7-hydroxymitragynine.** Treating them as the same thing is the single most common error in news coverage and online discussion — and it runs in both directions (alarmists citing 7-OH harms against leaf; sellers implying 7-OH tablets are "just kratom").

## Is 7-OH the same as mitragynine?

**No. Mitragynine is the parent alkaloid; 7-OH is its more potent opioid-active metabolite.** They differ in experimental receptor potency and disposition. Mitragynine itself is not federally scheduled, while 7-OH is the subject of a pending temporary-scheduling proposal as of October 12, 2026.

## Is 7-OH natural?

**It occurs naturally in kratom at trace levels and forms in the human body from mitragynine — but concentrated commercial tablets can deliver exposures unlike botanical leaf; labeling does not establish complete chemical identity.** "Natural" describes origin, not dose. The HHS October 2026 framework explicitly distinguishes natural botanical kratom (trace 7-OH) from enhanced products.

## Is 7-OH the same compound as mitragynine pseudoindoxyl?

**No.** 7-OH and MGPI have different chemical identities. MGPI was placed in temporary Schedule I on August 26, 2026, while 7-OH was not subject to a final temporary order as of October 12 (DOJ, October 9, 2026).

## How is 7-OH different from mitragynine?

| Feature | Mitragynine | 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) |
|---|---|---|
| Role | Parent alkaloid, most abundant in leaf | Metabolite of mitragynine; also sold directly in concentrates |
| Mu-opioid activity | Partial/weak agonist in most assays | More potent MOR agonist (Kruegel et al., 2019; Obeng et al., 2021) |
| Natural leaf levels | Dominant alkaloid | Trace |
| Commercial products | Leaf powder, teas, extracts | Tablets, shots, and concentrates built around 7-OH |
| Federal status (Oct 12, 2026) | Not federally scheduled | Proposed temporary-scheduling notice process; no final 7-OH order yet |

## How does the body make 7-OH from mitragynine?

**The liver converts part of each mitragynine dose into 7-OH, primarily via the CYP3A4 enzyme.** Human proof: when volunteers took the CYP3A4 inhibitor itraconazole, 7-OH exposure fell while mitragynine exposure rose (PMID 38481700). Human liver-preparation work identified CYP3A isoforms in the conversion pathway (PMID 31263758). This means CYP3A4 inhibitors, inducers, liver function, and genetics can all shift how much 7-OH a given mitragynine dose produces.

## Potency and pharmacology

## Is 7-OH an opioid?

**Yes, by the definition that matters: it activates the mu-opioid receptor, the same receptor family as morphine and oxycodone, and produces opioid-like effects including analgesia, respiratory depression (in animals), dependence, and withdrawal.** Its full pharmacology differs from classical opioids in the details, but "not a classical opioid" is not "not an opioid."

## Is 7-OH always stronger than morphine?

**There is no single established human potency multiplier.** Rat respiratory findings, antinociceptive experiments and in-vitro receptor assays assess different endpoints, so their relative-potency figures cannot be converted into consumer-dose comparisons (Kruegel et al., 2019; Obeng et al., 2021; PMID 41106041).

## How potent is 7-hydroxymitragynine?

**7-OH is substantially more potent at the mu-opioid receptor than its parent mitragynine — potent enough that Kruegel et al. (2019) identified it as the key mediator of mitragynine's analgesic effects in mice, and Kamble et al. (2020) showed human plasma metabolism increases its opioid potency and efficacy.** Precise cross-drug potency ratios (e.g., "X times morphine") depend on the assay, species, and endpoint — be skeptical of any single number quoted without those qualifiers.

## Why does potency matter more for 7-OH products than for leaf?

**Because exposure, formulation, and co-exposure determine risk, rather than the marketing category alone.** Leaf contains 7-OH at trace levels; the body makes modest additional amounts from mitragynine. Concentrated 7-OH tablets deliver the potent metabolite directly, at levels leaf never approaches. Same molecule, radically different exposure — which is exactly why the October 2026 federal framework regulates by concentration, not by the mere presence of the compound.

## Overdose and breathing risk

## Can 7-OH slow or stop breathing?

**Yes. In a controlled 2025 rat study, 7-OH produced dose-related respiratory depression that was reversed by naloxone (Zuarth Gonzalez et al., PMID 41106041).** Opioid-like respiratory depression is the mechanism behind fatal opioid overdose generally, and 7-OH has now demonstrated it experimentally.

## Is a lethal human dose of 7-OH known?

**No reliable lethal-dose threshold has been established for humans.** Rat respiratory depression and clinical toxicology observations do not permit calculation of a safe or lethal human amount (PMID 41106041).

## Can 7-OH cause a fatal overdose?

**No human lethal dose is established, but the mechanism for fatal overdose — opioid-like respiratory depression — is demonstrated, and the risk climbs with dose and with co-use of other depressants (alcohol, benzodiazepines, opioids).** Absence of a published lethal dose is not evidence of safety; it reflects how new concentrated 7-OH products are.

## Does naloxone (Narcan) reverse 7-OH overdose?

**The animal evidence says yes: 7-OH-induced respiratory depression in rats was naloxone-reversible (PMID 41106041).** That makes naloxone the rational field response to suspected 7-OH overdose with depressed breathing — the same as for any opioid-like toxicity. Human reversal data specific to 7-OH are limited, which is expected for an emerging product category, not a reason to withhold naloxone.

## What should someone do if a person appears to have overdosed on 7-OH?

**Call emergency services, give naloxone if available, and follow dispatcher breathing-support instructions.** Do not wait for a toxicology test. Naloxone reversed respiratory depression in a controlled rat study, but that does not define a human 7-OH antidote dose (PMID 41106041).

## What happens if you mix 7-OH with alcohol or benzodiazepines?

**You stack depressants on depressants.** Opioids, alcohol, and benzodiazepines all suppress breathing through overlapping and additive pathways. Polydrug exposure is the most important real-world multiplier of overdose risk for every opioid-like substance, and 7-OH is no exception.

## Dependence, withdrawal, and treatment

## Can 7-OH cause dependence?

**Yes. Repeated concentrated 7-OH use can produce physical dependence, craving, loss of control, and clinically diagnosed substance use disorder — documented in 2026 addiction-medicine case literature.**

## How long does concentrated 7-OH withdrawal last?

**No universal timeline is established.** Published cases report opioid-like withdrawal but are not sufficient to estimate typical onset or duration for all concentrated products (2026 clinical case series and reports).

## What does 7-OH withdrawal feel like?

**Published case reports describe an opioid-like withdrawal syndrome.** A 2026 case report detailed management of acute withdrawal after high-dose chronic 7-OH use (Lybik et al., PMID 41690384), and a separate 2026 case required inpatient medically managed withdrawal (Wightman & Hu, PMID 40758956). As with opioids generally, severity varies with dose, duration, and individual factors — and no universal timeline is established.

## Has buprenorphine been used for 7-OH use disorder?

**Yes, published clinical case reports and small observational series describe clinician-supervised use.** These do not yet establish an optimal protocol or efficacy from randomized 7-OH-specific treatment trials.

## Is there treatment for 7-OH use disorder?

**Yes — the same medications used for opioid use disorder are being applied.** A 2026 case report documented 7-OH use disorder treated with buprenorphine (Hendler et al., PMID 41875249), and a 2026 retrospective case series examined buprenorphine for 7-OH use (Fenske et al., PMID 42225057). These are early clinical reports, not trial evidence — but they establish that addiction medicine is treating 7-OH dependence within the existing OUD framework.

## Where can someone get help for 7-OH dependence?

**A primary-care clinician, an addiction-medicine service, or — in the US — the SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357.** 7-OH dependence is being managed as an opioid-related disorder; you don't need a 7-OH-specific program to get evidence-based help.

## Products: what's actually being sold

## What is in 7-OH products sold in gas stations and smoke shops?

**Tablets, shots, and concentrates marketed around 7-hydroxymitragynine content — frequently with labeling that doesn't match the contents.** A 2026 peer-reviewed study found elevated 7-OH levels in products misbranded as kratom (Brown et al., PMID 41065466), and a separate 2026 quantitative analysis documented 7-OH levels and stability across commercial kratom products (Avula et al., PMID 41825819).

## Can kratom extracts contain enhanced 7-OH rather than only leaf alkaloids?

**Yes.** Commercial analysis has identified products with enriched 7-OH and label-versus-content discrepancies; ordinary leaf evidence cannot stand in for enriched extract evidence (PMIDs 41825819 and 41065466).

## Are 7-OH products accurately labeled?

**Often not.** Beyond the misbranding findings above, the broader kratom-product literature has repeatedly documented label-vs-content mismatches and unreported alkaloids. A brand name, a COA, or a milligram number on the front of the package is not proof of what's inside — particularly in a product category that has existed largely outside consistent regulatory oversight.

## How do you read a 7-OH product label?

**Read the alkaloid name, not the marketing words.** The single most important line is which compound the milligrams refer to: *mitragynine* and *7-hydroxymitragynine* are distinct compounds; they also have different evidentiary and federal regulatory contexts, although no final temporary 7-OH scheduling order exists as of October 12, 2026. Terms like "nano," "enhanced," or "extract" describe processing — they don't change which alkaloid you're buying, and absorption claims attached to them are the manufacturer's own, not FDA-evaluated. If the label doesn't clearly name the alkaloid and amount, that's information in itself.

## How much 7-OH is in kratom leaf naturally?

**Trace levels — a small fraction of total alkaloid content, with mitragynine dominant.** This is the factual basis for the entire leaf-vs-concentrate distinction: the **proposed** HHS October 2026 concentration and ratio tests were intended to distinguish trace occurrences in botanical preparations from enriched products, not to define a safe dose.

## Drug testing

## Does a negative ordinary opiate screen rule out 7-OH exposure?

**No.** Standard urine opiate immunoassays mainly target morphine and codeine, and separate validated testing is required to identify compounds such as mitragynine and 7-OH. A negative common panel cannot rule out 7-OH exposure (American College of Medical Toxicology position statement, PMID 34780053).

## Do routine drug tests detect 7-OH?

**Typical opiate immunoassays do not reliably detect 7-OH.** A lab needs a validated, compound-specific test (such as a targeted chromatographic mass-spectrometry method) to identify it. Results depend on the analytes, method, detection limits, and sample timing (ACMT, PMID 34780053; Papsun et al., PMID 42135874).

## Can 7-OH be confused with other compounds in testing?

**Related alkaloids require validated compound-specific analytical methods.** Routine opioid screening does not establish which kratom-derived substance is present, and a product label alone does not provide forensic confirmation (ACMT, PMID 34780053; Papsun et al., PMID 42135874).

## Legal status — existing controls versus October 2026 proposals

> **As of October 12, 2026:** DOJ announced revised scheduling notices on October 9, but a notice of intent does not itself schedule a drug. Publication of a revised Federal Register notice was expected October 14; its final wording and any later DEA orders require rechecking before this article can be published.

## Is 7-OH legal in every U.S. state?

**Not necessarily.** Independent state and local laws can prohibit or restrict products even before a substance is federally scheduled. Check the current official rules for the relevant jurisdiction.

## Is 7-OH legal?

**No single answer covers every U.S. jurisdiction.** There is no final federal temporary Schedule I order for 7-OH as of October 12, 2026; other federal regulatory requirements and independent state or local restrictions can still apply. See the [full 7-OH monograph](/articles/7-hydroxymitragynine/) for evolving safety context. (DOJ, October 9, 2026.)

## Is October 14, 2026 the effective date of a federal 7-OH ban?

**No.** October 14 was an anticipated Federal Register notice-publication date, not an effective date. A subsequent separate temporary order would be required (DOJ, October 9, 2026).

## Is 7-OH federally Schedule I?

**Not under a final DEA temporary scheduling order as of October 12, 2026.** DEA announced a new notice of intent for 7-OH and MGPI; it would still need a separate subsequent temporary order. The earlier July 7-OH proceeding had not resulted in an effective scheduling order. Do not confuse an anticipated October 14 notice with the effective date of a control. (DOJ, October 9, 2026.)

## Are 7-OH products below the proposed thresholds proven safe?

**No.** Regulatory concentration and ratio cutoffs are not human safety levels and remain proposed as of October 12, 2026 (HHS, October 9, 2026).

## What are the federal 7-OH thresholds?

**HHS has recommended two proposed triggers, but they are not yet active legal exemptions or a final scheduling order.** For 7-OH and MGPI individually or combined when both are present: concentration **exceeding** 1 mg/g in solids or 1 mg/mL in liquids, **or** a **mass ratio** of mitragynine to 7-OH, MGPI or their combined mass **below 100:1**. These are proposed scheduling criteria, not safe-use thresholds. The existing August 26 Schedule I order for MGPI remains in effect. (HHS and DOJ, October 9, 2026.)

## What is the 100-to-1 ratio rule?

**It is a proposed product-composition test, not a diagnostic test for product safety or manufacturing method.** A mitragynine-to-7-OH/MGPI mass ratio below 100:1 would trigger the proposed approach even when concentration does not exceed the other proposed threshold. The exact future regulatory language must be confirmed in the Federal Register. (HHS, October 9, 2026.)

## What's the difference between 7-OH, MGPI, MGM-15, and MGM-16?

| Compound | Scientific distinction | Federal status as of October 12, 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| 7-OH | Opioid-active mitragynine metabolite; also sold as enriched formulations | No final federal temporary scheduling order; revised notice expected |
| MGPI | Distinct mitragynine pseudoindoxyl compound | **Temporarily Schedule I since August 26**; proposed future thresholds do not yet exempt products |
| MGM-15 / MGM-16 | Distinct semisynthetic opioid-active derivatives | **Temporarily Schedule I since August 26** |

The Department of Justice expressly states the three existing orders remain effective while the revised notice process proceeds (DOJ, October 9, 2026).

## Does the FDA approve concentrated 7-OH as a pain medicine?

**No FDA-approved indication for isolated concentrated 7-OH is established by the cited evidence.** Marketing, animal analgesia findings and human case reports do not amount to FDA approval.

## What does the FDA say about 7-OH?

**The FDA warns about concentrated 7-OH products and has taken enforcement actions; this does not amount to an approved medical indication.** Its warnings and public-health communications are distinct from DEA scheduling orders and should not be treated as clinical efficacy trials.

## What do poison centers and medical toxicologists say?

**Human poison-center reports and case literature raise concerns about intoxication, dependence, and withdrawal, but many reports do not establish a dose-response curve or isolate 7-OH from other exposures.** These observations complement, rather than replace, the rat respiratory-depression and naloxone experiments (PMID 41106041).

## The bigger picture

## Do human randomized trials establish 7-OH as a treatment for pain?

**No controlled human efficacy evidence cited here establishes isolated 7-OH as a proven pain treatment.** Preclinical analgesia outcomes should not be described as demonstrated human efficacy (Kruegel et al., 2019).

## Can a product label prove that a 7-OH tablet contains only one alkaloid?

**No.** Product labeling and isolated batch testing cannot independently prove complete composition; documented analytical discrepancies warrant caution (PMIDs 41825819 and 41065466).

## Why is 7-OH controversial?

**Because it sits at the collision of three unresolved fights:** (1) whether it should be treated as a dangerous unregulated opioid or a potential harm-reduction alternative; (2) whether regulation can surgically target concentrates without sweeping up botanical kratom; and (3) how much of the current evidence — mostly case reports, product analyses, and animal work — justifies emergency scheduling. The October 2026 proposed thresholds aim to distinguish trace botanical amounts from enhanced formulations; their effectiveness remains untested. Whether it works is now an empirical question.

### 7-OH vs. mitragynine vs. kratom leaf: the one-paragraph version

**Kratom leaf is a mitragynine-dominant botanical with the largest (still limited) human evidence base. Mitragynine is its major alkaloid, a partial opioid agonist your liver partly converts to 7-OH. 7-OH is the potent metabolite — trace in leaf, manufactured at high dose in tablets — with demonstrated respiratory depression in animals, documented dependence and withdrawal in humans, and a pending notice process that may result in future scheduling, not an order already in effect.** Each category requires its own evidence and regulatory interpretation.
