# Buzzers Cat’s Claw Tablets: What Testing Found, Hidden Opioids & Overdose Risk

> **Safety alert:** Utah public-health authorities have specifically warned consumers not to use Buzzers-branded Cat’s Claw tablets after tested products were found to contain opioid-acting kratom derivatives. If someone cannot be awakened, has slow or abnormal breathing, blue or gray lips, or collapses after using one of these products, call 911 and administer naloxone if available.

## Quick answer

In July 2026, the **Utah Department of Agriculture and Food (UDAF)** and the **Utah Poison Control Center (UPCC)** reported that Buzzers-branded Cat’s Claw tablets involved in an investigation **contained mitragynine pseudoindoxyl and MGM-15**.

Those are not ordinary Cat’s Claw constituents. They are potent opioid-acting compounds related to the rapidly changing kratom-derivative market.

The official warning was issued in connection with **a recent death under investigation and several severe health incidents**. Utah authorities advised the public not to buy or consume Buzzers Cat’s Claw tablets and asked retailers to remove them.

That finding is much stronger evidence than online speculation about what the tablets “feel like.” But it still needs an important qualifier: **testing of identified products does not prove that every batch, flavor, package, or similarly labeled Cat’s Claw product has identical contents.**

## What did Utah actually find?

On July 30, 2026, UDAF said its investigation of Buzzers-branded Cat’s Claw tablets identified:

- **MGM-15 (dihydro-7-hydroxymitragynine)**
- **mitragynine pseudoindoxyl**

Both compounds act at opioid receptors and are chemically distinct from the ordinary botanical constituents expected in genuine *Uncaria tomentosa* Cat’s Claw supplements.

The same announcement stated that multiple flavors were involved in overdose cases and that a death and several severe health incidents were being investigated.

This is a rare case where a vague “proprietary botanical extract” concern progressed to a concrete government laboratory finding.

## Why the Cat’s Claw label matters

Genuine Cat’s Claw is a South American vine, commonly sold as *Uncaria tomentosa*. It contains oxindole alkaloids and has been investigated primarily for anti-inflammatory and immunologic effects.

It is **not an established opioid product** and is not expected to mimic the acute effects of concentrated 7-OH or semi-synthetic kratom derivatives.

That means the phrase “Cat’s Claw extract” can create a serious category error when it appears on a product whose pharmacology comes from undisclosed opioid-active compounds.

A consumer may reasonably assume the product is comparable to a conventional herbal supplement when its actual risk profile can be very different.

## Why a “no kratom” or “no 7-OH” claim may not answer the real question

A laboratory result is only as broad as the panel that was run.

For example, a certificate can truthfully report:

- no mitragynine detected;
- no 7-hydroxymitragynine detected;
- no traditional kratom alkaloids detected;

while still providing **no information at all** about MGM-15 or mitragynine pseudoindoxyl if those analytes were not part of the test.

This is one reason “ND” or “non-detect” should never be interpreted without reading the analyte list.

**Non-detect means the named substance was not detected by that method at that reporting threshold. It does not mean “nothing psychoactive is present.”**

## MGM-15 is not ordinary kratom leaf

MGM-15 is a semi-synthetic derivative related to 7-hydroxymitragynine. Controlled human studies have not established a safe dose, pharmacokinetic profile, overdose threshold, or dependence timeline.

See the dedicated [MGM-15 evidence review](/articles/dihydro-7-hydroxy-mitragynine-mgm-15/) for the distinction between animal/receptor data and human evidence.

## Mitragynine pseudoindoxyl is also a distinct opioid-active compound

Mitragynine pseudoindoxyl is a rearranged mitragynine/7-OH-related compound with strong preclinical opioid pharmacology and emerging human withdrawal evidence.

It should not be treated as interchangeable with mitragynine, conventional kratom leaf, or 7-OH.

See [Mitragynine pseudoindoxyl: evidence, withdrawal, product market and federal status](/articles/mitragynine-pseudoindoxyl/).

## What symptoms triggered the poison-center concern?

Utah Poison Control warned about **life-threatening opioid overdose, dependence, and withdrawal** associated with adulterated Cat’s Claw and Kava products.

With potent opioid agonists, emergency warning signs include:

- inability to stay awake or be awakened;
- very slow, shallow, irregular, or stopped breathing;
- blue or gray lips or fingertips;
- severe confusion or collapse;
- pinpoint pupils in the context of profound sedation;
- choking, snoring, or gurgling in an unresponsive person.

Those symptoms warrant emergency care regardless of what the package says.

## What about naloxone?

When an undisclosed opioid is plausible, naloxone is appropriate emergency harm-reduction equipment.

Naloxone can reverse opioid effects, but:

- repeated doses can sometimes be needed;
- the person can become sedated again after naloxone wears off;
- an unknown product may contain additional non-opioid drugs;
- successful awakening does not replace medical evaluation after a serious overdose.

Call 911 when someone is unconscious or breathing abnormally.

## Are all Buzzers products proven to contain MGM-15 and pseudoindoxyl?

No.

The careful statement is narrower:

**Utah authorities reported finding MGM-15 and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl in tested Buzzers-branded Cat’s Claw tablets associated with their 2026 investigation.**

Product composition can change over time, between lots, and across formats. A later package should not automatically be assumed identical to a tested package—and it should not be assumed safe merely because the label or a partial certificate looks different.

## What about Reddit reports?

Reddit discussions became an early-warning signal because people were describing opioid-like effects, tolerance, and withdrawal from products labeled Cat’s Claw months before many official alerts were widely known.

Those anecdotes are useful for **finding a question worth investigating**. They are poor evidence for identifying a molecule.

Users cannot reliably determine whether a product contains MGM-15, pseudoindoxyl, a fentanyl analogue, tianeptine, a different novel opioid, or a mixture based on subjective effects alone.

The Utah laboratory finding is therefore much more important than any “this feels like X” post.

## Bottom line

Buzzers is no longer merely an internet rumor about a suspicious herbal extract.

In 2026, Utah authorities reported that tested Buzzers Cat’s Claw tablets contained **MGM-15 and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl**, and the products were investigated in connection with severe health events and a death.

The evidence does **not** justify claiming that every Buzzers product or every Cat’s Claw product contains the same substances.

It does justify treating mislabeled smoke-shop Cat’s Claw products as a serious product-identity and opioid-overdose problem rather than as ordinary herbal supplements.
