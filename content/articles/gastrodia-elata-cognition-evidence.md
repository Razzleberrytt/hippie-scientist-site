> **Bottom line:** Gastrodia elata, also called **Tianma**, finally has a modern placebo-controlled human cognition trial substantial enough to take seriously. The 2025 study was positive on its primary cognitive outcome, but it remains **one preparation-specific trial**. That makes the herb promising—not proven as a general memory or dementia supplement.

## Why Gastrodia elata is an interesting research herb

Gastrodia elata is an orchid used in East Asian medicine and studied for neurological effects ranging from dizziness and headache traditions to modern cognition and neuroprotection research.

The laboratory literature is much larger than the clinical literature. Animal and cell studies have explored antioxidant, inflammatory, neurotransmitter, and neurotrophic pathways.

That imbalance matters. Herbs can accumulate hundreds of mechanistic papers while still having very little evidence showing that people actually experience a meaningful benefit.

For cognition, a 2025 randomized trial changed the conversation because it supplied a more direct human test.[1]

## The 2025 trial enrolled 100 adults

The study was a 12-week randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial conducted in adults aged **40 to 65** with normal or mildly impaired cognition.[1]

One hundred participants were randomized 1:1 to Gastrodia elata extract tablets or placebo. Ninety-six completed the trial, and the full-analysis set included 98 participants.

The primary outcome was the Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale-cognitive subscale, or ADAS-cog.

That is a much more useful design than an uncontrolled supplement survey or before-and-after case series.

## The primary cognitive outcome favored Gastrodia

After 12 weeks, the change in total ADAS-cog score differed significantly between groups.[1]

The treatment group improved by about 2.15 points from baseline versus about 0.92 points in the placebo group.

The study also reported a significant between-group difference in a word-recall subscore.

Because the primary outcome was positive, this trial deserves more weight than studies that only find scattered secondary signals after a null primary result.

It still needs replication.

## Secondary outcomes were mixed

The Gastrodia group also showed a statistically significant between-group difference in total K-MMSE change, along with changes in BDNF and total antioxidant status.[1]

Amyloid-beta did **not** significantly differ between groups.

That mixed biomarker picture is useful. It prevents a simplistic mechanism story in which every Alzheimer's-related pathway is assumed to improve because one cognitive score changed.

Clinical outcome data and biomarkers should be reported separately.

## The study population limits generalization

Participants were middle-aged adults with K-MMSE scores in a specified range, not people with established Alzheimer's dementia.

That means the trial does not prove that Gastrodia treats Alzheimer's disease, prevents dementia, or improves cognition in healthy young adults.

Population boundaries matter as much as statistical significance.

A result in a carefully selected 40-to-65-year-old sample should remain attached to that population until other trials broaden the evidence.

## The tested extract matters

The intervention was a defined Gastrodia elata extract tablet used in a Korean clinical research setting.[1]

Herbal products can differ substantially in species authentication, growing conditions, processing, extraction, and constituent profile.

A positive result for one product should not automatically be transferred to every Tianma powder, tea, tincture, or multi-herb formula sold online.

This is one of the recurring reasons botanical evidence gets overstated.

## Safety was reassuring but short term

No serious adverse events were reported during the 12-week study.[1] One participant in the active group discontinued because of abdominal discomfort.

That provides some short-term preparation-specific safety information.

It does not establish rare-event risk, long-term safety, pregnancy safety, or comprehensive medication-interaction safety.

## What the preclinical literature can and cannot add

Animal work has explored Gastrodia extracts in models of stress, cognitive impairment, inflammation, and microbiome change.[2]

Those studies can help prioritize mechanisms for future human research.

They should not be stacked beside the human trial as if ten mouse experiments equal ten clinical replications. They answer different questions.

For a reader deciding how confident to be, the single human randomized trial is the anchor.

## What would strengthen the evidence

The next useful studies would include:

- independent replication by a separate research group;
- trials in clearly diagnosed mild cognitive impairment;
- longer follow-up;
- standardized chemical characterization of the tested extract;
- preregistered primary outcomes;
- head-to-head or dose-ranging work;
- confirmation that any cognitive change is durable after treatment stops.

If those studies reproduce the 2025 result, Gastrodia could become one of the more interesting evidence-based cognition herbs.

## Bottom line

Gastrodia elata now has a **real human signal worth watching**.

A randomized trial of 100 adults reported improvement on its primary cognitive measure, which is considerably stronger than mechanism-only supplement hype.

But one trial is still one trial. The evidence grade should remain preliminary until the result is independently replicated and shown to generalize beyond the specific extract and study population.
