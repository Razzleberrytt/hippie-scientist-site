# Research-Chemical Psychedelics: 4-HO-MET, 4-AcO-DMT, 1P-LSD, 2C-B-FLY and the Evidence Gaps

## Quick answer

Research-chemical psychedelics include dozens of compounds from several chemical families.

Common discussion names include **4-HO-MET, 4-AcO-DMT, 4-HO-DET, 5-MeO-MiPT, 1P-LSD, 1cP-LSD, AL-LAD, 2C-B-FLY and related tryptamines, lysergamides and phenethylamines.**

Many likely produce psychedelic effects substantially through serotonin 5-HT2A receptor signaling, but the human evidence base varies from small experimental literatures to little more than toxicology reports.

## Three very different families often get lumped together

### Tryptamines

Examples include 4-HO-MET and 4-AcO-DMT.

They may be discussed as “mushroom-like,” but that shorthand should not be mistaken for proof of identical pharmacokinetics, metabolism or psychological risk.

### Lysergamides

Examples include 1P-LSD and 1cP-LSD.

Some lysergamides may act partly as prodrugs or close analogues of LSD, but product identity and legal status can change rapidly and should be checked separately.

### Phenethylamines

Examples include 2C-B-FLY and NBOH/NBOMe-related compounds.

This category contains substances with markedly different potency and toxicity, making visual similarity of blotters or powders especially unhelpful.

## 4-HO-MET as a case study

4-HO-MET, also called metocin, is a synthetic tryptamine that has been identified in forensic drug material and in a published clinical toxicology case.

That gives us evidence that real human exposure occurs.

It does **not** give us a large controlled trial literature defining safe exposure, interaction risks, or the probability of severe psychological reactions.

## Why “similar to mushrooms” can mislead

A compound may share structural features with psilocin while differing in:

- absorption;
- metabolic pathways;
- active metabolites;
- duration;
- receptor profile;
- potency;
- available human evidence.

The phrase can be useful as a rough orientation but should not become a medical equivalence claim.

## Psychological toxicity is still toxicity

Serious adverse events from psychedelics can include:

- panic and severe anxiety;
- confusion;
- dangerous behavior;
- prolonged agitation;
- psychosis-like symptoms;
- accidental injury;
- worsening of underlying psychiatric vulnerability.

A person does not need to develop organ failure for an intoxication to become medically dangerous.

## Mislabeling and compound substitution

Blotters, pellets and small powder quantities can be visually indistinguishable across compounds.

That matters because duration and potency can differ dramatically.

A user expecting a familiar lysergamide but receiving a potent phenethylamine analogue may make decisions based on the wrong timeline and risk profile.

## Serotonergic combinations

Many psychedelic RCs interact with serotonin systems.

Combining serotonergic substances can increase unpredictability and, for some combinations, the risk of serotonin toxicity. Specific interaction evidence is sparse for many newer analogues, so absence of a published case report is not evidence that a combination is safe.

## Bottom line

RC psychedelics are a broad chemical neighborhood, not a single risk category.

The most defensible approach is to identify the **exact compound**, separate controlled human evidence from analogy, and assume that a vendor or forum label may be wrong until analytical testing says otherwise.
