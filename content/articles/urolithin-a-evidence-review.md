> **Bottom line:** Urolithin A has a real human research base, but the evidence is much narrower than the phrase “anti-aging supplement” suggests. Randomized trials support biological activity and show some signals for muscle endurance, strength, and mitochondrial-related biomarkers. However, the newest 2026 meta-analysis found the pooled six-minute-walk result statistically inconclusive and rated that evidence low certainty. Urolithin A is therefore best described as **promising but not established as a broad anti-aging or longevity intervention**. [1]

## What urolithin A actually is

Urolithin A is a metabolite related to dietary ellagitannins and ellagic acid, compounds found in foods such as pomegranate and some berries and nuts. Human gut microbes can convert these dietary precursors into urolithins, but people differ in whether and how efficiently they produce urolithin A.

That distinction matters. Evidence from a trial of purified urolithin A cannot automatically be transferred to pomegranate juice, an ellagic-acid supplement, or another product that merely contains a precursor. Likewise, evidence about a food should not be rewritten as if participants received isolated urolithin A.

Mechanistically, urolithin A is studied largely because of **mitophagy**—the cellular process that removes damaged mitochondria. That mechanism is scientifically interesting, but a change in a mitochondrial biomarker is not the same thing as a demonstrated improvement in lifespan, disability, or disease risk.

## What the newest meta-analysis found

A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis collected randomized controlled trials of oral urolithin A and muscle-related outcomes. The authors could quantitatively pool only the six-minute walk test; other endpoints were too heterogeneous and were synthesized narratively. [1]

For the pooled six-minute-walk result, the estimated improvement was not statistically conclusive. The review rated the certainty of that evidence as **low**. That is an important counterweight to stronger marketing language built around individual favorable secondary outcomes.

This does not mean every trial was negative. It means the total evidence base is still too small and heterogeneous to support a confident claim that urolithin A reliably improves general physical function.

## The older-adult randomized trial: endurance signal, primary endpoints mixed

A 2022 randomized clinical trial enrolled 66 adults aged 65 to 90 and compared urolithin A with placebo for four months. [3]

The study reported improvement in measures of muscle endurance. But the two primary endpoints—six-minute walk distance and maximal skeletal-muscle ATP production—did not both show the kind of clear between-group improvement that would justify a simple “it improves mitochondrial function and walking ability” summary.

This distinction is central to reading supplement trials well:

- a positive secondary endpoint can be meaningful;
- a null primary endpoint still matters;
- biomarker changes do not automatically equal a clinical benefit;
- a four-month trial cannot establish long-term healthy-aging or longevity effects.

## The middle-aged trial: strength improved, primary peak-power endpoint did not

Another 2022 randomized placebo-controlled trial studied untrained, overweight, middle-aged adults for four months. [4]

The investigators reported improvements in muscle-strength measures and several mitochondrial or inflammatory biomarkers. Yet the study's primary endpoint, peak power output, did **not** significantly improve.

The trial also has an important provenance issue: multiple authors were employees, board members, or scientific advisers of the study sponsor, Amazentis, which developed the branded urolithin A ingredient. That does not invalidate the data, but it should remain visible when interpreting a small commercial evidence base.

## First-in-human work established bioavailability and short-term biological activity

The 2019 first-in-human trial was designed primarily around safety, bioavailability, and molecular responses rather than proving a clinical anti-aging effect. [5]

Urolithin A was detectable in plasma, and repeated administration altered acylcarnitines and skeletal-muscle mitochondrial gene-expression signals. Those findings helped establish that oral urolithin A can reach humans systemically and produce measurable biological effects.

They do **not** establish that the compound extends human lifespan, prevents frailty, prevents dementia, or substitutes for exercise.

## What the 2024 systematic review adds

A 2024 systematic review identified five human studies totaling 250 healthy participants. [2] Across those studies, the authors found signals involving inflammatory markers, mitochondrial-related gene expression, muscle strength, and endurance.

At the same time, several important outcomes did not consistently improve, including measures of mitochondrial ATP production, anthropometrics, cardiovascular outcomes, and broad physical function.

That mixed pattern is more informative than labeling the evidence simply “positive” or “negative.” Urolithin A appears biologically active, but the clinically important outcome picture is still developing.

## Does urolithin A improve mitochondria?

**It affects mitochondrial-related biology in human studies.** That is a narrower and better-supported statement than saying it “rejuvenates mitochondria.”

Human trials have reported changes in mitochondrial gene expression, acylcarnitines, mitophagy-related proteins, and related biomarkers. [2,4,5]

What has not been established is that these molecular changes reliably translate into major long-term outcomes such as fewer mobility limitations, less disability, lower disease incidence, or longer life.

A biomarker should be treated as an intermediate measurement unless a clinical outcome has been demonstrated.

## Is urolithin A an anti-aging supplement?

That phrase is currently too broad.

Human studies are short compared with the timescale of aging. They mostly involve relatively small groups and focus on muscle performance, mitochondrial biomarkers, or related physiology. There is no human trial showing that urolithin A extends lifespan.

A more defensible description is:

> Urolithin A is a microbiome-related metabolite with early-to-moderate human evidence for selected muscle and mitochondrial outcomes, but **no established broad anti-aging or longevity effect**.

## Safety: what the trials can and cannot tell us

Across the published trials summarized in recent reviews, urolithin A was generally tolerated over the studied periods, and adverse events did not produce a strong short-term safety signal. [1,2,3,5]

That is useful, but it is not equivalent to proving long-term safety for every population. Existing trials are too small and too short to rule out rare adverse effects or define safety in pregnancy, severe chronic disease, complex medication use, or years of continuous exposure.

The research doses reported in these trials describe **study conditions**, not a personalized dosing recommendation.

## Evidence-quality issues worth keeping visible

Several features limit certainty:

- the randomized evidence base is still small;
- trials use different populations and outcome measures;
- many outcomes are secondary or biomarker endpoints;
- some major trials have manufacturer involvement;
- study durations are measured in weeks or months, not years;
- “healthy aging” is a much broader claim than muscle endurance or mitochondrial biomarkers.

These limitations do not erase the positive signals. They define how far those signals can responsibly be generalized.

## Practical evidence verdict

| Question | Current answer |
|---|---|
| Is urolithin A orally bioavailable? | **Yes.** Human pharmacokinetic work supports systemic exposure. |
| Does it affect mitochondrial-related biomarkers? | **Yes, in several trials.** Clinical importance remains uncertain. |
| Does it improve muscle endurance or strength? | **Some trials show favorable signals.** Results vary by endpoint. |
| Does it reliably improve six-minute walk performance? | **Not established.** The 2026 pooled estimate was inconclusive and low certainty. |
| Does it extend human lifespan? | **No evidence establishes this.** |
| Is it proven to prevent age-related disease? | **No.** |
| Is short-term tolerability reasonably characterized? | **Yes, within the studied populations and durations.** |
| Is long-term safety fully established? | **No.** |
