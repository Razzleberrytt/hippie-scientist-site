> **Bottom line:** A frightening nighttime event is not one diagnosis. **Nightmares** are usually remembered REM dreams. **Sleep terrors and sleepwalking** are NREM disorders of arousal in which the brain is partly asleep and partly awake, often with confusion and limited recall. **REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD)** involves abnormal loss of REM muscle atonia so dream-related movements can become forceful or injurious. The stage, recall pattern, behavior and injury risk matter much more than simply calling everything a “bad dream.”[1-6]

## Why these disorders get confused

All three categories can involve dramatic nighttime behavior:

- screaming;
- fear;
- rapid heart rate;
- movement;
- disturbing imagery;
- confusion after waking; or
- reports from a bed partner that something alarming happened.

But those surface features can come from very different sleep states.

The most useful first question is:

> **Was the person experiencing a remembered dream, an incomplete NREM arousal, or actual REM dream enactment?**

## Quick comparison

| Feature | Nightmare | Sleep terror / NREM disorder of arousal | REM sleep behavior disorder |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typical sleep state | REM | NREM, often deeper N2/N3 / slow-wave sleep | REM |
| Dream recall | Usually good | Often absent or fragmentary | Often present and may match behavior |
| Large motor behavior | Not defining | Can include sitting, walking, running or complex behavior | Can include punching, kicking, grabbing or jumping from bed |
| Confusion after event | Usually limited once awake | Common | Often more alert after awakening |
| Main immediate risk | Distress / sleep avoidance | Falls, wandering, injury in complex cases | Injury to sleeper or bed partner |
| Diagnostic emphasis | Dream content + distress/impairment | Event history, triggers, differential diagnosis | REM without atonia + video PSG / clinical pattern |

This table is a guide, not a diagnostic test.

## Nightmares: distressing REM dreams with recall

Nightmares are vivid dysphoric dreams, often involving threat, fear or helplessness.[1]

People generally awaken sufficiently to remember the dream and can often describe it in detail.

The distress can become clinically important when nightmares are:

- frequent;
- repeatedly disruptive;
- causing fear of sleep;
- impairing daytime function; or
- occurring in a broader psychiatric or trauma-related context.

Nightmare disorder is therefore not simply “having a bad dream once.”[1]

## A nightmare does not require acting out the dream

This is an important distinction.

Someone can wake terrified from a nightmare without ever punching, kicking or leaving the bed.

The dream is the central event.

Large motor activity during REM raises a different question: **is normal REM muscle atonia being lost?**

That is the RBD pathway.

## Sleep terrors are NREM disorders of arousal

Sleep terrors occur during incomplete arousal from NREM sleep, commonly N3 or sometimes N2.[2]

An episode can involve:

- abrupt sitting up;
- screaming or crying;
- intense fear behavior;
- sweating or rapid heart rate;
- difficult-to-console behavior; and
- confusion or disorientation.[2]

Unlike a classic nightmare, there is often **little or no vivid dream recall** afterward.[2]

## Why sleep terrors can look more dramatic than the person's memory suggests

In a disorder of arousal, different parts of the brain can occupy different sleep-wake states at the same time.[2,5,6]

Motor and emotional systems may become active while systems responsible for full orientation, memory and reflective awareness remain partly asleep.

That can produce a striking mismatch:

- the bed partner sees obvious fear or complex behavior;
- the sleeper may remember almost nothing the next morning.

Recent research has complicated the older claim that NREM parasomnias are always completely unconscious: some people report fragmentary or even more elaborate mental experiences.[6]

The responsible conclusion is therefore **limited or variable recall**, not “NREM parasomnias never involve dream-like experience.”

## Sleepwalking belongs to the same NREM family

Sleepwalking, confusional arousals and sleep terrors are grouped as **disorders of arousal**.[5]

Behavior can range from simple to complex:

- sitting up;
- wandering;
- manipulating objects;
- eating;
- leaving the bedroom; or
- in severe adult cases, potentially dangerous behavior.[5]

This is why safety matters even when the person is not fully conscious of the episode.

## Adults can have sleep terrors and sleepwalking

NREM parasomnias are more common in children, and many improve with age.[2]

But they can persist into or occur in adulthood.[2,5]

Adult sleep-center populations may show:

- more complex motor behavior;
- sleep-related injury or violence;
- daytime sleepiness; and
- reduced quality of life.[5]

That does not mean every adult who once sleepwalked has a severe disorder. It means “that only happens to kids” is not an accurate rule.

## Sleep deprivation and fragmentation can prime NREM parasomnias

The 2024 sleep-terror review describes a combination of **homeostatic sleep pressure** and factors that fragment sleep as important contributors.[2]

That means episodes can become more likely when sleep is:

- chronically restricted;
- irregular;
- fragmented by another sleep disorder; or
- disrupted by environmental or behavioral factors.

This is another reason to ask about sleep apnea, restless legs, substances, medications and schedule instability rather than treating the event in isolation.

## REM sleep behavior disorder is a different mechanism

During normal REM sleep, most skeletal muscles are actively inhibited.

In RBD, that REM atonia is reduced or absent.[3,4]

The sleeper can then enact dream content through behaviors such as:

- punching;
- kicking;
- grabbing;
- shouting;
- leaping from bed; or
- striking a bed partner.

RBD is therefore not merely “a vivid nightmare with movement.”

It is a disorder involving abnormal REM motor control.

See [REM Sleep Behavior Disorder: Red Flags](/articles/rem-sleep-behavior-disorder-red-flags/).

## Why RBD has a different neurologic significance

Adult-onset isolated RBD has an important long-term association with synucleinopathies such as Parkinson disease and dementia with Lewy bodies.

That does **not** mean one episode of dream movement predicts Parkinson disease.

But it does mean repeated adult-onset forceful dream enactment deserves a different level of evaluation from an ordinary nightmare.[3,4]

## Sleep paralysis is also a REM parasomnia—but almost the opposite motor problem

Sleep paralysis occurs when REM muscle atonia persists briefly **after awareness returns**.

The person is awake but cannot move.

RBD involves too little REM atonia during REM sleep; sleep paralysis involves REM atonia lingering into wakefulness.

See [Sleep Paralysis](/articles/sleep-paralysis/).

## What about trauma-related nightmares and complex behavior?

Trauma can complicate the differential.

Some people have recurrent trauma-related nightmares. A newer proposed category called trauma-associated sleep disorder describes trauma-related dream content plus disruptive nocturnal behavior and autonomic activation.

That literature remains an evolving area and overlaps with PTSD and RBD-like presentations.

The existence of overlap is another reason not to diagnose a nighttime behavior from one video clip or one remembered dream.

## Video helps—but stage matters

A home video can be extremely useful for showing:

- movement complexity;
- vocalization;
- eyes open or closed;
- duration;
- interaction with the environment; and
- injury risk.

But a video cannot directly tell whether the person is in REM, N3 or another state.

When events are unusual, injurious or diagnostically ambiguous, video-polysomnography can help determine:

- sleep stage;
- REM muscle tone;
- respiratory events;
- limb movement patterns; and
- possible seizure-like activity.

## Seizures are an important mimic

Adult NREM disorders of arousal can resemble nocturnal epilepsy, particularly when events are stereotyped, abrupt or motorically complex.[5]

Features that increase concern for a seizure differential include highly stereotyped repeated episodes or patterns inconsistent with ordinary parasomnia.

This is one reason recurrent violent or unusual adult-onset sleep behavior deserves evaluation rather than automatic labeling as sleepwalking.

## Safety comes before perfect classification

If someone is:

- falling from bed;
- leaving the house;
- striking a partner;
- encountering stairs;
- handling dangerous objects; or
- experiencing other potentially injurious behavior,

reduce environmental hazards while the diagnosis is being clarified.

The exact label can take time. Injury prevention does not need to wait.

## What the evidence does not support

Current evidence does **not** justify claims that:

- every frightening dream is a night terror;
- every sleep terror is remembered as a vivid nightmare;
- sleepwalking happens only in children;
- dream enactment automatically means RBD;
- every RBD-like event predicts Parkinson disease;
- sleep paralysis and RBD are the same phenomenon;
- a home video alone establishes the sleep stage; or
- all adult nighttime motor events are parasomnias rather than possible seizures or sleep-disordered breathing.

## A practical decision tree

For a dramatic nighttime event, ask:

1. **Was there a vivid remembered dream?**
2. **Was the person confused and difficult to awaken?**
3. **Did the person walk or perform complex behavior?**
4. **Did movements clearly enact dream content?**
5. **Was there injury risk?**
6. **Are episodes new in adulthood, recurrent, stereotyped or violent?**
7. **Are snoring, apnea, medications, trauma, sleep deprivation or neurologic symptoms present?**

That pattern is much more useful than one umbrella label.

## Bottom line

Nightmares, NREM disorders of arousal and RBD can all produce frightening nights, but their biology is different.[1-6]

The simplest map is:

- **nightmare:** disturbing REM dream, usually remembered;
- **sleep terror/sleepwalking:** incomplete NREM arousal, often confused with limited recall;
- **RBD:** REM dream enactment because normal muscle atonia is lost.

The more violent, injurious, recurrent or adult-onset the behavior becomes, the stronger the case for formal sleep evaluation rather than self-labeling it from the internet.
