# Dicloqualone (SL-164): Methaqualone Analogue Evidence, Sedation & Safety Gaps

## Quick answer

**Dicloqualone, often called SL-164, is a quinazolinone sedative structurally related to methaqualone.**

CFSRE has confirmed it in U.S. drug material and describes pharmacology consistent with sedative GABAergic effects.

The major limitation is the human evidence. There is no mature modern clinical literature establishing a safe exposure, pharmacokinetics, dependence liability, interaction profile, or reliable potency comparison with methaqualone.

## Why people call it a Quaalude analogue

Methaqualone and dicloqualone belong to the quinazolinone chemical family.

That makes “analogue” a reasonable chemical description. It does not mean identical pharmacology.

Changing substituents on a sedative scaffold can alter receptor activity, metabolism, active metabolites, duration, and toxicity.

So “Quaalude analogue” should not be translated into “the same drug with a different legal status.”

## What U.S. forensic work found

CFSRE reported dicloqualone in a drug material submitted from Chicago.

Importantly, the sample also contained **fentanyl, heroin and diphenhydramine**.

That finding does not prove dicloqualone caused any particular clinical effect. It shows that the compound can exist in a **polysubstance illicit-drug environment**, where a user may be exposed to several depressants at once.

That context is more useful for safety than treating the compound as a laboratory-pure curiosity.

## What is known about pharmacology

Historical and preclinical evidence supports sedative activity involving GABAergic mechanisms.

Modern direct human pharmacokinetic data remain sparse. We do not have the level of controlled evidence needed to state a dependable half-life, safe range, or methaqualone-equivalent potency.

Anecdotal duration or strength estimates should therefore remain anecdotal.

## Why subjective effects cannot identify it

Many depressants can produce overlapping effects:

- drowsiness;
- disinhibition;
- slowed reaction time;
- memory impairment;
- poor balance;
- reduced consciousness.

A person cannot reliably determine that a powder was dicloqualone because it “felt like a Quaalude.”

Analytical chemistry is required to establish identity.

## Polysubstance depressant risk

The Chicago material illustrates the most important practical problem.

If a novel sedative appears alongside fentanyl or heroin, the person can experience both sedation and opioid respiratory depression.

Alcohol, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, sleep medications, sedating antihistamines, and other depressants can also increase impairment.

Naloxone can reverse an opioid component but does not reverse dicloqualone.

## Does dicloqualone cause dependence?

There is not enough modern human evidence to quantify dicloqualone-specific dependence or withdrawal.

Sedative GABAergic pharmacology creates a reason for caution, but evidence from benzodiazepines or methaqualone should not be copied over as though it were direct dicloqualone data.

This is an area where the site should say **unknown** rather than invent certainty.

## When exposure becomes an emergency

Call for urgent medical help when someone is difficult or impossible to wake, has slow or abnormal breathing, turns blue or gray, has a seizure, collapses, or has severe confusion with inability to protect their airway.

If opioid exposure is possible, give naloxone when available.

## Bottom line

Dicloqualone is a real, analytically confirmed RC sedative—not merely an internet legend.

But the human evidence is too thin to support the confident “modern Quaalude” framing often seen in community discussion.

The strongest current message is **confirmed market presence, plausible sedative pharmacology, serious polysubstance context, and substantial human-data uncertainty**.
