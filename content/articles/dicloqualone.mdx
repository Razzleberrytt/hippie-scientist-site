# Dicloqualone (SL-164): Methaqualone-Analogue Masterclass

> **Emergency safety:** Profound sedation, abnormal breathing, repeated vomiting while very drowsy, collapse, seizure-like activity, severe agitation/delirium, hypoxia, or inability to stay awake require urgent medical care. A product sold as a “Quaalude analogue” may contain another drug or mixture, so unexpected symptoms should not be dismissed as normal.

## Quick answer

**Dicloqualone, also called SL-164, is a 4-quinazolinone sedative structurally related to methaqualone.** It is not methaqualone itself.

The human evidence base is small but unusually informative: published case reports document **prolonged agitated delirium, myoclonus, tachycardia, hyperreflexia, hypoxia and altered mental status** after analytically confirmed or laboratory-linked SL-164 exposure.

That pattern is important because it contradicts the simple assumption that a methaqualone analogue will produce only calm sedation. Severe NPS intoxication can be mixed, paradoxical and neurologically complicated.

The compound also creates a laboratory challenge. In one investigation, high-resolution mass spectrometry initially detected unknown peaks with masses resembling other sedatives; a reference standard and additional analytical work were needed to confirm SL-164.

## History and market emergence

SL-164 originated from older medicinal-chemistry work on substituted quinazolinones. Decades later it reappeared in the research-chemical market as a “Quaalude-like” sedative.

Published human intoxication reports emerged around 2021–2022. CFSRE later reported that dicloqualone had been identified in U.S. drug materials beginning in California in early 2022 and subsequently in additional locations.

In its 2025 monograph, CFSRE documented a Chicago drug-material sample containing dicloqualone alongside **fentanyl, heroin and diphenhydramine**. That finding is a strong reminder that modern NPS sedatives can appear inside a broader opioid/depressant supply rather than as isolated laboratory chemicals.

## Chemistry and relationship to methaqualone

Dicloqualone shares the **4-quinazolinone scaffold** associated with methaqualone-type sedative-hypnotics.

Structural similarity helps explain why sedative/GABAergic activity is expected, but it does not establish:

- equal potency;
- equal dose;
- equal duration;
- equal overdose risk;
- equal dependence profile.

“Quaalude analogue” is therefore a chemical-family shorthand, not a validated human-equivalence statement.

## Pharmacodynamics

Historical pharmacology and modern summaries support **GABA-receptor-facilitating sedative activity** for dicloqualone.

Methaqualone-class drugs can alter GABA-A receptor function and produce CNS depressant effects. That mechanism is consistent with sedation and impaired coordination, but the human SL-164 cases show that intoxication may also involve **agitation, delirium and myoclonus**.

The exact receptor-subtype profile and human dose-response of modern illicit dicloqualone products remain poorly characterized.

## Pharmacokinetics and metabolism

There is no robust controlled human pharmacokinetic program for dicloqualone.

Published toxicology has identified **hydroxylated metabolites** in serum or urine. The 2024 LC-QTOF-MS paper showed how parent SL-164 and a hydroxy metabolite could initially resemble other compounds by accurate mass until retention time, fragmentation and reference-standard analysis clarified the identity.

A serum concentration reported in that investigation should not be treated as a therapeutic, safe or toxic threshold because the literature is too sparse and the case context involved other substances.

## Human effects: what is documented

### Prolonged agitated delirium case

A 22-year-old man developed:

- prolonged agitated delirium;
- myoclonus;
- tachycardia;
- subfebrile temperature;

after intentional exposure involving SL-164 and opium-poppy tea.

SL-164 and hydroxylated metabolites were found by LC-QTOF-MS. Symptoms resolved over roughly 72 hours with medical treatment.

### “Methaqualone” pill that was actually SL-164

A separate case involved a 21-year-old who believed he had taken methaqualone and also used cocaine.

He developed:

- hypoxia;
- hyperreflexia;
- myoclonus;
- altered mental status.

Laboratory analysis of the alleged methaqualone pills identified **SL-164 instead**.

This is strong product-mislabeling evidence: a familiar drug name on a pill did not match the actual chemical.

## Acute toxicity and overdose

Documented and class-supported concerns include:

- profound sedation;
- impaired coordination;
- altered consciousness;
- agitation or delirium;
- myoclonus;
- hyperreflexia;
- tachycardia;
- hypoxia;
- vomiting and aspiration risk;
- injury while impaired.

The combination of sedative properties with paradoxical agitation/myoclonus makes home interpretation especially unreliable.

## Interactions and polysubstance risk

### Opioids

This is a major concern because CFSRE has identified dicloqualone in drug material alongside fentanyl and heroin.

Any sedative mixed with opioids can deepen loss of consciousness and respiratory compromise.

### Alcohol and other sedatives

Alcohol, benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, barbiturates, sedating antihistamines and related depressants may add to CNS depression.

### Stimulants

A stimulant may partially mask perceived sedation without eliminating physiologic impairment. Mixed stimulant/depressant exposure can also produce confusing agitation, cardiovascular stress or delayed collapse.

## Tolerance and dependence

Direct human longitudinal studies are absent.

Methaqualone-class sedative pharmacology makes **tolerance and physical dependence biologically plausible**, but it would be overreach to assign dicloqualone a precise dependence incidence or a methaqualone-equivalent withdrawal timeline.

Dependence is also not identical to a substance use disorder.

## Withdrawal

A specific SL-164 withdrawal syndrome has not been established in controlled human evidence.

Class-level sedative withdrawal concerns can include:

- rebound insomnia;
- anxiety;
- autonomic symptoms;
- tremor;
- agitation;
- confusion;
- seizure risk in severe depressant dependence.

Because product identity and potency are uncertain, the site does not publish a DIY dicloqualone taper schedule or conversion chart.

## Addiction / substance-use-disorder education

Warning signs of problematic use include:

- repeated inability to cut down;
- escalating frequency;
- using despite blackouts, injuries or medical symptoms;
- craving;
- prioritizing use over work, family or sleep;
- mixing with opioids or alcohol despite known risk.

Physical dependence alone does not prove addiction, but continued compulsive use despite harm is clinically important.

## Treatment and support

There is no dicloqualone-specific approved withdrawal or detox protocol.

Emergency care is appropriate for abnormal breathing, hypoxia, collapse, seizure, severe delirium, persistent unconsciousness or dangerous agitation.

People using sedative-hypnotic NPS regularly may benefit from medically supervised evaluation before abrupt discontinuation, especially when alcohol, benzodiazepines or opioids are also involved.

U.S. readers can use **FindTreatment.gov** or the **SAMHSA National Helpline (1-800-662-HELP)** to locate treatment.

## Drug checking, toxicology and detection

Dicloqualone is a good example of why **non-targeted high-resolution mass spectrometry** matters in NPS toxicology.

Useful methods include:

- LC-QTOF-MS;
- LC-HRMS;
- GC-MS;
- targeted confirmation with reference material.

The 2024 report demonstrates that accurate mass alone may be insufficient when an unknown compound resembles another library entry. Retention time, fragmentation and authentic standards can be essential.

Routine hospital drug screens are not designed to comprehensively detect SL-164.

## Counterfeit and mislabeled product risk

The 2022 case involving pills represented as methaqualone but identified as SL-164 is direct evidence that product naming can be wrong.

CFSRE's U.S. drug-material work adds another risk: dicloqualone can be found in a mixture with **opioids and diphenhydramine**.

A tablet or powder should therefore be treated as chemically uncertain unless tested with a method capable of identifying the relevant NPS.

## Forensic interpretation

Detection proves exposure, not automatically the cause of a clinical syndrome or death.

Interpretation should consider:

- other drugs;
- metabolites;
- timing;
- specimen type;
- concentration;
- product composition;
- clinical findings.

Sparse case literature means there is no validated lethal concentration or impairment threshold for dicloqualone.

## Epidemiology and current trend

The available trend evidence is primarily **case-based and drug-material surveillance**, not population prevalence.

CFSRE reported U.S. detections beginning in 2022 and confirmed a 2024 Chicago sample. Published European clinical cases demonstrate international availability.

Those observations establish market presence without telling us how common use is in the general population.

## Legal and regulatory status — dated October 3, 2026

CFSRE's June 2025 U.S. monograph states that dicloqualone was **not currently scheduled in the United States** at the time of its report.

That is a dated federal-status statement, not a guarantee of legality in every jurisdiction. Analogue laws, state statutes and later scheduling actions can matter.

This page does not infer methaqualone's Schedule I status onto dicloqualone merely because the molecules are related.

## Special populations

There are no adequate dicloqualone-specific studies in pregnancy, breastfeeding, adolescents, older adults or people with respiratory, liver or kidney disease.

Class-level sedative pharmacology supports extra caution in:

- respiratory disease;
- sleep apnea;
- older adults at risk of falls;
- people taking opioids or other sedatives;
- people with seizure history.

## Myths and common misconceptions

### “SL-164 is just methaqualone under another name.”

No. It is a distinct methaqualone analogue.

### “A sedative analogue cannot cause agitated delirium.”

False. A published SL-164 case documented prolonged agitated delirium with myoclonus.

### “A pill sold as methaqualone probably contains methaqualone.”

Not necessarily. One published case found SL-164 in pills reportedly taken as methaqualone.

### “Routine tox screens will catch it.”

Not reliably. High-resolution or targeted mass spectrometry may be needed.

### “Methaqualone's legal status automatically proves dicloqualone's status.”

No. Legal classification must be checked for the exact molecule and jurisdiction.

## FAQ

### Is dicloqualone the same as SL-164?

Yes. SL-164 is a common research/market name for dicloqualone.

### Is it the same as methaqualone?

No. It is a structurally related analogue.

### Can it cause delirium?

Yes. Prolonged agitated delirium has been documented in a published human intoxication case.

### Can routine testing miss it?

Yes. Confirmation may require high-resolution or targeted mass spectrometry with current reference material.

### Is it federally scheduled in the U.S.?

CFSRE's June 2025 monograph stated that dicloqualone was not then scheduled in the United States. State law and later actions can differ.

## Bottom line

Dicloqualone is not simply a nostalgia-branded “Quaalude analogue.”

The human literature is still too thin for dose equivalence or withdrawal precision, which makes uncertainty itself a central part of the safety message.

## How to interpret the evidence without overreaching

Dicloqualone has a longer chemical history than many newly appearing NPS, but age does not equal a modern clinical evidence base. Historical pharmacology, analytical identification, and contemporary market detection answer different questions. A source that confirms identity or receptor activity does not automatically establish present-day product composition, human dose-response, or the frequency of severe outcomes.

The most defensible way to read the evidence is in layers. **Chemical and analytical studies** can establish what the molecule is and how laboratories can identify it. **Preclinical pharmacology** can support a sedative-hypnotic mechanism. **Human case reports or toxicology detections**, when available, show that real exposure occurs. None of those layers by itself creates a validated safety margin for unregulated material.

### Dependence, withdrawal, and prolonged impairment

For sedative-hypnotic compounds, repeated exposure raises concern for tolerance and physical dependence even when molecule-specific withdrawal studies are sparse. The exact onset, severity, and duration of withdrawal from dicloqualone have not been established well enough to support a fixed timeline or self-taper conversion. Severe or escalating symptoms after stopping a regularly used sedative deserve medical assessment rather than an internet-derived schedule.

Likewise, subjective recovery from intoxication does not prove that psychomotor function has fully normalized. Unknown formulation strength, co-ingestants, and individual metabolism can make impairment difficult to predict.

### Testing and counterfeit uncertainty

Novel sedatives may be missed by routine screening panels. Definitive identification may require mass-spectrometric testing and an updated laboratory library. This matters when a product is sold under a familiar sedative name: branding, tablet appearance, or seller description cannot establish that dicloqualone is actually present or that it is the only active compound.

For harm reduction, the evidence boundary is therefore as important as the evidence itself: known sedative-class hazards should be visible, while unsupported potency rankings, equivalence charts, and precise withdrawal schedules should stay out.

## Substance use disorder and recovery support

Dependence and a sedative use disorder are not identical. A harmful pattern can involve impaired control, hazardous use, or continued use despite cognitive, respiratory, psychiatric, work, or relationship consequences.

Treatment evidence is exceptionally weak: a Cochrane review found no qualifying randomized trials for methaqualone dependence, and direct dicloqualone treatment evidence is even thinner. There is no validated analogue-specific detox medication, conversion ratio, or self-taper protocol.

U.S. readers can use [FindTreatment.gov](https://findtreatment.gov/) or [SAMHSA treatment resources](https://www.samhsa.gov/substance-use/treatment). Seizure, delirium, unresponsiveness, or abnormal breathing requires urgent medical evaluation.


## Bottom line

Dicloqualone is a real, analytically confirmed RC sedative—not merely an internet legend.

But the human evidence is too thin to support the confident “modern Quaalude” framing often seen in community discussion.
