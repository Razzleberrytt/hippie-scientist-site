# Cat's Claw & Kava Products Hiding Opioids: Buzzers, Homiez and the 2026 Poison-Center Warnings

## Quick answer

In July 2026, Utah public-health authorities warned that some smoke-shop products marketed with **Cat's Claw or Kava branding were associated with severe opioid overdoses**.

The strongest confirmed finding involved **Buzzers-brand Cat's Claw tablets**. Utah's Department of Agriculture and Food reported that tested Buzzers products contained **MGM-15 and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl**, two opioid-active kratom-derived compounds.

The Utah Poison Control Center separately named **Buzzers and Homiez** among products involved in its warning and said other brands might be involved.

That does **not** mean every Cat's Claw supplement, every Kava supplement, every Buzzers product, or every Homiez product contains these opioids. It means the front-of-package botanical label is not enough to establish what is actually in a smoke-shop tablet.

## What Utah actually confirmed

Utah authorities reported laboratory testing of Buzzers Cat's Claw tablets after a death and multiple severe health incidents.

The tested samples contained:

- **MGM-15 / dihydro-7-hydroxymitragynine**
- **mitragynine pseudoindoxyl**

Both are potent opioid-acting derivatives in the broader kratom alkaloid family.

That is fundamentally different from ordinary Cat's Claw (*Uncaria tomentosa*), which is a botanical supplement and does not explain opioid toxidrome findings such as profound sedation and respiratory depression.

## What the Homiez warning means—and does not mean

The Utah Poison Control Center's broader alert named **Buzzers and Homiez** products and warned that others might also be involved.

The careful distinction is important:

- Buzzers Cat's Claw tablets had a publicly reported state testing result identifying MGM-15 and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl.
- Homiez was named in the poison-center warning, but that alert did not publish an equivalent brand-wide laboratory result proving that every Homiez item contained those exact compounds.
- Product contents can vary by batch, flavor, manufacturer, reseller, or time.

So this site will not convert a poison-center suspicion into a universal composition claim.

## Why the labels are especially concerning

The poison-center warning said some products can carry reassuring statements such as “no kratom” or “no 7-OH.”

That can create a false sense of security because **MGM-15 and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl are not the same molecule as 7-hydroxymitragynine**, even though they are chemically related to the same broader family.

A label can therefore be literally or technically framed in a way that still leaves a consumer unaware of an opioid-active derivative.

## Why Kava creates additional confusion

Kava (*Piper methysticum*) is itself psychoactive and can cause sedation, dizziness, and impaired coordination. It is not an opioid.

That means a product combining Kava branding with an undisclosed opioid can be particularly confusing: a person may interpret unexpectedly strong sedation as “just the Kava” instead of recognizing an opioid emergency.

CDC poison-center data also show increasing serious outcomes involving Kava exposures and increasing co-use with kratom-containing products, creating a broader safety context even apart from the 2026 adulteration warnings.

## What opioid toxicity can look like

A suspected opioid overdose can include:

- very slow, irregular, or stopped breathing;
- inability to stay awake or be awakened;
- blue or gray lips or fingertips;
- pinpoint pupils;
- limpness or collapse;
- choking, gurgling, or abnormal snoring sounds.

If those symptoms occur after a product sold as Cat's Claw, Kava, kratom, or a “botanical extract,” **do not assume the label rules out an opioid.**

Call emergency services. Naloxone is appropriate when opioid toxicity is suspected and can be given even when the exact drug is unknown.

## Dependence and withdrawal are also possible

The poison-center alert specifically warned about **dependence and withdrawal**, not only acute overdose.

That makes sense pharmacologically for opioid-active derivatives. A product can be dangerous even when it does not cause an immediate emergency if repeated exposure produces tolerance and physical dependence.

Because product contents may be unknown, self-designed taper schedules based only on a package label can be unreliable.

## The bigger lesson: botanical branding is not chemical identification

These products expose a broader problem in the gray-market supplement and smoke-shop space.

A package can look like:

- an herbal supplement;
- a wellness product;
- a Kava item;
- a Cat's Claw extract;
- a kratom alternative;
- a “no 7-OH” product.

None of those descriptions independently proves the active ingredients.

Only appropriate analytical testing can answer that question for a specific sample.

## Bottom line

The 2026 Buzzers finding is not an internet rumor: **Utah authorities reported laboratory-confirmed MGM-15 and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl in tested Buzzers Cat's Claw tablets**.

The broader warning around Homiez and other Cat's Claw/Kava smoke-shop products is serious but should remain carefully worded because composition can vary and not every named product has the same published testing evidence.

The practical rule is simple: **a botanical-looking label does not reliably exclude a potent opioid.**
