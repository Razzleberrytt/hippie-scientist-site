# SR-15099: What Is It, and Can It Reverse Opioid Tolerance?

<NPSDisclaimer />

**SR-15099 is an experimental synthetic opioid that activates the mu-opioid receptor.** Its published research is primarily laboratory and animal work. It is not an approved medication, supplement, or proven opioid-tolerance treatment.

**Most important distinction:** The study often described online as a “tolerance reset” experiment was conducted with **SR-17018**, a related but different compound. Researchers did not establish that SR-15099 resets opioid tolerance in people.

For full study-by-study explanations and references, read [SR-15099: Pharmacology, Opioid Tolerance Claims, Human Evidence and Risks](/articles/sr-15099/).

## Why scientists investigated SR-15099

The mu-opioid receptor receives chemical signals involved in opioid-related effects. It can send those signals through different internal pathways.

Researchers call compounds such as SR-15099 **G-protein-biased agonists** when their measured signaling favors particular G-protein pathways relative to beta-arrestin recruitment in an experimental system. This does not mean the compound has only one effect, and it does not prove lower risk in humans.

In the 2017 paper by Schmid and colleagues, SR-15099 was tested with other related experimental opioids in cellular and mouse studies. The researchers described differences between pain-related mouse responses and some measures of suppressed breathing at the tested conditions. Those results cannot establish that it is safe for human use. [Schmid et al., PMID 29149605](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29149605/).

The follow-up work by Stahl and colleagues also included SR-15099 in receptor and mouse-brain signaling experiments, showing the importance of distinguishing laboratory receptor activity from proven treatments. [PMID 34819362](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34819362/).

## What about reversing tolerance?

**Tolerance** is when repeated use of a drug can make the same amount produce less of an effect. **Withdrawal** refers to symptoms that can appear when a physically adapted body stops or reduces exposure. These concepts overlap in real life, but they are not the same.

In a 2020 study, morphine-tolerant mice were switched to **SR-17018**, not SR-15099. Three days later, mice receiving SR-17018 had regained morphine sensitivity in a hot-plate test *while signs of withdrawal were suppressed*. Mice receiving a comparison treatment without a replacement opioid also regained morphine sensitivity, but showed withdrawal. That distinction makes the experiment interesting and limits the interpretation of “reset.” [Grim et al., PMID 31443104](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31443104/).

A later study reported that repeated SR-17018 exposure *did* produce tolerance in a separate mouse warm-water tail-immersion experiment. Results differ depending on the model measured. [Pantouli et al., PMID 33345829](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33345829/).

It would be scientifically incorrect to present either finding as a controlled human SR-15099 outcome.

## Is SR-15099 safe?

Human respiratory effects, potential dependence, toxic exposures, interactions, and long-term risks are not established. Because SR-15099 acts at an opioid receptor, sedation and suppressed breathing are serious potential hazards. An apparently favorable laboratory signaling profile is not an overdose-safety guarantee. Independent research has challenged the simple explanation that reducing beta-arrestin-2 signaling by itself prevents opioid-related respiratory depression. [Bachmutsky et al., PMID 34002697](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34002697/).

There is no validated human dose or safe opioid-conversion or withdrawal-substitution protocol for SR-15099. Do not infer one from SR-17018 experiments or online reports. Product identity and purity in unregulated markets may be uncertain.

For suspected opioid poisoning, call emergency services and use naloxone if available, following emergency instructions. Specific clinical response data for SR-15099 are unavailable.
