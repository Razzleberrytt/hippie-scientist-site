# The Hippie Scientist: teach the science in plain English

**Owner direction, 2026-10-13:** Every science video, caption, article and reusable graphic should be understandable to someone without a scientific background. An unfamiliar scientific word is **welcome only when it is taught in context**. Preserve accuracy; do not replace uncertainty with confident but false simplifications.

## Repeatable way to teach any topic

1. **Start with the human question.** Example: “How are mitragynine and 7-OH different?”
2. **Explain using familiar words.** “Atoms are tiny building blocks. A molecule is a connected group of atoms.”
3. **Name the scientific idea and define it nearby.** “An oxygen-and-hydrogen pair is called a *hydroxyl group*.”
4. **Show why this matters.** Point to the actual difference in two correctly identified chemical diagrams.
5. **Name the limit.** “This drawing does not by itself tell us how strong either substance is, what a person feels, or which effects are proven.”
6. **Offer the source.** PubChem is a publicly searchable chemistry database. A CID is its compound-record number; a CID alone is not evidence of a health outcome.

## Where the rule applies

- **Narrated videos:** no opening wall of acronyms; natural one-idea-at-a-time narration; technical vocabulary taught the first time. Include enough hold time to learn a new word, not so much that the video becomes repetitive. Audio dictates edit timing; no slowing narration to fit visuals.
- **On-screen labels and graphics:** a professional name should have a nearby ordinary-language explanation; ensure it fits phone-safe areas when muted. Make clear when a visual is a computer-estimated illustration rather than a measured molecule or actual drug-receptor footage.
- **Website articles:** begin with “what is it?” and “why should I care?”; explain study terminology (randomized trial, placebo, relative risk, sample size) when first used; show what was actually measured and how many people or samples were involved. Keep direct source citations.
- **Asset library:** allow simple searches (“atom map,” “possible shape”), teach difficult words inside optional details, preserve exact compound/CID and asset hash identity. A reusable graphic is not automatically science/craft approved.
- **Harm-reduction topics:** use clear, nonjudgmental language. Accurately separate user-reported experiences, animal/cell findings and established clinical evidence. Do not infer potency, safety or withdrawal from a chemical drawing alone.

## Examples and explanations

| Specialist language | Teach it like this |
|---|---|
| “7-hydroxymitragynine exhibits an additional hydroxyl substituent” | “The two molecule maps differ at one spot. The 7-OH map shows an oxygen-and-hydrogen pair, called a *hydroxyl group*.” |
| “CID-verified molecular connectivity” | “This diagram shows which atoms connect. We checked it against the PubChem chemistry database. The record number is there so anyone can look it up.” |
| “An ETKDG/MMFF computed conformer” | “A computer estimated one possible 3D shape. The calculation is not a photograph or a direct measurement of that shape.” |
| “Receptor binding” | “Some chemicals interact with parts of cells called *receptors*, which can help pass along signals. A chemical structure picture doesn't show whether, where or how strongly this happened in a person.” |
| “RCT” | “A randomized controlled trial: a study where people are assigned by chance to different treatments or comparison groups, helping researchers make a fairer comparison.” |
| “Statistically significant” | “The pattern would be relatively unlikely under the study's particular statistical assumptions if there were really no difference; it doesn't tell us how big or important the difference is by itself.” |

## Five sign-off questions for a reviewer

- Can a curious 12-year-old follow the main idea without looking up vocabulary?
- Is each necessary specialist term defined at its **first public-facing use**?
- Does every chemical or research image say what is source-established and what is only illustrated or predicted?
- Can someone watching **without sound** still follow the argument, with readable text rather than walls of captions?
- Did simplification change the actual claim, exaggerate certainty, invent a human effect or omit a critical limitation?

If any answer is **no or unknown**, revise and recheck. Automatic word screening only spots some omissions; it cannot prove comprehension, scientific truth, or excellent storytelling. Human comprehension and qualified scientific checks remain distinct.
